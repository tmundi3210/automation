## Materials, Rhodium/Gold Finishes & Quality-Cost

*Scope: what your six launch SKUs should be MADE of, how they should be finished and labeled, what "high-quality" means as a testable gate, and how to lower price without lowering that graded quality. Every quantitative claim is tagged `[FACT-source]`, `[FACT]` (materials-science/regulatory), `[ESTIMATE]`, `[METHOD]`, or `[UNKNOWN]`. Retail price, margin and the demand read belong to the economics and market specialists — this section hands them a graded, cost-engineered materials spec.*

### Bottom line up front

- **Default base metal for a skin-contact 18–25 line: brass with a nickel-FREE (palladium or white-bronze) barrier under the finish** — cheapest workable substrate that can still pass the skin-safety gate, *provided* the barrier is nickel-free and the gold is thick enough to stay non-porous. [METHOD]
- **Add ONE premium/hypoallergenic tier on sterling 925** for your rhodium-white pieces and any piece you want to legally call **vermeil**. Sterling is nickel-free by composition and is the *required* base for vermeil and the standard base under rhodium. [FACT]
- **316L stainless is the "worn-bare, sensitive-skin" option** — durable, tarnish-resistant, low nickel release — but it is nickel-SAFE, not nickel-FREE, and it is hard to plate. Use it where the piece is sold as steel-toned or lightly PVD'd, not where you need a deep plated gold look. [FACT]
- **Skin-safety is earned by an after-wear test, not by the word "hypoallergenic."** The gate is EN 1811 nickel release run *after* EN 12472 wear pre-conditioning: ≤0.2 µg/cm²/week for pierced-ear posts, ≤0.5 for general prolonged contact. [FACT]

### 1. Base material: brass vs sterling 925 vs stainless 316L

There is **no universal winner** — the right base is a function of your price/quality/skin-safety target. The three candidates the brief names, graded on the axes that actually decide cost and safety:

| Axis | Brass (Cu-Zn) | Sterling 925 | Stainless 316L |
|---|---|---|---|
| Relative cost (ordinal) | **Cheapest** of the three | **Dearest** — tracks silver spot | Cheap raw, but plating/forming pushes finished cost up | 
| Nickel status | Usually low-nickel itself; **risk comes from a nickel barrier**, not the brass | **Nickel-free** (92.5% Ag + ~7.5% Cu) | **Nickel-SAFE but NOT nickel-free** (~10–14% Ni; passive Cr-oxide film holds release low) |
| Tarnish | Copper oxides → green/verdigris; dezincification; "season cracking" | Tarnishes readily → black **Ag₂S** (the reason for rhodium) | Excellent — self-healing passive film, often worn bare |
| Barrier before gold? | **Mandatory** (Cu diffuses into and stains gold) | Often an adhesion/leveling layer; less demanding | Needs an **activation strike** (Wood's nickel or sulfamate-Ni) or plating fails adhesion |
| Best fit | Mid-fashion, value gold-look, high volume | Premium tier, genuine vermeil, rhodium-white | Sensitive-skin worn-bare / steel-tone / lightly PVD'd |

Sources for the composition/limit facts: sterling = 92.5% Ag is the FTC 16 CFR Part 23 threshold for the word "sterling" [FACT — FTC 16 CFR Part 23]; 316L ~16–18% Cr / ~10–14% Ni / ~2–3% Mo and its passive-film behavior [FACT — stainless metallurgy]. **"Surgical steel" is a marketing phrase, not a purity or release guarantee** [FACT].

**Recommendation for a six-SKU DTC line to 18–25 women:** a **two-tier base strategy** —
1. **Value tier = brass + nickel-free barrier + gold electroplate.** Brass is the cheapest workable base and takes plating well; the pivotal skin-safety decision is that the barrier under the gold must be **palladium / Pd-Ni(low) / white bronze (Cu-Sn-Zn), not bright nickel** [FACT], so that when the microns-thin gold eventually wears or pores, the exposed layer is not a nickel source.
2. **Premium tier = sterling 925** for the rhodium-white pieces and anything marketed as vermeil. Sterling buys you a real precious-metal base, a nickel-free skin-safety story, and the legal right to the word "vermeil."

Why not "just use 316L for everything": it sidesteps wear-through by being worn bare, but it is hard to cold-form and hard to plate (the passive film causes adhesion failure without an activation strike), and it is **not** nickel-free — so a hypoallergenic claim on 316L still rests on an EN 1811 result, not the "surgical" label [FACT].

**Nickel-safety, stated precisely (this is the load-bearing safety fact):**
- The EU reference test is **EN 1811**; **REACH Annex XVII entry 27** caps released nickel at **0.5 µg/cm²/week for prolonged skin contact and 0.2 µg/cm²/week for posts inserted into pierced ears** [FACT]. The US has **no** equivalent federal nickel-release limit — so for your US venture this is a **skin-safety and differentiation bar, not a legal mandate** [FACT]. That is *more* reason to hold it, not less: nickel is the most common contact allergen, sensitization is lifelong, and a bad first pair of earrings permanently removes a young buyer.
- **"Nickel-free" (no nickel present) ≠ "nickel-safe" (nickel present but release below the limit).** Never conflate them in copy [FACT].
- **"Hypoallergenic" is unregulated** and promises nothing measurable [FACT].
- The honest gate is **EN 1811 run AFTER EN 12472 wear-and-corrosion pre-conditioning**, on the worst-case exposed layer (barrier + base, not just the gold) — because plating is microns thin and develops pores/scratches that expose what's underneath [FACT]. The DMG (dimethylglyoxime) spot test is a cheap qualitative **screen only** (pink = fail), never a certificate [FACT].
- **What % of your specific cohort is nickel-sensitized** is a real number to pull from dermatology literature, not to guess [METHOD].

### 2. Rhodium over silver — explained correctly

Your brief's intuition ("rhodium makes silver shiny and keeps it from un-shining") points at the right thing but for the wrong reason. The correct science:

- **Silver tarnish is a chemical reaction, not lost polish.** Metallic silver reacts with sulfur species in air and on skin — hydrogen sulfide, carbonyl sulfide, sulfur in rubber/wool/eggs/some cosmetics — to form **black silver sulfide, Ag₂S** (and silver chloride, AgCl, where sweat/chloride is present). It proceeds at trace ppb sulfur; the ~7.5% copper in sterling adds its own darkening [FACT].
- **Rhodium (Rh, atomic number 45) is a platinum-group metal that is hard (~800–1000 HV, roughly an order of magnitude harder than soft gold at ~60–90 HV), bright bluish-white, highly reflective, and chemically inert** [FACT]. Gold is noble and does not tarnish; rhodium likewise does not.
- So a thin rhodium layer over sterling does **two jobs in one coat**: (1) as an inert PGM it is a **barrier** that keeps sulfur from reaching the silver, so the Ag₂S reaction can't proceed at the surface; and (2) as a hard, bright bluish-white, mirror-reflective metal it supplies the "shiny and stays shiny" **cosmetic** [FACT].
- **White gold is rhodium-plated for the same reason** — the white-gold *alloy* (gold + Pd/Ni/Ag/Zn) is only a pale off-white/grey on its own, so it is rhodium-dipped to get the bright white buyers now expect [FACT].
- **Thickness classes:** rhodium flash ~0.05–0.1 µm (color/cosmetic, wears at high-touch points); standard decorative ~0.1–0.25 µm. Thicker is *not* simply better — above ~0.25–0.5 µm rhodium tends to micro-crack and the precious-metal cost climbs steeply [FACT]. A strike/barrier underlayer is normal because thin rhodium can be porous and silver/copper can creep through pores.
- **Re-dip is normal maintenance, not a defect.** On high-touch worn pieces (ring shanks) the thin rhodium wears through and the warmer base shows; re-dip is periodic (trade practice commonly ~1–3 years on a worn ring) [ESTIMATE — trade practice]. Any specific SKU's wear life / re-dip interval is an **[ESTIMATE] gated by tests, never a guaranteed "lasts N years."**

**Cost flag:** rhodium is a volatile PGM — **~US$8,000/oz on 2 Jul 2026 (Umicore), ~$8,100 on 3 Jul, with a Kitco range of ~$7,700–8,700 on 6 Jul 2026** [FACT-source: Umicore PMM / Kitco], versus gold at **~$4,141/oz** [FACT-source: Kitco]. Rhodium is roughly **~2×** the per-ounce price of gold right now. Because a rhodium coat is very thin, per-piece rhodium is small in absolute terms, but the price is the most volatile input in your stack — **re-pull spot before any COGS build** [METHOD]; the live number is [UNKNOWN] until pulled.

### 3. Gold finish options with their exact FTC 16 CFR Part 23 labels

These thresholds are **law-adjacent naming boundaries**, not quality opinions — calling an article by a term it doesn't meet is a deceptive act under the FTC Guides. Verified against the FTC / eCFR text:

| Finish term | FTC threshold (all conditions bind) | Where it fits |
|---|---|---|
| **Gold flash / gold wash** | **< 0.175 µm** of ≥10k gold — may **NOT** be called "plate" | Decorative, low-touch only; where "wears off in weeks" complaints come from if oversold |
| **Gold electroplate / gold plated** | electrolytic layer of **≥10k gold, ≥0.175 µm** (≈7 millionths of an inch) on all significant surfaces | Most fashion jewelry lives here — your value tier |
| **Heavy gold electroplate** | same fineness, **≥2.5 µm** (≈100 millionths of an inch) | High-touch pieces (rings, bracelet clasps) |
| **Vermeil** | **sterling-silver base + ≥10k gold + ≥2.5 µm** — all three bind | Premium plated tier; a base-metal-core piece is **never** vermeil at any thickness |
| **Gold-filled / rolled gold plate** | a karat-gold layer **mechanically bonded** (heat+pressure, not electroplated) at **≥1/20 (5%) of total weight** for gold-filled; lesser fraction = "rolled gold plate," marked with its fraction+karat | Durable, ~50–100× the gold of electroplate, wears for decades — but not solid gold and not plating |

Sources: [FACT — FTC 16 CFR Part 23 / eCFR §23.4–§23.5, confirmed via ftc.gov and law.cornell.edu]. Purity basis: karat = parts-in-24 (24k pure, 18k = 75.0%, 14k = 58.3%, **10k = 41.7% is the FTC floor for calling something "gold"**) [FACT].

**Hard label rules you must not cross:** no calling gold-over-brass "vermeil" (wrong base); no calling sub-0.175 µm flash "plated"; no calling nitride-only PVD "gold plated" (a "gold plated" marking needs *real gold at the surface*); rolled gold plate must carry its fraction+karat. **Confirm the label word against an XRF/coulometric thickness + karat + base check at the worst-case point before printing**, and clear any borderline claim with counsel [FACT — enforcement].

**Adjacent finish facts worth knowing:**
- **Hard gold** co-deposits ~0.1–0.5% Co or Ni to reach ~130–200 HV (vs soft gold ~60–90 HV) for high-touch wear — but it is brittle, so **form first, then plate**, and **Ni-hardened gold puts nickel in the surface layer**, which conflicts with a hypoallergenic claim (use Co-hardened or soft gold there) [FACT].
- **PVD/IP gold** (TiN/ZrN ceramic, ~1500–2500 HV) is the scratch-resistance ceiling; premium stacks cap it with a thin real-gold flash for true color. Two honesty rules: nitride alone is not "gold," and PVD doesn't color-match electroplated gold under all lighting — **matched sets should share one finishing route** [FACT].
- **E-coat / anti-tarnish topcoat**: a clear cataphoretic lacquer (5–25 µm) seals pores and lets an economically thin gold (0.25–0.5 µm) wear like a thicker layer — the standard fashion-jewelry cost play — but it is a **durability aid, not a legal substitute for karat/thickness**, and for skin-contact it must survive EN 12472 wear to count toward nickel compliance [FACT].

### 4. Quality tiers and the acceptance tests that gate "high-quality"

**"High-quality" is a gate, not an adjective.** A piece is high-quality only when it **clears every gate its label claim and wear duty require, judged at the worst-case point** (recess, inside of a ring, high-wear edge) — never on the proud average, because that is where a plated piece actually fails in service [FACT]. Reject any single-number "quality score."

**The acceptance battery** (each printable claim is earned by the matching accredited test):

| Gate | Test / standard | Passes / earns |
|---|---|---|
| Thickness & label | XRF (ISO 3497 / ASTM B568), cross-checked by coulometric (ISO 2177 / ASTM B504), at worst-case low-current-density point | The FTC label word ("electroplate" / "heavy" / "vermeil") |
| Adhesion | 180° wrap-bend + cross-hatch/tape + heat-quench, **ASTM B571** | Coating stays bonded (distinguish cohesive micro-crack of hard gold from adhesive peel = reject) |
| Tarnish / corrosion | Salt spray **ISO 9227 / ASTM B117**; artificial-sweat soak; sulfur-vapor | A printed "tarnish-resistant" claim (accredited chamber) |
| Wear / abrasion | Taber **ASTM D4060**, rub jig vs retained standard; deposit hardness ASTM B578 | "Scratch-resistant"; endpoint = first base-metal read-through |
| Porosity | Ferroxyl (Prussian-blue at pores) / **ASTM B735** nitric-acid-vapor | No galvanic corrosion time-bomb; porosity falls steeply above ~1–2.5 µm |
| Nickel release | DMG screen; accredited **EN 1811 after EN 12472** wear pre-conditioning | "Hypoallergenic / nickel-safe" (0.5 / 0.2 µg/cm²/week) |

Sources: [FACT — the named ASTM/ISO/EN standards]. **The porosity gate is why thickness cannot simply be minimized to save cost** — a shiny thin flash on an active base metal passes visual inspection and is a corrosion time-bomb [FACT].

**Two-layer test plan** [FACT — QC practice]: (1) **Pre-production qualification** — plate witness coupons alongside real parts, run the full battery in sequence against **written numeric pass/fail criteria set before testing**, require three consecutive conforming lots before scale-up. (2) **Per-batch routine** — 100% visual at 10×, first-article XRF + sampling, one wrap-bend witness, a ferroxyl spot-check, color vs a retained master panel, recorded before parts move to stock. **Criteria invented after seeing results always pass and are not a real gate.**

**In-house screen vs accredited result:** DMG spot, ferroxyl, DIY salt-fog, rub jig and color panels **control process drift cheaply but are comparative only and are never quoted as accredited certificates.** Only accredited send-outs (EN 1811, conformant salt-spray, certified karat/thickness) can anchor a **legal or marketing claim** [FACT].

**Suggested quality tiers for the six SKUs:**
- **Tier A (value / low-touch — studs, pendants):** brass + nickel-free barrier + **gold electroplate ≥0.175 µm** (or 0.25–0.5 µm under e-coat). Gate on adhesion, porosity, nickel-after-wear.
- **Tier B (heavy-wear — rings, huggie hoops, bracelets):** brass or sterling + nickel-free barrier + **heavy gold electroplate ≥2.5 µm** (or Co-hard gold). Add wear/abrasion and salt-spray gates.
- **Tier C (premium white / bright):** **sterling 925 + rhodium** 0.1–0.25 µm, or genuine **vermeil** (sterling + ≥2.5 µm ≥10k gold). Gate on thickness/label + tarnish + nickel.

### 5. Value-engineering levers — lower price WITHOUT lowering quality

**Definition:** value engineering holds the acceptance gate fixed, moves **one** lever, and **re-runs the same battery**; if it still passes at the worst-case point, the saving is real. A cost-down that changes *what the piece can pass* is a **quality cut, not value engineering**, and is refused [FACT — protocol]. The cost-quality relationship is a **diminishing-returns curve**: below a floor the piece fails a gate and is worth nothing however shiny; above the "knee," extra gold micron and extra testing buy mostly *perceived* quality. **Trim toward the knee, never toward the floor.**

The legitimate levers (move one at a time, re-test):

1. **Base-metal lever** — pick the cheapest substrate that still clears the nickel + porosity gates for the piece's duty. A cheaper nickel-bearing base is acceptable **only behind a compliant nickel-free barrier** that keeps EN 1811 within limits after EN 12472 wear.
2. **Selective thickness placement** — put the class thickness (e.g. ≥2.5 µm) on high-wear, skin-contact, visible zones; allow a thinner-but-still-porosity-safe deposit in hidden recesses. Gold micron is the single largest, most price-volatile driver, so this is a real cut — but **never below the FTC label class, never below the ~1–2.5 µm porosity-safe band** over an active base.
3. **Barrier architecture (the best lever — cuts cost AND raises durability at once)** — a hard sulfamate-nickel or Pd/white-bronze underlayer (HV ~200–400) levels and load-supports a **thinner gold** that then survives the wear gate it couldn't survive alone; a Pd/white-bronze barrier can be nickel-free for compliance. Moves the whole curve favorably instead of sliding down it.
4. **Finish selection to the gate, not to prestige** — specify rhodium only where a white piece must stay bright (it's a volatile PGM); an e-coat-protected thinner gold may pass the same gates for less. Use gold-filled only where decades-long wear is actually claimed.
5. **Batch/run economics** — fill the bath and the run, amortize setup and once-per-product accredited send-out tests over more pieces, share witness-coupon burn. **Hard limit:** the per-batch drift checks (color-vs-master, first-article XRF, wrap-bend, ferroxyl) survive any batch-size increase — bigger batches amortize fixed cost, they do **not** license skipping the per-batch gate.

**False economies that are quality cuts, not value engineering — refuse these** [FACT]:
- Thinning noble metal **below the ~1–2.5 µm porosity-safe band** on an active base (corrosion time-bomb that passes visual).
- **Deleting the barrier underlayer** to save a step (loses wear support; exposes tarnish and, if the deleted-toward layer is nickel, the EN 1811 limit).
- Dropping to a **cheaper nickel-bearing base without a compliant nickel-free barrier** (fails nickel release after EN 12472 wear).
- **Skipping per-batch QC** (ships drift).
- Quoting **in-house-screen results as accredited claims.**

**Perceived vs real quality:** you *may* cut real over-spec that sits above the knee and is invisible to the buyer (gold micron beyond the wear gate); you may *raise* perceived quality cheaply (color control, heft, packaging) to support price. You may **never** cut real quality below a gate while preserving the premium look — that ships a piece that looks premium and fails in weeks.
