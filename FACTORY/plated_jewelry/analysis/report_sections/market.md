## US Jewelry Demand & Consumer Read

_Analyst: `jewel_market` specialist. Scope: two orthogonal cuts of the catalog — **category** (earrings / necklaces / rings / bracelets / anklets) and **finish tier** (fashion/costume plated vs fine/solid) — plus **who buys** and **what is trending**, for a DTC rhodium-/gold-plated fashion-jewelry brand targeting US women ~18–25._

**Honesty contract (binds every number below).** Every quantitative claim carries one tag: **[FACT-source]** (a cited public figure or regulatory definition), **[ESTIMATE-basis]** (a number I derived, with the method shown), **[METHOD]** (the recipe to measure a number not yet pulled), or **[UNKNOWN]** (an honest gap). No market size, unit count, solid-vs-plated %, demographic/gender/ethnicity purchase rate, competitor share, or transaction price is asserted as a measured actual for this venture. Where the owner wants a hard number that no public source can carry, I give the method and route it to the poll (`jewel_poll`) or a data pull. **Read the tags — a $-figure with no tag would be a fabrication, and there are none here.**

> **Critical framing note before any number.** Secondary market-research vendors disagree with each other by 2x+ on the same "US costume jewelry" figure (see below). Treat every vendor market-size number as a **positioning/order-of-magnitude signal, not a measured truth** — they use different definitions (costume-only vs all-fashion vs fashion+demi-fine), different base years, and undisclosed methods. The only *government* jewelry-spend instrument (BLS Consumer Expenditure Survey) is bundled with watches and is a per-household gradient, not a market total.

### 2. Category mix — which types sell most

**The single most important structural fact for this venture:** demand must be read **per buyable subtype, not per top category** — a hot subtype (huggie hoops, paperclip chains) hides inside a flat category average, and revenue-share and unit-share rank the categories *differently*.

- **By revenue share, rings lead** the overall US jewelry market — **~39–43%** [FACT-source: Grand View Research / Arizton]. **But this is fine-jewelry-weighted**: rings carry engagement/wedding/milestone value, so they dominate *dollars*, not *units*, and that dollar dominance largely does **not** transfer to an 18–25 fashion-plated brand. [METHOD-caveat: revenue share ≠ unit share].
- **Necklaces** are the fastest-growing category — projected **~8.0% CAGR**, the highest of the five [FACT-source: Grand View Research / Polaris].
- **Earrings** are the structural fit for this venture: **lowest price point → lowest barrier to entry → impulse buys and frequent collection updates** [FACT-source: Grand View Research]. For a low-AOV, high-frequency, Gen-Z DTC model, earrings are the natural volume/entry category even though rings win the industry's dollars.
- **Bracelets / anklets:** no clean public unit-share split was retrievable [UNKNOWN — public].

**Decision read (tagged):** for *dollars per unit* rank rings/necklaces; for *units, frequency, and impulse* — the economics of a 6-SKU plated launch — rank **earrings first, then necklaces, then rings** as an [ESTIMATE-basis: category price-point + impulse-frequency logic, corroborated by earrings' low-barrier and necklaces' high-CAGR public signals]. **The precise per-subtype rank for *this* audience is a poll question, not a public fact** (see §7).

**[METHOD] to measure per-subtype demand without a sales dataset (from KB1 — demand triangulation):** over a documented storefront/marketplace frame, triangulate three public proxies — (a) marketplace best-seller rank (ordinal, velocity-weighted), (b) search-query volume (relative, seasonal), (c) retail assortment share (supply-side, lagging) — into a frame-qualified ranking. Require ≥2 of 3 to agree before acting. Carry each proxy's bias; never read any as an absolute unit count.

### 3. Buyer demographics

**Gender.** **Women account for ~67% of US jewelry purchases; men ~33%** [FACT-source: carattrade / Branvas 2025 compilations]. In the *costume/fashion* sub-segment the female skew is stronger — one compilation puts the female share of the costume-jewelry market near **~58%** and demi-fine at **~78% women** [FACT-source: secondary compilations / Persistence MR — treat as low-confidence, definitions vary]. The owner's "mostly girls" prior is **supported as a directional prior, not proven for this store** — confirm against the store's own analytics and the poll. [METHOD: treat "18–25 women" as a hypothesis to test, per KB2, not a hardcoded fact.]

**Age / the 18–25 cohort.** Gen Z behavior is the strongest public signal in this section:
- **58% of Gen Z bought jewelry 3+ times in 2023** (vs 41% of boomers) — **frequency, not occasion, is the Gen-Z pattern** [FACT-source: nuOrder / MVI Marketing survey]. This is the demand engine for a low-AOV replenishment model.
- **~50% of Gen Z splurge on themselves monthly or more** [FACT-source: same]. **Self-purchase, not gifting**, dominates this cohort.
- **Affordability-first:** more than a third of Gen Z cap a self-purchase under $500 [FACT-source: same], and on marketplaces the realized fashion price sits far lower (§6). Fits plated, not solid.
- Directional style signals (verify with the poll, not fact for your buyer): Y2K/bold revival, gender-neutral framing (~42% of Gen Z prefer non-gendered merchandising per a 2023 McKinsey read), sustainability salience [FACT-source: McKinsey via Accio/Kaleya — style *narratives*, ordinal not cardinal].

**Income.** The load-bearing caveat: **households >$75k = ~72% of *fine* jewelry purchases (~$850/yr avg)** [FACT-source: Branvas/abercrombie compilation] — that statistic is about **fine, not fashion**, and does **not** describe your buyer. **Fashion jewelry is reported at ~23% of jewelry market *value* but ~47% of *unit* sales at ~$35 average** [FACT-source: carattrade/classywomen compilation — low-confidence blog stat, flagged]. That value-vs-unit inversion is the whole thesis: **the plated tier is a units-and-frequency game at a low price point, not a dollar-per-piece game.**

**Ethnicity — deliberately not asserted as a rate.** Per KB2's ethics guardrail: **no per-ethnicity purchase rate exists publicly and none will be invented.** The only admissible public input is **Census/ACS race/ethnicity *population base rates* as a denominator** (never a purchase signal) plus *observable occasion behavior* (e.g., gifting calendars). The ecological fallacy binds — a group average is never read as an individual trait — and protected-class ad targeting is off-limits on Meta/TikTok anyway. **[METHOD/UNKNOWN]:** if the owner wants an ethnicity read, it comes from (a) ACS base rates for the trade area and (b) the store's own aggregated, privacy-safe first-party signal — not a demographic purchase-rate table, which does not exist.

### 4. "How many people buy" — a sized band, never a single number

The KB2 recipe: **buyers = population × prevalence × frequency**, each factor separately sourced and tagged, output as a **band**.

- **Population** — US women ~18–24 ≈ **~14 million** [ESTIMATE-basis: well-established ACS order of magnitude; **flag: pull exact figure from ACS table B01001 before use**].
- **Prevalence (share who buy fashion jewelry in a year)** — no clean public rate for *this* cohort. Anchors: 80% of US adults have self-purchased jewelry (lifetime) [FACT-source: Bridgewater/Branvas]; 58% of Gen Z bought 3+ times in a year [FACT-source: nuOrder]. A defensible **annual fashion-jewelry-buyer prevalence for Gen-Z women ≈ 55–70%** [ESTIMATE-basis: bracketed by the two anchors; confirm via poll].
- **Frequency** — Gen-Z multi-purchase pattern implies **≈ 2–4 fashion-jewelry buys/yr** among buyers [ESTIMATE-basis: "3+ times" anchor].

**Illustrative band (all inputs tagged, do not quote as measured):** 14M × (0.55–0.70) × (2–4 buys) ≈ **~15M–39M fashion-jewelry purchase occasions/yr from US women 18–24** [ESTIMATE-basis: population × prevalence × frequency, each factor flagged; this is a TAM-shaped sizing band, not a sales forecast, and every factor needs confirmation]. **Your obtainable share of this is a function of reach/CAC, owned by `jewel_economics`, not this section.**

### 5. Solid-gold vs gold-plated / fashion split

**There is no clean, current, public *national unit-share* statistic for solid vs plated.** What exists:
- The value-vs-unit inversion above: **fashion (plated/base) ≈ ~47% of units but only ~23% of value; fine (solid) is the mirror** [FACT-source: secondary compilation — low confidence, flagged]. Structurally sound because solid gold's per-unit price is multiples of plated.
- The macro driver: **rising gold prices are pushing buyers from solid toward plated** [FACT-source: Coherent MI / Persistence MR] — the split is moving toward plated in units, exactly the venture's tailwind.

**[METHOD] to estimate the split honestly (KB1 solid-vs-plated method):** over a defined storefront/marketplace frame, classify each sampled SKU's finish against the FTC definitions, then compute **assortment share** (share of listings) and **demand-weighted share** (weighted by a demand proxy) — always reported "within frame F," never as a national fact. The FTC finish ladder anchors the classification:

- **Gold electroplate ("gold-plated"):** electrolytic layer, **≥10K gold, ≥0.175 micron** on base metal ("heavy gold electroplate" ≥2.5 micron) [FACT-source: 16 CFR §23, eCFR — flag micron/karat thresholds for verification against live text before any labeling use].
- **Vermeil:** **sterling-silver base**, gold ≥10K, **≥2.5 micron** on all significant surfaces [FACT-source: 16 CFR §23.4].
- **Gold-filled / overlay / rolled gold plate:** ≥10K layer **mechanically bonded**, ≥1/20 of weight [FACT-source: 16 CFR §23].
- **Sterling silver = 92.5% Ag; "rhodium" is a regulated PGM quality-mark term** [FACT-source: 16 CFR §23].
- **Marketing terms ("gold-tone," "gold-dipped," "18K gold plated" without micron) are NOT FTC finish classes** — a SKU using them is routed to an [UNKNOWN]-quality bucket, not counted as verified plate.

**Rhodium positioning [FACT — materials fact, plating science owned by `jewel_materials`]:** rhodium is a hard, bright-white, highly reflective, tarnish- and corrosion-resistant **platinum-group metal**, electroplated over sterling silver or white gold to give a bright white finish and slow tarnish — the owner's "keeps silver shiny / prevents un-shine" intuition is directionally correct. It sits as the **premium white-metal option in the fashion core** of the finish ladder. Combined with a gold-plated tone, the brand's "rhodiumgold" grounding maps cleanly onto: **rhodium-plated silver-tone (bright white) + gold-plated tone (warm)** — two finishes on the same fashion rung.

### 6. Price-tier landscape (listed prices, positioning not transactions)

Observed **listed** gold-plated prices on a marketplace scan (Etsy market pages): roughly **$19–$179, with a large cluster under $25 and most under $60** [FACT-source: Etsy gold-plated listings, July 2026]. Corroborated by the Gen-Z "under $500, usually far under" affordability signal and the ~$35 fashion-average compilation stat (§3).

**[METHOD — KB3 price-tier ladder]:** build brackets from **listed** prices across a provenance-tagged storefront sample, and **flag the listed-vs-realized gap** — discounts (the Etsy scan showed 25–40% off), promos, bundles, and returns move realized price below listed, so this is a **positioning ladder, never measured transaction prices**. Keystone (~2x wholesale) explains why bands separate finish tiers. Indicative ladder to confirm, not assert:

| Tier | Indicative listed band | Finish character |
|---|---|---|
| Mass / costume | ~$5–20 | base metal, thin/again plate |
| **Mid fashion (venture's likely lane)** | **~$20–60** | **rhodium-plated silver / gold electroplate** |
| Premium demi-fine adjacent | ~$60–150 | vermeil, gold-filled, heavier plate |
| Fine | $150+ | solid gold / karat-marked |

### 7. Competitor / brand landscape — a METHOD, not a list of asserted rivals

**Honesty rule (KB3):** the competitor set is defined as public storefront **CATEGORIES, not named rivals with measured share.** A market-research report does list companies "covered" for US costume jewelry — Louis Vuitton, Cartier, Sabika, Yanbal, Richline, Stella & Dot, Lulu Ave, Giorgio Armani [FACT-source: GlobeNewswire / vendor report, Nov 2025] — but per method **these are named as sector players in a vendor report, NOT asserted as this store's verified competitors with measured share**, and most (LV, Cartier, Armani) are not even in the plated-DTC lane. Do not treat that list as your competitive set.

**[METHOD] to map the real landscape (do this, don't guess):**
1. **Frame competitors as four public categories:** (a) DTC plated-fashion brands, (b) marketplace sellers (Etsy/Amazon/Temu), (c) fast-fashion accessory (mall/e-comm), (d) demi-fine adjacent (vermeil/gold-filled DTC).
2. **Sample storefronts with provenance** (discovery path, date, sampling-bias flag) and harvest per-SKU observable attributes: category, finish wording, base metal, listed price, positioning copy.
3. **Normalize finish wording onto the FTC definitions** (§5); flag vague/non-compliant claims as [UNKNOWN]-quality.
4. **Plot a price-tier × finish/style positioning map**; read crowding vs gaps as **whitespace *hypotheses to confirm with demand signals* — never asserted openings** (an empty cell can mean unmet need OR absent demand).
5. **Trend durability (KB3):** before betting a SKU on a trend, require **≥2–3 independent signal families to agree** (search volume, social engagement, marketplace rank, retail newness, editorial) and separate durable classics (studs, hoops, huggies, tennis chains) from single-platform fads. Read every signal as ordinal, never a unit count.
