## Domain 1 — Technical / Process

| # | Risk | Mechanism | Severity | Likelihood | Mitigation | Kill-criterion |
|---|------|-----------|----------|------------|------------|----------------|
| T1 | Moving-wire elastomer-seal feedthrough cannot hold sputtering-grade vacuum (Q1, core machine concept) | [FACT] Magnetron sputtering needs a clean base pressure of roughly 1e-5 to 1e-6 Torr before backfilling argon to a few mTorr; residual water/oxygen degrades film adhesion and color. A wire sliding through thick rubber leaks by: (a) gas dragged in along the moving wire surface; (b) micro-channels at the wire–elastomer interface (real wire is neither perfectly round nor smooth); (c) permeation/outgassing of the elastomer; (d) progressive seal abrasion enlarging the leak path and shedding particles. [FACT] Industrial continuous (air-to-air) PVD wire/strip coaters do not use sliding elastomer seals for high vacuum: they use differential pumping (separately pumped close-clearance orifice stages), load-locks, or in-chamber spool-to-spool payoff. A single rubber seal is a leak, not a solution. | high | [ESTIMATE] High (>90%) that the naive single-rubber-seal design fails to reach usable base pressure; method: standard vacuum-engineering leak physics plus absence of any industrial precedent for sliding elastomer high-vacuum wire seals; confidence high. | (1) Differential pumping: 2–3 small orifice stages per side, each with its own roughing pump, wire never touching orifice walls; or (2) eliminate the feedthrough: payoff and take-up spools inside the chamber (batch-continuous, one spool per pump-down) — the standard DIY-feasible answer; or (3) load-lock spool exchange. Partial good news [FACT]: sputtering RUNS at mTorr working pressure, so seals only preserve mTorr against atmosphere during deposition — but the chamber must still first reach clean base pressure; in-chamber spooling sidesteps both problems. | If a prototype with N affordable differential-pumping stages cannot reach base pressure below ~1e-4 Torr with the wire stationary, or cannot hold stable working pressure with acceptable water/O2 background with the wire moving, abandon air-to-air feed-through and commit to in-chamber spool-to-spool. |
| T2 | Coating destroyed by post-coat forming: bending coated wire into rings cracks or delaminates the gold stack (Q3) | [FACT] Bending wire of radius r around bend radius R imposes outer-fiber tensile strain ≈ r/R: e.g. 1 mm wire into a ~17 mm ID ring ≈ 5–6% surface strain. Soft pure gold is very ductile, but (a) hardened (Co/Ni-brightened) gold electrodeposits crack at a few percent strain, (b) a hard nickel barrier interlayer cracks first and telegraphs cracks through the gold, (c) any adhesion weakness becomes flaking at the strained interface. Heat-assisted forming makes it worse: [FACT] heating plated steel promotes substrate oxidation at bare/porous spots and drives Au–Ni and Au–Fe interdiffusion (significant above roughly 150–200 °C over time), discoloring and embrittling the coating. Industry standard is form-first-then-plate for exactly this reason. | high | [ESTIMATE] High (70–90%) for hard-gold or nickel-barrier stacks at ring-forming strains; medium (30–50%) for thin soft gold on a ductile interlayer; method: strain arithmetic vs published ductility classes of electrodeposits; confidence medium-high. | Prefer form-then-plate (bend bare wire into rings, then coat) — dissolves the entire risk; honest default recommendation. If plate-then-form is retained: soft (99.9%) gold over a ductile interlayer (sulfamate nickel or palladium in ductile condition, or copper), maximize bend radius / minimize wire diameter, form cold on a polished mandrel with no sliding contact, and qualify every candidate stack with the [FACT] ASTM B571 bend-to-fracture adhesion test at the actual ring radius before committing. | If bend tests at the target ring radius show through-coating cracking or flaking on all affordable coating stacks, plate-then-form is dead; pivot to form-then-plate (machine concept changes from wire feed-through to rack/barrel batch coating of formed rings). |
| T3 | Porosity and wear-through at "minimum gold per piece" thickness: rust spots and rapid gold loss on a high-wear product (rings) | [FACT] Thin gold films are porous; below roughly 1 µm, pores expose the substrate. [FACT] Gold is strongly cathodic to steel, so every pore becomes a galvanic cell in sweat/humidity — steel corrodes preferentially and rust blooms through the gold. [FACT] Recognized thickness classes: US FTC "gold electroplate" ≥0.175 µm, "heavy gold electroplate" ≥2.5 µm, vermeil ≥2.5 µm gold (on silver); decorative sputtered gold is typically only 0.05–0.3 µm. Rings are among the highest-abrasion jewelry items; sub-micron gold on a ring wears through in weeks. The core cost goal (least gold per piece) pulls directly against the premium/scratch-resistant goal. | high | [ESTIMATE] High (>80%) that flash-thickness (<0.5 µm) gold on steel rings shows corrosion spots or wear-through within months of daily wear; method: known porosity-vs-thickness behavior of gold deposits plus galvanic couple physics; confidence high. | Engineer the stack, not just the gold: dense leveling barrier interlayer 2–5 µm (nickel — noting [FACT] nickel is a Class 1 skin sensitizer and the EU restricts nickel release from jewelry, so palladium or bronze barriers are the compliant alternative), then ≥1–2.5 µm gold for ring wear surfaces; run porosity acceptance tests (nitric-acid-vapor porosity test, neutral salt spray) and abrasion tests before launch; position thinner-gold products as low-wear items (earrings, pendants), not rings. | If the cost model (Q11) shows the gold+barrier thickness needed to pass porosity/wear acceptance makes cost per ring exceed the market price of competing vermeil or gold-filled product, steel-ring economics are dead at premium positioning — retreat to fashion-grade positioning or different substrate. |
| T4 | Sputtering cannot economically deliver durable jewelry thickness; wrong tool for the thickness layer (Q6 cheapest-vs-best) | [FACT] Sputtering is line-of-sight: a round wire needs rotation or multiple opposed targets for full-circumference coverage. [FACT] Planar magnetron targets erode in a racetrack with typically only ~20–40% target-material utilization — a large fraction of an expensive gold target is locked-up capital (recoverable, but not usable film). Practical DIY-scale gold sputter rates are of order 0.1–1 µm/h at the substrate, so reaching ≥1–2.5 µm durable class means very slow line speeds or many passes, while [FACT] gold electroplating deposits micrometers in minutes at mA/cm² with near-100% anode/salt utilization. Sputtering's economic niche here is the thin decorative/color/seed layer, not the thickness layer. | med | [ESTIMATE] High (>80%) that an all-sputtered route fails the cost-per-durable-piece comparison against electroplating; method: rate and utilization arithmetic from standard magnetron practice; confidence high. | Hybrid architecture: electroplating (or electroless) for the thickness build; reserve PVD for what it is uniquely good at — [FACT] the watch-industry "gold IP" route: hard PVD nitride (TiN/ZrN, gold-colored, HV ~2000+) as the scratch-resistant body with a thin true-gold sputtered top flash for color and skin contact. Simultaneously answers Q9 (scratch resistance) better than any all-gold route. | Measure it: if (deposition rate × coated circumference fraction × line speed) cannot reach target thickness at required throughput within the machine's duty cycle and target cost, drop sputtering as the thickness route (keep as color/seed/IP topcoat only). |
| T5 | Fresh soft gold is scratched by the machine itself: exit seal and guides burnish the coating (Q2) | [FACT] Pure gold is one of the softest metals in use (electrodeposited soft gold ~HV 20–90; hardened gold ~HV 120–200); any sliding contact marks it. The owner's exit concept — coated wire dragging through a thick rubber seal — guarantees continuous sliding contact on the freshest, softest surface, plus every downstream guide, pulley and the take-up spool where turns of coated wire press and rub against each other under winding tension. | high | [ESTIMATE] High (~90%) for a rubber exit seal; medium (40–60%) for ordinary guides/spooling without specific design care; method: hardness of gold vs contact stresses in wire handling; confidence high. | Make the exit non-contact (differential-pumping orifices the wire never touches — same fix as T1); place the last driven/contacting element before the deposition zone; large-diameter soft-covered rollers only if contact is unavoidable; wind with interleaf or large-pitch traverse and minimal tension; or coat already-formed pieces so no wire handling follows coating. | If coated-wire handling trials show visible burnishing/scratching at acceptance-test magnification after any economically buildable exit + take-up arrangement, the continuous coated-wire format fails; coat formed pieces instead. |
| T6 | DIY machine capability ceiling: home-built high-vacuum + magnetron system underperforms and contaminates films | A working sputter coater is a system, not a chamber: [FACT] two-stage pumping train (roughing + turbo/diffusion), vacuum gauging across 9 decades, DC (or pulsed-DC/RF) magnetron supply with arc suppression, mass-flow-controlled argon, water-cooled target mounting — a poorly cooled thin gold target warps, debonds or locally melts under plasma heat load. DIY chambers with elastomer everywhere and no bakeout carry high water outgassing: films come out hazy, dark, poorly adherent. Each subsystem is individually buildable; integrating them to run repeatably and "maximally autonomously" at home is where DIY projects stall. | med | [ESTIMATE] Medium-high (50–70%) that a from-scratch DIY build either misses film-quality spec or costs more in time+parts than a used commercial unit; method: comparison with hobbyist sputtering community outcomes and the used-equipment market; confidence medium. | Buy-before-build for the vacuum core: used SEM sputter coaters deposit gold routinely and appear on the secondary market; spend DIY effort on wire-transport/fixturing around a proven coater. Stage the ambition: batch coater first, prove film quality with acceptance tests (XRF thickness, tape/bend adhesion, porosity), only then attempt continuous transport. [UNKNOWN: actual local availability and price of used coaters — a supplier-search task, not to be invented here.] | Set a budget/time box: if the DIY vacuum core exceeds the delivered price of a used commercial coater of equal capability before producing an acceptance-passing film, stop building and buy. |
| T7 | Electroplating chemistry: hazard class and bath-control complexity of gold plating on steel at home (Q8) | [FACT] The highest-performance gold plating baths are based on potassium gold cyanide, KAu(CN)2 — hazard class: acutely toxic by ingestion, skin contact and inhalation (cyanide; GHS H300/H310/H330), with lethal HCN gas evolved on acid contact; not home chemistry. [FACT] Named cyanide-free alternative: the gold sulfite (and thiosulfate) bath family — safer, but less stable and more contamination-sensitive. [FACT] Plating gold directly onto steel/stainless fails without preparation: stainless carries a passive chromium-oxide film requiring a Wood's nickel strike for adhesion; immersion (displacement) gold on bare steel gives non-adherent smut unless a strike is used. Bath control (pH, gold content, contaminants, drag-out losses) is a continuing discipline; drag-out is also a direct gold-cost leak. | high | [ESTIMATE] Medium (40–60%) that an untrained home operator experiences adhesion failures, bath poisoning/instability, or a safety-relevant handling event within the first production year on self-mixed chemistry; method: known failure modes of small plating shops; confidence medium. | Never run cyanide chemistry at home — adopt sulfite-based commercial pre-mixed solutions in small volumes with full PPE, ventilation, spill containment and documented waste disposal; use the standard sequence (degrease → pickle/activate → Wood's nickel or proprietary strike → barrier → gold) as a written spec with per-step acceptance checks; alternatively outsource plating entirely and keep only forming/finishing in-house. | If the operator's premises, ventilation, waste-disposal route or jurisdiction cannot lawfully and safely support even the cyanide-free bath, in-house electroplating is dead; outsource plating or go PVD-only. |
| T8 | "Scratch-resistant premium gold look" is physically self-contradictory in an all-gold coating (Q9) | [FACT] Softness is intrinsic to high-purity gold; at jewelry coating thickness no pure-gold surface is scratch resistant. Hardening options each cost something: Co/Ni-hardened gold gains hardness (~HV 120–200) but loses ductility (conflicts with T2 forming) and shifts color; clear organic/ceramic topcoats alter the look and wear off unevenly; only a hard sublayer architecture — [FACT] the PVD TiN/ZrN "gold IP" stack used across the watch industry, nitride hardness of order HV 2000 carrying the mechanical load under a thin real-gold flash — delivers both scratch resistance and gold appearance, and it requires PVD, not electroplating. | med | [ESTIMATE] High (>80%) that the goal as imagined (thick soft gold that resists scratching) is unattainable and must be redefined as a stack-architecture choice; method: hardness physics of gold vs abrasive encounters in daily wear; confidence high. | Redefine the premium spec as a stack: hard nitride PVD body + thin gold top flash for the premium line (where the owner's sputtering interest genuinely pays off), hardened electroplated gold at 1–2.5 µm for the mid line; define scratch resistance by a measurable acceptance test (pencil/Taber or defined-load stylus), not adjectives. | None hard — mitigable by architecture; kills only the specific "thick soft gold = scratch-proof" assumption, not the venture. |
| T9 | Substrate/heat mismatch: chosen steel plus heat-assisted forming degrades the stack (Q5) | Interlocking substrate problems: [FACT] carbon steel scales/oxidizes when heated in air (visible temper colors from ~200–300 °C), wrecking any coated or to-be-coated surface; [FACT] Au–Ni interdiffusion becomes significant with time above roughly 150–200 °C, dulling color and embrittling the interface — "heat-assisted bending" of plated wire sits in exactly the harmful window; [FACT] spring steels resist forming (high yield strain, springback) while stainless needs strike activation to plate at all (see T7); mild steel forms easily but rusts aggressively at any coating pore (see T3). No single off-the-shelf steel is simultaneously easy-plating, easy-forming, and corrosion-benign. | med | [ESTIMATE] Medium-high (50–70%) that the first substrate choice needs revision after forming/corrosion trials; method: enumeration of the conflicting requirements; confidence medium. | Form cold on annealed ductile substrate (annealed 304/316 stainless, or copper/brass wire — the fashion-jewelry norm, plates easily) and eliminate the heating step; if steel spring-temper is wanted for fit, form and heat-treat BEFORE plating; keep any post-plate thermal excursion below ~150 °C and brief. Run a small substrate matrix trial (3–4 candidate wires through the full process) before locking the spec. | If no candidate substrate passes forming + adhesion + porosity acceptance together, the wire-format product must change (e.g. to cast/stamped blanks, plated after forming). |
| T10 | Houseware extension: glass/ceramic plating and food contact do not inherit the jewelry process (Q10) | [FACT] Electroplating requires a conductive surface — bare glass and glazed ceramic cannot be electroplated without prior metallization (electroless silvering/copper, conductive paint, or a PVD seed). [FACT] Metal adheres poorly to glass without a reactive adhesion layer (Cr or Ti under the gold in PVD practice). [FACT] Metallic gold itself is inert with food-use precedent, but the interlayers that make thin coatings viable (nickel, copper, adhesion metals) are exactly the layers exposed at pores and wear-through, and food-contact articles fall under food-contact-materials regulation (e.g. EU Regulation 1935/2004; FDA in the US) — a different compliance regime than jewelry. Cutlery and cups also see dishwashing chemistry and utensil abrasion far harsher than jewelry wear. | med | [ESTIMATE] High (>80%) that a straight transplant of the jewelry process onto glasses/cups/spoons fails technically or on food-contact compliance; method: conductivity/adhesion physics plus the regulatory boundary; confidence high. | Treat houseware as a separate later project: PVD with Cr/Ti adhesion layers on exterior, non-food-contact, non-dishwasher decorative zones only (rim exteriors, stems, handles); label accordingly; or buy pre-metallized blanks and decorate. Do not gold-coat the food-contact bowl of spoons or the lip zone of cups until a compliant stack is qualified. [UNKNOWN: the owner's target market's specific food-contact rules — jurisdiction not stated.] | If food-contact qualification of the stack is required for the intended products and its testing/compliance cost exceeds the houseware line's projected margin, keep the extension decorative-only or drop it. |

## Domain 2 — Business / Economic

| # | Risk | Mechanism | Severity | Likelihood | Mitigation | Kill-criterion |
|---|------|-----------|----------|------------|------------|----------------|
| B1 | Unit-economics inversion: minimizing gold per piece optimizes the smallest cost line | [FACT] At jewelry-plating thicknesses (flash ~0.1–0.5 µm up to "heavy" 2.5 µm), the gold mass on a ring-sized piece of steel wire is of order milligrams or less; gold material cost per piece is therefore small compared with the true per-piece cost drivers at home scale: owner labor, energy, vacuum/bath consumables, rejects, and machine amortization at low utilization. The brief's central lever — "least metal" per piece — attacks a minor cost line. [ESTIMATE — method: order-of-magnitude coating-mass calculation vs typical craft labor and capex amortization at tens-to-hundreds of pieces/month; confidence medium] gold is plausibly under ~10–20% of fully loaded cost per acceptable piece at realistic DIY throughput, so halving gold thickness barely moves cost while directly degrading wear life and perceived value (couples to B5). | high | high — this inversion is structural, not scenario-dependent | Build the Q11 cost model FIRST with gold as one explicit line among labor, energy, consumables, reject rate, and amortization; run sensitivity on gold thickness to show where cost actually lives; set thickness by durability/complaint-rate target, not gold-minimization. | If the completed cost model shows non-gold cost per acceptable piece already exceeds the achievable retail price of comparable plated jewelry at every realistic throughput, the minimum-gold strategy is moot and the venture must repivot (design/brand premium) or stop. |
| B2 | Buy-vs-build trap: the machine becomes the project and consumes the venture | [FACT] The riskiest, most novel element (continuous moving-wire vacuum feedthrough through elastomer seals) is NOT required to sell gold-plated rings — contract electroplating/PVD services and off-the-shelf benchtop plating kits reach market in weeks. The owner's own Q7 (DIY vs buy vs commission) is exactly the decision this risk sits inside. | high | high — the brief's emotional center of gravity is the machine, not the sales channel | Gate rule: first revenue must come from outsourced coating (contract plater) or bought-in comparable product before any machine capex; time-box and budget-box any DIY build as a separate cost center; use the planned supplier-outreach drafts to obtain contract-plating and custom-build quotes so DIY is always compared against a real number, not an imagined one. | DIY build exceeds its time/budget box by 2x without producing a single piece that passes the Q8 acceptance tests → stop building, switch to buy/outsource; separately, if a contract-plating quote beats projected DIY cost per piece at target volume, do not build. |
| B3 | Machine capex vs realistic output: a continuous line at hobby volume has ruinous cost per piece | [FACT] A continuous feed-through coater is an equipment class scaled for continuous meters of wire; a one-person jewelry venture plausibly sells tens to low hundreds of pieces per month, so even a working machine runs at a few percent utilization and amortization dominates per-piece cost. [ESTIMATE — method: vacuum-component class reasoning (pumps, gauges, PSU, chamber, gold target), no specific vendor prices asserted; confidence low-medium] a self-built batch sputtering rig sits in a low-thousands to tens-of-thousands USD-equivalent band; a commissioned custom continuous wire line plausibly an order of magnitude above. [UNKNOWN — actual quotes; blocker: supplier outreach (Q7 drafts) not yet executed] — real numbers must come from the saved outreach round, never invented. | high | high if the continuous machine is built before demand is proven; medium if batch-first path is taken | Sequence capex to demonstrated demand: (1) contract plating / bench batch kit, (2) small batch sputter or plating cell, (3) continuous machine ONLY when the order book saturates batch capacity; evaluate the continuous concept as a scale-up option in the cost model, not the starting point. | If amortized machine cost per piece at demonstrated (not nameplate) volume exceeds the best contract-plating quote per piece by a set margin (e.g. >2x) after the pilot quarter, abandon in-house coating for that volume tier. |
| B4 | Competition from mature gold-plated / PVD jewelry supply chains | [FACT] Gold-flash-plated and gold-PVD (ion-plated stainless) jewelry is a mature commodity produced by industrial platers/coaters at very large scale and sold at low retail price points; those lines have amortized equipment, bulk gold purchasing, and debugged processes. A home operation cannot win on price — only on design, customization, story/brand, or local service. The brief as stated (cheapest possible plated ring on steel wire) points straight at the segment where incumbents are strongest. | high | high — the commodity segment exists today and is directly substitutable | Reposition away from commodity: designer/limited/custom pieces where the coating is an enabler, not the product; validate the price premium empirically with a small test batch produced via contract plating BEFORE any machine spend; treat the DIY machine, if built, as a capability for fast custom iteration rather than a cost weapon. | If pilot-channel sales only move at or below prevailing commodity plated-jewelry price points (no measurable premium for design/brand after a defined test period), the business case for in-house manufacture fails; remain a designer sourcing finished blanks, or stop. |
| B5 | Perceived-value ceiling and labeling constraints on "premium-look" thin plate | [FACT] The jewelry market and its regulators distinguish solid gold / gold-filled / vermeil / gold-plated / flash; marking rules (e.g. US FTC Jewelry Guides and national hallmarking regimes) restrict what may be called "gold" and some tiers require minimum thickness and truthful qualification — thin plate on steel sits at the bottom of the perceived-value ladder regardless of day-one looks. Minimum-gold-per-piece pushes toward flash thickness, where wear-through and tarnish complaints arrive within weeks-to-months of daily wear, destroying marketplace reviews — the primary sales asset of a small brand. Compliant labeling ("gold-plated steel") caps the price the look can command. | high | medium-high — certain if flash thickness is chosen for cost reasons | Pick a defensible durability tier and market on it (e.g. thicker plate with hard-gold alloy or PVD-over-stainless positioned on scratch resistance — aligns with the Q9 goal); label truthfully from day one; define a wear/complaint threshold in the Q8 acceptance tests; offer a re-plate or replacement warranty priced into margin. | If pilot sales show wear-through returns/complaints above a preset threshold (e.g. >5–10% of units in the first 90 days) at the chosen thickness, and moving to the durable tier makes cost per piece exceed the validated price, kill the cheap tier. |
| B6 | Gold price exposure on targets, baths, and unsold finished stock | [FACT] Gold-bearing consumables front-load capital: a sputtering target is mostly inventory (planar magnetron targets typically reach only roughly 20–40% material utilization before replacement — established sputtering practice), and a gold plating bath carries dissolved gold whether or not pieces are being made. Gold spot volatility marks this working capital up and down. Critically, gold locked in FINISHED thin-plated goods is effectively unrecoverable at small scale (refining milligram coatings off steel is uneconomic), so unsold inventory converts gold from asset to write-off. [ESTIMATE — method: coating-mass and small-target gold-content order of magnitude, no spot price asserted; confidence medium] minimum viable gold inventory (one small target OR one small bath make-up) sits in a hundreds-to-low-thousands USD-equivalent capital band at recent gold price levels; [UNKNOWN — exact figure requires current spot price and specific target/bath size at purchase time]. | med | high — price volatility and inventory lock-up are certainties; magnitude is the variable | Buy gold consumables just-in-time against orders; use supplier target-reclaim/buy-back programs ([FACT] spent-target and bath reclaim services are standard in the industry — names via the Q7 outreach round, not invented here); keep finished-goods stock minimal (made-to-order); if quoting ahead, add a gold-surcharge clause; store gold stock and gold-bearing product per the P-series practical-storage KB (secure, insured, documented). | If the minimum viable gold inventory plus safety stock exceeds the owner's declared risk capital for consumables, defer in-house coating and stay on contract plating (where the plater carries the gold float). |
| B7 | Plate-then-form reject economics: yield uncertainty multiplies cost per acceptable piece | [FACT] The proposed sequence (coat straight wire, then heat-assist bend into rings) is inverted relative to standard industry practice (form first, then plate), precisely because bending a coated wire strains the coating at the bend radius and risks cracking, crazing, or adhesion failure — every reject carries the FULL process cost plus its locked, unrecoverable gold. Cost per acceptable piece = cost per attempt / yield, so yield is a first-order economic variable, not a technical footnote. [ESTIMATE — method: generic learning-curve behavior of novel small-scale processes; confidence medium] early-phase yields for an undebugged plate-then-form route can plausibly sit below 50%, doubling-plus effective unit cost until stabilized. [UNKNOWN — actual achievable yield; blocker: no pilot data exists yet; it is the Q3/Q4 specialists' first measurement.] | high | high in the first production phase; medium after debugging IF the route proves viable at all | Make measured yield a mandatory input to pricing (no price list before a pilot yield number); carry form-then-plate as the engineering baseline against which plate-then-form must justify itself economically; include an explicit reject/rework line in the Q11 cost model; scrap-stream discipline (segregate gold-bearing rejects for eventual bulk reclaim). | If stabilized pilot yield leaves cost per acceptable piece above the form-then-plate (in-house or outsourced) alternative, abandon plate-then-form as a production route — it remains at most a niche capability. |
| B8 | Houseware extension: food-contact compliance costs can dwarf the niche's revenue | [FACT] Plated cups and spoons are food-contact articles subject to dedicated regimes (e.g. EU Regulation 1935/2004, US FDA food-contact requirements; metal-release/migration limits), and common gold-plating stacks use nickel interlayers — nickel release is specifically regulated (EU nickel-release limits for skin contact; stricter scrutiny for food contact) and is an allergy/liability issue. Compliance means per-SKU migration testing at accredited labs, documentation, and ongoing liability exposure. [FACT] Glass is non-conductive, so the electroplating route does not directly apply — houseware needs different process work (PVD or metallization seed), i.e. additional engineering spend on top of compliance spend, for a side extension of the brief. [UNKNOWN — actual lab testing cost per SKU in the owner's jurisdiction; blocker: jurisdiction and lab quotes not yet gathered.] | med | medium — materializes only if the houseware extension is pursued as food-contact product | Defer houseware until the jewelry line is cash-positive; if pursued, either position as decorative/non-food-surface only (gold on exterior, declared not food-contact) or obtain lab quotes and regulatory scoping BEFORE any process development; never ship gold-on-nickel-on-steel drinkware without migration test reports. | If compliance testing plus process adaptation cost per houseware SKU exceeds the projected first-year gross margin of that SKU, keep the extension decorative-only or drop it. |
| B9 | The "maximally autonomous" assumption: unattended-operation savings that never materialize | [FACT] Home-built vacuum/plasma and plating equipment in practice requires attended operation — seal wear on a moving-wire feedthrough, plasma arcing, pump behavior, bath chemistry drift are all supervision-demanding failure modes (exactly the "common errors and breakdowns" KB the brief itself requests). If the business case assumes lights-out continuous operation (labor cost ~0 per meter of wire) and reality requires the owner present, the labor line silently re-enters the cost model and can flip contribution margin negative. Autonomy is an outcome of a debugged, instrumented, interlocked process — a late-stage achievement, not a design input. | med | high — for any first-build DIY machine | Cost-model with ATTENDED operation as the base case; treat autonomy as a staged upgrade (sensors, interlocks, auto-shutdown) justified only by measured stable runs; require N consecutive fault-free attended runs before any unattended economics are admitted into pricing. | If the venture's economics only close under unattended continuous operation, and a defined pilot period cannot demonstrate safe fault-free unattended runs, the continuous-machine business case fails — revert to batch/attended sizing or outsource. |

## Domain 3 — Safety / Regulatory / Compliance

| # | Risk | Mechanism | Severity | Likelihood | Mitigation | Kill-criterion |
|---|------|-----------|----------|------------|------------|----------------|
| S1 | Cyanide gold electroplating bath — acute lethal toxicity in a home setting | [FACT] The industry-standard gold electroplating electrolyte is potassium gold cyanide (KAu(CN)2), an acutely toxic cyanide salt; [FACT] contact of any cyanide bath with acid (e.g. the pickling acids in the same process's prep line) evolves hydrogen cyanide gas, rapidly lethal in an unventilated home workshop. Skin absorption and accidental ingestion routes also exist. [FACT] Cyanide-free alternatives exist and are commercially established: gold(I) sulfite baths and thiosulfate(-sulfite) baths; they trade some deposit hardness/bath stability but remove the acute-lethality class. | high | High that the hazard is encountered if the electroplating route is pursued naively, because most published gold-plating recipes and many commercial concentrates are cyanide-based [FACT]; the exposure event itself is low-probability per run but catastrophic. [ESTIMATE, high confidence in the framing] | Hard rule: cyanide chemistry is excluded at home — specify cyanide-free sulfite or thiosulfate gold baths only, sourced as commercial ready-made concentrates (never self-mixed from gold salts); physically segregate all acids from any plating chemicals; ventilated bench, gloves, eye protection, spill kit; keep bath volumes small (liters, not tens of liters). | If acceptance tests show the premium scratch-resistance/hardness spec cannot be met by any cyanide-free bath plus interlayer strategy, do NOT escalate to cyanide at home — that branch is killed for the home site; it may only continue in a licensed industrial facility or by outsourcing the plating step. |
| S2 | Illegal effluent and hazardous-waste disposal from the plating line (spent baths, drag-out, rinse water) | [FACT] Spent plating baths, strike solutions, pickling acids and metal-bearing rinse waters are classed as industrial/hazardous waste in essentially all developed jurisdictions; discharging them to a domestic drain or septic system violates wastewater-pretreatment / hazardous-waste rule classes and is the single most common legal failure mode of home plating shops. ## Cross-domain top-5 (ranked)

**1. The signature machine concept is dead as drawn: a sliding rubber seal on a moving wire neither holds vacuum nor spares the coating (T1 + T5 + S3).**
[FACT] Sputtering needs ~1e-5–1e-6 Torr base pressure; a wire dragged through an elastomer seal leaks by gas drag, interface micro-channels, permeation and abrasion, and no industrial air-to-air PVD wire coater uses sliding elastomer seals — they use differential pumping, load-locks, or in-chamber spool-to-spool. [FACT] The same exit seal slides over the freshest, softest gold (soft gold ~HV 20–90) and burnishes it. [ESTIMATE] >90% failure likelihood for the naive design (vacuum-engineering leak physics; confidence high). Mitigation: in-chamber spool-to-spool (batch-continuous) or differential-pumping orifice stages the wire never touches; safety side requires metal chamber + interlocked HV regardless. Kill: prototype cannot reach <~1e-4 Torr base with wire stationary or hold stable working pressure with wire moving → abandon air-to-air feed-through.

**2. Plate-then-form is backwards: ring-bending strain cracks the coating, and every reject carries full process cost plus unrecoverable gold (T2 + B7).**
[FACT] Bending imposes ~r/R outer-fiber strain (~5–6% for 1 mm wire into a ~17 mm ring); hardened gold and nickel barriers crack at a few percent strain, and heat-assisted forming drives Au–Ni/Au–Fe interdiffusion above ~150–200 °C. Industry does form-then-plate for exactly this reason. Economically, cost per acceptable piece = cost per attempt / yield, and [ESTIMATE] early yields of an undebugged plate-then-form route can plausibly sit below 50% (learning-curve reasoning; confidence medium). [UNKNOWN] actual achievable yield — no pilot data exists. Mitigation: adopt form-then-plate as the engineering and economic baseline (this pivot also dissolves risk #1 by removing the wire feed-through entirely); qualify any plate-then-form stack with ASTM B571 bend tests at the actual ring radius [FACT]. Kill: bend tests fail on all affordable stacks, or stabilized pilot yield leaves unit cost above the form-then-plate alternative.

**3. The machine consumes the venture: buy-vs-build trap plus ruinous amortization at hobby volume (B2 + B3 + T6 + B9).**
[FACT] Contract plating/PVD services and benchtop kits reach market in weeks without any machine; a continuous line at tens-to-hundreds of pieces/month runs at a few percent utilization, so amortization dominates per-piece cost, and [FACT] first-build DIY vacuum equipment requires attended operation — the "maximally autonomous" labor saving is a late-stage outcome, not a design input. [ESTIMATE] 50–70% that a from-scratch DIY build misses film-quality spec or costs more than a used commercial coater (hobbyist-community outcomes; confidence medium). [UNKNOWN] real capex — supplier quotes not yet gathered (Q7 outreach drafts), never invented. Mitigation gate: first revenue from outsourced coating before any machine capex; buy a used SEM sputter coater before building; time/budget-box any build. Kill: build exceeds its box by 2x without one acceptance-passing piece, or a contract-plating quote beats projected DIY cost per piece at target volume.

**4. Home wet chemistry is the compliance chokepoint: cyanide toxicity, hazardous effluent, and zoning/insurance all converge on "keep the home site dry" (S1 + S2 + S7 + T7).**
[FACT] The standard gold bath is potassium gold cyanide KAu(CN)2 — acutely toxic (GHS H300/H310/H330), evolving lethal HCN on acid contact — excluded from home use outright; the named cyanide-free alternative is the sulfite/thiosulfate bath family. [FACT] Spent baths and rinse waters are hazardous waste in essentially all developed jurisdictions (drain disposal illegal — rule class; specific statute [UNKNOWN], jurisdiction unstated), and residential zoning/insurance classes typically exclude undisclosed chemical manufacturing. Three independent risks converge on one architecture: PVD-dry-at-home, wet chemistry outsourced or moved to a licensed/industrial site. Kill: no legal affordable waste route, or zoning/insurance definitively excludes the wet line → home wet plating is dead; go PVD-only at home or outsource plating (the venture continues, the SITE configuration changes).

**5. The "minimum gold, premium look, cheapest price" triangle does not close: gold-thrift optimizes the smallest cost line while destroying durability, labeling headroom, and competitiveness (B1 + T3 + B5 + B4 + T8).**
[FACT] At plating thickness the gold per ring is milligrams — [ESTIMATE] plausibly under ~10–20% of fully loaded unit cost at DIY throughput (order-of-magnitude mass vs labor/amortization; confidence medium) — while [FACT] sub-micron gold over steel is porous and galvanically rust-prone, wears through on rings in weeks-to-months, drops the product into the lowest labeling tier (FTC/hallmarking rule classes tie descriptors to thickness), and lands in the commodity segment where industrial platers are strongest. [FACT] The only stack that delivers scratch resistance AND gold look is the watch-industry PVD TiN/ZrN "gold IP" hard body (~HV 2000) under a thin gold flash — which is where the owner's sputtering interest genuinely pays off. Mitigation: set thickness by durability/complaint target, position on design/brand/scratch-resistance, not price. Kill: cost model shows the durable stack exceeds validated market price at every positioning, or pilot sales command no premium over commodity plated goods.
