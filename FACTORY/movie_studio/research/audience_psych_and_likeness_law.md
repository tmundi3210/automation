# PART A — AUDIENCE PSYCHOLOGY

## A1. Visual attention: faces, gaze direction, motion/contrast salience

### A1.1 Faces capture early attention (well-replicated)
- [FACT] Faces attract gaze early and largely independent of task. Cerf, Frady & Koch, "Faces and text attract gaze independent of the task: Experimental data and computer model" (*Journal of Vision*, 2009) tracked eye movements in free-viewing and search tasks and found adults fixate faces preferentially; adding a face-detection channel (Viola-Jones) to a bottom-up Itti-Koch saliency model raised fixation-prediction AUC to >0.84, versus ~0.53 for the classic bottom-up model on natural scenes. Interpretation in the literature: face-fixation is not explained by low-level physical saliency alone — it is a semantic/social pull. (Source 1; PubMed 20053101.)
- [FACT] The "faces beat salience" pattern replicates in newer real-world-stimulus work (e.g., "Faces strongly attract early fixations in naturally sampled real-world stimulus materials," ACM ETRA 2020). (Source 2.)
- [ESTIMATE — high confidence] Practical read: in a 3–6 s reel frame, a human face is the near-certain first-fixation anchor. Craft implication: put the face where you want attention to START, then use composition/motion to hand attention off to the product/setting. Basis: convergent eye-tracking evidence above.

### A1.2 Gaze-direction (gaze cueing) effects — REAL but CONTESTED in magnitude/direction
- [FACT] Gaze cueing (observers shift attention toward where a depicted face looks) is a robust basic-attention phenomenon and has been imported into ad research. "Judgments at Gaze Value: Gaze Cuing in Banner Advertisements, Its Effect on Attention Allocation and Product Judgments" (*Frontiers in Psychology*, 2017; PMC5454066) tests whether a model's gaze steers attention to the advertised product and shapes product judgments. (Source 3 — PMC body was 403-blocked; claim rests on the abstract/indexed summary, so treat the specific effect sizes as [UNKNOWN] pending fetch.)
- [FACT] **The intuitive rule "make the model look AT the product" is contested.** Sajjacholapunt & Ball, "The influence of banner advertisements on attention and memory: human faces with averted gaze can enhance advertising effectiveness" (*Frontiers in Psychology*, 2014; PMC3941030) found averted gaze (model looking toward the ad content) improved attention/memory outcomes — but the broader literature does NOT uniformly show that gaze-to-product beats gaze-to-viewer for downstream persuasion. Direct-gaze ("looking at the viewer") independently drives engagement/arousal and trust signaling. (Source 4.)
- [FACT] There IS positive evidence for the classic story: a ResearchGate-hosted eye-tracking study reports face presence + gaze directed toward the product (vs. no face / gaze toward viewer) strongly influenced attention to and memorization of the ad, ad/brand evaluation, and purchase intention. (Source 5.) And D'Ambrogio et al., "How celebrity status and gaze direction in ads drive visual attention to shape consumer decisions" (*Psychology & Marketing*, 2023) shows gaze direction interacts with celebrity status. (Source 6.)
- [ESTIMATE — medium confidence] Honest synthesis: gaze cueing moves *attention* reliably; whether "gaze-to-product" or "gaze-to-viewer" wins on *memory/purchase* depends on goal and context and the studies disagree. Do NOT hard-code "always look at the product." Treat gaze direction as an A/B variable per shot. Basis: the direct conflict between Sources 4 and 5 above.

### A1.3 Motion and contrast salience
- [FACT] Bottom-up saliency (Itti & Koch lineage) predicts fixations from low-level features — luminance/color contrast, orientation, and **motion/flicker** are strong salience channels. Cerf et al. (Source 1) is built on exactly this Itti-Koch bottom-up substrate, adding faces/text on top. Motion is one of the most potent exogenous attention captures in that framework.
- [ESTIMATE — high confidence] Reel craft: a single moving element against a still field (a car door opening, hair moving, fabric catching wind) is a reliable attention magnet; competing motion everywhere destroys the cue. High local contrast on the intended focal point does the same for a still frame. Basis: bottom-up saliency theory + its empirical fixation-prediction success.

## A2. Male vs. female response to idealized male/female imagery
_No folk-evo-psych laundering. Each claim is tied to a specific study, effect direction stated, contestation flagged. Evolutionary interpretations are labeled as the authors' framing, not as established fact._

### A2.1 Female body shape — waist-to-hip ratio (WHR)
- [FACT] Singh (1993) reported that a lower WHR (~0.7) and moderate body weight were rated more attractive for female figures across observers; WHR was proposed as a parsimonious cue. (Sources 7, 8.)
- [FACT — contested methodology] Tassinary & Hansen (1998) criticized Singh's line-drawing stimuli for confounding WHR with waist size and body weight, built 27 "unconfounded" stimuli, and got results that complicate the clean 0.7 story. Henss (1995) replicated with somewhat different results. Cross-cultural work is mixed: an overall 0.7 + lighter-weight preference appears in many samples, but e.g. one study found Ugandan raters preferred ~0.5 WHR and heavier weight. (Sources 8, 9.) 
- [ESTIMATE — medium confidence] Honest read: "there is a broad tendency toward lower WHR for female attractiveness in many populations" is defensible; "0.7 is a universal constant" is NOT — weight, culture, and stimulus artifacts move it. Basis: the replication/critique record above.

### A2.2 Male body shape — shoulder-to-hip ratio & muscularity
- [FACT] Frederick & Haselton, "Why Is Muscularity Sexy? Tests of the Fitness Indicator Hypothesis" (*Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin*, 2007), across six studies: **moderate** muscularity is rated most attractive (an inverted-U, not "more is better"); women rated more muscular men as sexier and more physically dominant but less committed as long-term mates. (Source 10.)
- [FACT — methodological caveat carried by the authors' critics] Frederick et al. used drawings / computer-generated male figures, some at supra-normal strength, which may bias the "moderate" optimum. (Source 10 commentary.) Later work (e.g., "Cues of upper body strength account for most of the variance in men's bodily attractiveness," PMC5745404) finds upper-body-strength cues explain a large share of variance in men's rated bodily attractiveness. (Source 11.)
- [FACT] Cross-cultural preference research pairs women's WHR with men's shoulder-to-hip ratio (V-taper) as complementary dimorphism cues (Plymouth PEARL cross-cultural paper). (Source 12.)
- [ESTIMATE — medium confidence] Read for the male persona: a V-taper / evident-but-not-extreme upper-body strength cue is the supported "attractive to women" target, NOT maximal bodybuilder mass. Basis: inverted-U finding + upper-body-strength variance result.

### A2.3 Status cues — the car-status experiment (asymmetric by sex)
- [FACT] Dunn & Searle, "Effect of manipulated prestige-car ownership on both sex attractiveness ratings" (*British Journal of Psychology*, 2010, 101:69-80; doi:10.1348/000712609X417319; PubMed 19302732). Same models, same expression/posture, seated in a high-status car (Silver Bentley Continental GT) vs. neutral (Red Ford Fiesta ST). Result: the MALE target was rated significantly more attractive by female raters in the high-status car; the FEMALE target's ratings by male raters did NOT differ by car. (Sources 13, 14.)
- [FACT] Replication/extension: "Luxurious cars increase perceptions of status, social dominance, and attractiveness" (*Personality and Individual Differences*, 2023) supports the status→attractiveness link. (Source 15.)
- [ESTIMATE — medium-high confidence] Read: luxury-status props (car, watch, setting) plausibly lift the MALE persona's attractiveness signal to a female audience more than they lift the FEMALE persona's to a male audience. This is exactly the "classic-luxurious" genre's leverage — but it is one experimental paradigm; treat as a working hypothesis, not law. Basis: Dunn & Searle + one replication.

### A2.4 Idealized imagery has a documented downside (must inform "taste")
- [FACT] Grabe, Ward & Hyde (2008), meta-analysis, *Psychological Bulletin*, 134:460-476: 77 studies / 141 effect sizes. Exposure to media depicting ultra-thin ideals was associated with increased body dissatisfaction, thin-ideal internalization, and disordered-eating beliefs in women; mean effect sizes small-to-moderate (d ≈ −0.28, −0.39, −0.30). (Sources 16, 17.)
- [ESTIMATE — high confidence] Implication for the house style: idealized imagery *works* on attention and aspiration AND measurably harms a slice of the audience. This is the ethical/brand reason to hold the "aspirational-not-degrading" line, independent of platform rules.

## A3. Aspiration vs. identification: parasocial & self-congruity
- [FACT] Parasocial interaction/relationship theory originates with Horton & Wohl (1956): one-sided emotional bonds audiences form with media personas. It is a primary explanatory framework in influencer research. (Source 18.)
- [FACT] Han et al. (2024), meta-analysis of social-media-influencer impact, *Psychology & Marketing* (250 effect sizes / 53 studies): Source Credibility Theory (trustworthiness, expertise) is the most robust explanatory framework; Parasocial Interaction Theory and Congruity Theory are also significant contributors to attitudinal outcomes. (Source 18.)
- [FACT] Self-congruity theory (Sirgy lineage): persuasion rises when the brand/persona's symbolic image matches the consumer's self-concept. Self-influencer congruence affects purchase intention indirectly, mediated by parasocial interaction and perceived (altruistic) motive. (Sources 18, 19.)
- [ESTIMATE — medium confidence] Two distinct levers for the two personas: (a) **aspiration** — audience wants to BECOME/possess what the persona has (drives luxury-lifestyle desire, the Dunn-&-Searle status route); (b) **identification/self-congruity** — audience sees their ideal self in the persona (drives follow/trust, the parasocial+credibility route). Motivational-quote + travel content leans identification; luxury-car/place content leans aspiration. Basis: the meta-analytic frameworks above.

# PART B — LIKENESS / CELEBRITY LAW & POLICY

> **The owner's premise, stated plainly and then corrected:** the idea is to make composite images evoking famous people while "not revealing the face, so it's safe." **This premise is legally wrong.** The protected thing is IDENTIFIABILITY, not the face. Hiding the face does not make an identifiable-person implication safe. This file gives the true boundary and a safe lane — it does NOT give guidance for maximizing deniability while still evoking a specific real person. **That lane is closed. Say so to the owner.**

## B1. Right of publicity protects IDENTIFIABILITY, not just the face

- [FACT] **Onassis v. Christian Dior-New York, Inc.** (N.Y. Sup. Ct. 1984). Dior ran an ad using a look-alike (Barbara Reynolds) of Jacqueline Kennedy Onassis. The court held a representation conveying "the essence and likeness" of a person, intended to and in fact conveying that it was the plaintiff, violates NY Civil Rights Law §§ 50/51 — **even though it was not actually her.** A look-alike triggered liability. (Source 21.)
- [FACT] **Midler v. Ford Motor Co.**, 849 F.2d 460 (9th Cir. 1988). Ford hired a sound-alike to imitate Bette Midler's voice for a commercial after she declined. Held: "The human voice is one of the most palpable ways identity is manifested… To impersonate her voice is to pirate her identity." Voice — no name, no face, no image — was enough. (Source 22.)
- [FACT] **White v. Samsung Electronics America, Inc.**, 971 F.2d 1395 (9th Cir. 1992). A robot in a blonde wig, gown, and jewelry posed by a Wheel-of-Fortune-style game board evoked Vanna White. The robot was NOT her statutory "likeness," but the court let the California **common-law** right-of-publicity claim reach the jury: the right is "not limited to the appropriation of name or likeness," and defendants "appropriated part of her identity." Identity by CONTEXT + associated objects, with no depiction of the person at all. (Source 23.)
- [FACT] Doctrinally, right-of-publicity claims split into (i) appropriation of name/likeness (literal depiction) and (ii) **evocation** that reminds the audience of a particular celebrity — reaching voice, characterization, catchphrases, and closely associated objects/context. Identifiability through ANY of these is the test. (Source 24.)

### State-law landscape
- [FACT] **California Civil Code § 3344**: liability for anyone who "knowingly uses another's name, voice, signature, photograph, or likeness… on or in products… or for purposes of advertising or selling… without prior consent." Statutory damages the greater of $750 or actual damages, plus attributable profits; punitive damages and prevailing-party attorney's fees available; injunctive relief added in recent amendments. Note the list explicitly includes **voice** and **name**, not just photograph. (Source 25.) California ALSO has a common-law right (the White basis) that reaches broader "identity" evocation.
- [FACT] **New York Civil Rights Law §§ 50 & 51**: NY's statutory privacy/publicity right — bars use of a living person's name, portrait, picture (and, post-amendment, voice) for advertising or trade without written consent; § 51 gives the civil action (injunction + damages, punitive available). (Sources 21, 26.)
- [FACT] **NY Civil Rights Law § 50-f** (signed Nov 30, 2020; effective May 29, 2021): a post-mortem right of publicity for "deceased performers" and "deceased personalities" domiciled in NY at death; defines a "digital replica" and creates liability where a digital replica in a scripted audiovisual work or live musical performance is "likely to deceive the public into thinking it was authorized." Damages: greater of $2,000 or compensatory, plus profits, plus punitive. (Companion § 52-c addresses sexually explicit deepfakes.) (Source 26.)

### 2024–2026 AI-likeness statutes (status verified)
- [FACT] **Tennessee ELVIS Act** ("Ensuring Likeness Voice and Image Security Act") — signed Mar 21, 2024; **effective July 1, 2024.** First U.S. law specifically targeting AI voice/likeness cloning; extends the right of publicity to explicitly include **voice** alongside name/photograph/likeness, and imposes liability on tools whose "primary purpose or function" is producing an unauthorized digital replica. (Source 27.)
- [FACT] **California AB 1836** — signed Sept 17, 2024; **effective Jan 1, 2025.** Amends the Civil Code to bar nonconsensual production/distribution of digital replicas of a **deceased** personality's voice or likeness without estate/heir consent. (Source 28.)
- [FACT] **California AB 2602** — signed Sept 17, 2024; **effective Jan 1, 2025.** Voids contract terms allowing a digital replica of a performer's voice/likeness (for new performances fixed on/after Jan 1, 2025) unless the use is specifically described and the performer had legal/union representation. (Source 28.)
- [ESTIMATE — medium confidence] Direction of travel is toward BROADER, not narrower, protection of AI-generated likeness — and a federal "NO FAKES"-style approach has been under active consideration. Any "faceless but identifiable" workaround is aiming at a shrinking target. Basis: the 2024-25 statute cluster above. [UNKNOWN] exact status of any federal statute as of July 2026 — not verified here.

## B2. Platform layer (Meta / Instagram)
- [FACT — indexed summaries; official pages 403-blocked] Meta's **Authentic Identity Representation / Account Integrity** standards: Meta "does not allow the creation of accounts or profiles that are created or used to deceive others" and prohibits impersonation and identity misrepresentation; it removes millions of fake/impersonating accounts. (Source 29 — `transparency.meta.com/policies/community-standards/authentic-identity-representation/` and `.../account-integrity-and-authentic-identity/`; both 403, claim from indexed summary.)
- [FACT] Meta **manipulated-media / AI-content labeling**: from May 2024 Meta expanded "AI info" labels across video/audio/image when it detects industry-standard AI indicators (C2PA provenance) or when the uploader discloses AI generation; the manipulated-media policy was broadened after Oversight Board feedback. Meta's own oversight noted enforcement is "inconsistent." (Sources 30, 31.) Practical upshot: AI-generated persona content will be, and per policy should be, **disclosed/labeled as AI**.
- [ESTIMATE — high confidence] Combined platform read: content implying a real celebrity's presence/endorsement risks the impersonation/deception standards regardless of face-hiding; and AI-generated persona content requires AI disclosure. Basis: the two policy lines above.

## B3. The honest verdict for the owner's idea
- [FACT-grounded conclusion] Hiding the face does NOT make an identifiable-person implication safe. Onassis (look-alike, face shown but not the person), Midler (voice only), and White (a robot + context, no person depicted at all) each found liability/triable claims with **no reliance on the real person's actual face.** Silhouette, hair, styling, associated objects, setting, and **name-adjacent cues** can all establish identifiability. (Sources 21-24.)
- [FACT] Separately, deceptively implying a celebrity's **association or endorsement** is an **FTC problem**. Under the FTC Endorsement Guides (16 CFR Part 255, revised 2023), an "endorsement" is any message consumers are likely to believe reflects a party other than the advertiser; a material connection must be disclosed when a "significant minority" (the FTC suggests 10% or even less) would not expect it; a paid/implied celebrity endorsement without clear disclosure is "likely to be deceptive." Implying a famous person backs the personas — without their involvement — is deceptive endorsement. (Sources 32, 33.)
- **Verdict to relay to the owner, plainly:** the "famous person, face hidden, so it's safe" plan is NOT safe. It risks (a) state right-of-publicity liability (identifiability, incl. common-law evocation), (b) the emerging AI-likeness statutes, (c) Meta impersonation/deception enforcement, and (d) FTC deceptive-endorsement exposure. Face-hiding changes none of that.

## B4. The SAFE LANE the specialists CAN deliver
- [ESTIMATE — high confidence, legally reasoned] Deliver **celebrity-CODED aesthetics with fictional/anonymous figures that point at NO specific real person.** The visual GRAMMAR of fame is not owned by anyone; a specific person's identity is. Buildable elements:
  - **Paparazzi visual grammar** — flash falloff, long-lens compression, "caught candid" framing, motion blur, crowd-edge composition. Genre, not identity.
  - **Entourage / bodyguard staging** — generic dark-suited silhouettes, earpiece, formation walking. Archetypes, not real security details.
  - **Red-carpet / backstage / step-and-repeat framing** — but with a FICTIONAL sponsor wall (invented brand/logo), never a real event's marks or a real logo.
  - **Fictional-fame worldbuilding for the two personas** — give each persona their OWN invented name, backstory, invented brand associations, invented press. The personas ARE the celebrities of their own fictional world; they never stand in for a real one.
- **Hard constraint:** no real logos, no real event names, no real associated objects tied to a specific living/dead person, no name-adjacent cues (initials, signature-look items), no look-alike casting of a specific person's silhouette/hair/style. Generic-luxe, not specific-person.

## B5. Decision checklist (the specialist must run this before any celebrity-coded output)
1. Is any REAL, specific person identifiable by ANY cue — face, silhouette, hair, voice, styling, associated object, setting, name, initials, or name-adjacent hint? → **If yes, STOP. Redesign or kill.**
2. Does the content imply a real person's presence, association, or endorsement? → If yes, STOP (right of publicity + FTC).
3. Are all logos/venues/events/brands FICTIONAL/invented? → If no, replace.
4. Is the persona's fame self-contained fictional worldbuilding (own name/backstory)? → If no, fix.
5. Is the output labeled/disclosed as AI-generated per Meta policy? → If no, add disclosure.
6. Does it clear the Part-A5 taste floor (aspirational-not-explicit, no nudity/solicitation)? → If no, restyle.
- Only if 1-2 are firmly NO and 3-6 are YES does the content ship.
