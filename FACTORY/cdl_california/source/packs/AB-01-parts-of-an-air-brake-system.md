# AB-01 · Parts of an air brake system

> **Handbook:** Section 5.1, pages 5-1–5-5 · **Test:** Air Brakes · **Exam weight:** High · **Study time:** ~45 min

## What you'll be able to answer
- The **3** braking systems inside an air brake system, and what a vehicle needs to count as "air brakes" for your CDL.
- What the compressor, governor, air tanks, drains, alcohol evaporator and safety valve do — and the key pressures (**125**, **100**, **150 psi**).
- How an S-cam drum brake works, and how wedge, disc and CamLaster brakes differ.
- What each gauge and warning tells you, including the low-air warning range (**55–75 psi**).
- What spring brakes are, when they come on by themselves (**20–45 psi**), and how the parking brake controls work.
- Which vehicles must have ABS, where the yellow lamp is, and what ABS does (and does not) do.

## Learn it

**Air brakes** work by using **compressed air** (air squeezed into a tank under pressure) to put on the brakes. They are a good, safe way to stop large, heavy vehicles — but only if they are kept in good shape and used the right way. (p. 5-1)

An air brake system is really **3 braking systems** in one (p. 5-1):
- **Service brake** — puts on and lets off the brakes when you use the **brake pedal** in normal driving.
- **Parking brake** — puts on and lets off the parking brakes when you use the **parking brake control**.
- **Emergency brake** — uses parts of the service and parking systems to **stop the vehicle if the brake system fails**.

**What counts as "air brakes" for your CDL (p. 5-1):** the vehicle must have **air gauges** and **low pressure warning device(s)**. The examiner checks these during the vehicle inspection test. If your road-test vehicle does not have them, it does not count as having air brakes, and you get a **"No Air Brakes" ("L") restriction** on your CDL.

**[CA]** A full service brake application must send **at least 90 percent** of the air pressure left in the tanks (with the brakes applied) to every brake chamber (CVC §26502). (p. 5-1)

If you want to pull a trailer with air brakes, you also need Section 6 (Combination Vehicles). (p. 5-1)

**Key words**
- **psi** = pounds per square inch, the unit your air pressure gauges use.
- **Compressed air** = air squeezed into a small space so it pushes hard; it is what works air brakes.
- **Air compressor** = the engine-driven pump that fills the air tanks.
- **Governor** = the part that tells the compressor when to start and stop pumping.
- **Cut-out pressure** = the tank pressure at which the governor stops the compressor (around 125 psi).
- **Cut-in pressure** = the tank pressure at which the governor lets the compressor start pumping again (around 100 psi).
- **Reservoir** = another word for an air storage tank.
- **Drain valve** = a valve at the bottom of an air tank that lets out collected water and oil.
- **Alcohol evaporator** = a device that puts alcohol into the air system so ice is less likely to form in cold weather.
- **Safety valve** = a relief valve on the first tank that lets air out if pressure gets too high (usually 150 psi).
- **Foot valve / treadle valve** = other names for the brake pedal.
- **Foundation brakes** = the brakes at each wheel that actually do the stopping.
- **Brake drum** = the metal drum at each end of an axle; the wheel is bolted to it, and the brake works inside it.
- **Brake shoes and linings** = the parts pressed against the inside of the drum to make friction.
- **Brake chamber** = the round can that air flows into; the air pushes out a rod to work the brake.
- **Push rod** = the rod that comes out of the brake chamber when air enters it.
- **Slack adjuster** = the arm that links the push rod to the brake camshaft; the push rod moves it, and it twists the camshaft.
- **S-cam** = an S-shaped cam that spreads the brake shoes against the drum.
- **Application pressure** = how much air pressure you are sending to the brakes right now.
- **Brake fade** = brakes losing their stopping power, usually from too much heat.
- **Wig wag** = a mechanical arm that drops into your view when air pressure gets low.
- **Spring brakes** = brakes put on by powerful springs; air pressure holds the springs back while you drive.
- **Modulating control valve** = a spring-loaded dash lever that puts the spring brakes on a little at a time.
- **ABS** = anti-lock braking system; a computer system that keeps wheels from locking up in hard braking.
- **ECU** = electronic control unit, the ABS computer.

### 5.1.1 Air compressor and governor (p. 5-1)
- The **air compressor** pumps air into the **air storage tanks** (reservoirs).
- It is driven by the engine through **gears or a v-belt**.
- It may be **air cooled** or cooled by the **engine cooling system**.
- It may have **its own oil supply** or use **engine oil**. If it has its own oil supply, **check its oil level before driving**.
- The **governor** controls when the compressor pumps:
  - Tank pressure rises to **cut-out** (around **125 psi**) → the governor **stops** the compressor.
  - Tank pressure falls to **cut-in** (around **100 psi**) → the governor lets the compressor **start** again.
- *Why:* this keeps tank pressure in a working band, never empty and never overfilled.
- *Worked example:* your gauge climbs to about 125 psi and stops rising — that is cut-out. You use the brakes and pressure slowly falls. At about 100 psi you hear the compressor kick in again — that is cut-in.

> Note — handbook conflict: p. 5-1 gives cut-out as "around 125 psi", while the leakage test on p. 5-8 says cut-out is **120–140 psi** (or the maker's level). Both are "normal"; 125 falls inside 120–140. For a question about the governor, use **125 cut-out / 100 cut-in**.

### 5.1.3 Air tanks, tank drains and the alcohol evaporator (pp. 5-1–5-2)
**Air storage tanks** hold the compressed air. How many tanks, and how big, depends on the vehicle. They hold enough air to use the brakes **several times even if the compressor stops working**. (p. 5-1)

**Tank drains (p. 5-2):**
- Compressed air usually contains some **water and compressor oil**. Both are bad for the brakes. They collect in the **bottom** of the tank.
- *Why it matters:* the **water can freeze** in cold weather and cause **brake failure**.
- Every tank has a **drain valve in the bottom**. Drain the tanks **completely**. There are **2 types**:

| Type | How it works | Your job |
|---|---|---|
| **Manual** | Open it with a **quarter turn** or by **pulling a cable** | Drain the tanks yourself **at the end of each day of driving** |
| **Automatic** | Water and oil are pushed out on their own | May also have a manual drain; some have **electric heaters** so the drain does not freeze |

**Alcohol evaporator (p. 5-2):**
- Some systems use it to put **alcohol** into the air. This lowers the chance of **ice** in brake valves and other parts in **cold weather**. Ice inside can stop the brakes from working.
- Check the alcohol container and fill it as needed — **every day in cold weather**.
- It does **not** replace draining: you still drain the tanks daily to get rid of water and oil (unless the system has **automatic** drains).

### 5.1.6 Safety valve and brake pedal (p. 5-2)
**Safety valve:**
- Installed in the **first tank** the compressor pumps air into.
- It protects the tank and the whole system from **too much pressure**.
- It is usually set to open at **150 psi**.
- **If it lets air out, something is wrong.** Have a **mechanic** fix the fault.

**Brake pedal** (also called the **foot valve** or **treadle valve**):
- Push down **harder** → **more** air pressure goes to the brakes.
- Let up → air pressure drops and the brakes release.
- Every time you release the brakes, some air leaves the system, so tank pressure goes down. The compressor must make it back up.
- **Pumping the pedal for no reason** (pressing and releasing again and again) can let air out **faster than the compressor can replace it**. If pressure gets too low, **the brakes will not work**.

### 5.1.8 Foundation brakes (pp. 5-2–5-3)
Foundation brakes are at **each wheel**. The most common type is the **S-cam drum brake**.

**Drums, shoes and linings (p. 5-2):** the drums sit on each end of the axles, and the wheels bolt to them. To stop, the **shoes and linings** press on the **inside of the drum**. That makes **friction**, which slows you — and makes **heat**. How much heat a drum can take depends on how **hard** and how **long** you brake. **Too much heat can make the brakes stop working.**

**How an S-cam brake works, step by step (p. 5-2, Figure 5.2):**
1. You push the brake pedal. Air goes into each **brake chamber**.
2. The air pushes the **push rod** out.
3. The push rod moves the **slack adjuster**.
4. The slack adjuster twists the **brake camshaft**, which turns the **S-cam** (shaped like the letter "S").
5. The S-cam spreads the **brake shoes** apart and presses them against the inside of the **drum**.
6. When you let up, the S-cam turns back and a **return spring** pulls the shoes away from the drum, so the wheels roll freely.

Figure 5.2 (described): a drum brake showing the brake chamber, slack adjuster, adjusting nut, brake cam, cam roller, shoe lining, return spring, axle and drum.

**Other brake types (p. 5-3):**

| Type | How it works | Adjustment |
|---|---|---|
| **CamLaster** | A special cam slides the shoes down a sloped ramp so they touch the drum evenly | **Built-in** system keeps it adjusted all the time (an S-cam needs an **outside slack adjuster**) |
| **Wedge** | The chamber push rod drives a **wedge** between the ends of 2 shoes, spreading them against the drum. May have 1 or 2 brake chambers | May be self-adjusting or need **manual** adjustment |
| **Disc** | Chamber and slack adjuster, like an S-cam, but they turn a **"power screw"** that clamps the disc (rotor) between the pads of a **caliper**, like a big C-clamp | — |

**Wedge and disc brakes are less common than S-cam brakes.** (p. 5-3)

### 5.1.9 Gauges, low-air warnings and the stop light switch (p. 5-3)
**Supply pressure gauges (5.1.9):**
- **Every** air-brake vehicle has a pressure gauge connected to the air tank.
- They tell you **how much pressure is in the air tanks**.
- A **dual** air system has **one gauge for each half** of the system — or **one gauge with 2 needles**.

**Application pressure gauge (5.1.10):**
- Shows how much air pressure **you are applying to the brakes**. (**Not every vehicle has one.**)
- If you need **more and more application pressure to hold the same speed**, your **brakes are fading**. **Slow down and use a lower gear.**
- A need for more pressure can also come from brakes **out of adjustment**, **air leaks** or **mechanical problems**.

**Low air pressure warning (5.1.11):**
- **Required** on every vehicle with air brakes.
- A warning **you can see** must come on when tank pressure falls **between 55 and 75 psi** (on **older** vehicles: **½ of the governor cut-out pressure**).
- It is usually a **red light**. A **buzzer** may also sound.
- **Wig wag:** drops a mechanical arm into your view when pressure falls to between 55 and 75 psi.
  - **Automatic** wig wag: rises out of view by itself once pressure is back up.
  - **Manual reset** wig wag: you must put it back "out of view" by hand, and it **will not stay up until pressure is above 55 psi**.
- **Large buses:** the warning commonly comes on at **80–85 psi**.
- *Worked example:* half of a 125 psi cut-out is 62.5 psi — inside the 55–75 range.

**Stop light switch (5.1.12):** drivers behind you must be warned when you brake. An **electric switch worked by air pressure** turns on the **brake lights** when you put on the air brakes.

### 5.1.13 Front brake limiting valve (p. 5-4)
- Some vehicles made **before 1975** have a **front brake limiting valve** with a cab control marked **"normal"** and **"slippery."**
- In **"slippery,"** the valve **cuts the front brake air pressure in half**.
- It was meant to stop front-wheel skids on slippery roads — but it actually **reduces your stopping power**.
- **Front wheel braking is good under all conditions.** Tests show front-wheel skids from braking are **not likely even on ice**.
- **Keep the control in "normal"** for normal stopping power.
- Many vehicles have **automatic** front limiting valves. They reduce front brake air **except** when you brake very hard (**60 psi or more** application pressure). **The driver cannot control these.**

### 5.1.14 Spring brakes (p. 5-4)
- **All trucks, truck tractors and buses** must have **emergency brakes and parking brakes**.
- These must be held on by **mechanical force**. *Why:* air pressure can **eventually leak away**.
- **Spring brakes** usually do this job: **powerful springs** are **held back by air pressure** while you drive. **Take the air away and the springs put the brakes on.**
- Two ways the air goes away:
  1. You use the **parking brake control**, which lets the air out of the spring brakes.
  2. A **leak** loses all the air — the springs then put the brakes on by themselves.
- Tractor and straight truck spring brakes come **fully on** when pressure drops to **20–45 psi** (**typically 20–30 psi**).
- **Don't wait** for that. When the **low-air warning light and buzzer first come on**, bring the vehicle to a **safe stop right away**, while you can still control the brakes.
- Spring brake power depends on **brake adjustment**. If the brakes are out of adjustment, **neither** the regular brakes **nor** the emergency/parking brakes will work right.

### 5.1.15 Parking brake controls (pp. 5-4–5-5)
- **Newer vehicles:** a **yellow, diamond-shaped, push-pull knob**. **Pull out = parking brakes (spring brakes) on. Push in = off.**
- **Older vehicles:** may use a **lever**.
- **Use the parking brakes whenever you park.**
- **Caution — never push the brake pedal down while the spring brakes are on.** *Why:* the springs plus the air pressure together can **damage the brakes**. Many systems are built to prevent this, but not all are, and those that are may not always work — so make it a habit.

**Modulating control valve:**
- A **spring-loaded** dash handle that puts the spring brakes on **gradually**, so you can feel the braking.
- The farther you move the lever, the harder the spring brakes come on.
- *Why:* so you can control the spring brakes **if the service brakes fail**.
- **To park:** move the lever **as far as it will go** and hold it with the **locking device**.

**Dual parking control valves (Figure 5.3):**
- When main air pressure is lost, the spring brakes come on. Some vehicles, such as **buses**, have a **separate air tank** that can release the spring brakes so you can move in an emergency.
- One valve is **push-pull**, for parking. The other is **spring-loaded in the "out" position**: **push and hold** it and air from the separate tank releases the spring brakes; let go and they come back on.
- The separate tank has enough air to do this **only a few times**. **Plan carefully**, or you could be stuck in a dangerous spot.

Figure 5.3 (described): dash controls — **yellow** "Parking brakes, pull to apply, push to release"; **red** "Trailer air supply / tractor protection, pull to apply, push to release"; **blue** "Emergency parking brake release, push and hold." Setting the parking brakes closes the tractor protection valve and sets the spring brakes at the same time.

### 5.1.16 Anti-lock braking systems (p. 5-5)
- **ABS** is a computer system that **keeps your wheels from locking up** during **hard braking**.
- **Who must have ABS:**
  - **Truck tractors** with air brakes built **on or after March 1, 1997**.
  - **Other** air-brake vehicles (trucks, buses, trailers, converter dollies) built **on or after March 1, 1998**.
  - Many older vehicles were given ABS **voluntarily**.
- **How to tell:** check the **certification label** for the date of manufacture. On older towed units, look **under the vehicle** for the **ECU** and **wheel speed sensor wires** coming from the back of the brakes.
- **Yellow ABS malfunction lamps:**
  - Tractors, trucks and buses: on the **instrument panel**.
  - Trailers: on the **left side**, **front or rear corner**.
  - Dollies made on or after **March 1, 1998**: a lamp on the **left side**.
- **Bulb check:** on newer vehicles the lamp lights at **start-up**, then goes out quickly. On older systems it may stay on until you are going **over 5 mph**.
- If the lamp **stays on** after the bulb check, or **comes on while driving**, you may have **lost ABS control at one or more wheels**.
- ABS is an **addition** to your normal brakes. It does **not increase or decrease** your normal braking power. It works **only when wheels are about to lock**.
- ABS does **not necessarily shorten your stopping distance**, but it helps you **keep control** in hard braking.

## Exam traps
- **Trap:** Air brakes are one braking system worked by the pedal. → **Correct:** They are **3** systems — **service, parking and emergency**. (p. 5-1)
- **Trap:** The governor stops the compressor at 150 psi. → **Correct:** Cut-out is **around 125 psi**. **150 psi** is where the **safety valve** opens. (pp. 5-1–5-2)
- **Trap:** The compressor starts pumping again when pressure falls to 55 psi. → **Correct:** Cut-in is **around 100 psi**. 55 psi has to do with the low-air warning. (pp. 5-1, 5-3)
- **Trap:** If the safety valve lets out air, the system is working as designed. → **Correct:** **Something is wrong** — have a **mechanic** fix it. (p. 5-2)
- **Trap:** You only need to drain the air tanks in cold weather. → **Correct:** Drain manual tanks **at the end of each day** of driving; water and oil build up in all seasons, and frozen water can cause brake failure. (p. 5-2)
- **Trap:** An alcohol evaporator means you no longer need to drain the tanks. → **Correct:** You **still drain daily** to remove water and oil, unless the tanks have automatic drains. (p. 5-2)
- **Trap:** Fanning the brake pedal keeps the brakes ready. → **Correct:** Pumping the pedal needlessly lets air out **faster than the compressor can replace it**; if pressure gets too low, the brakes will not work. (p. 5-2)
- **Trap:** The supply pressure gauge shows how hard you are braking. → **Correct:** The **supply** gauge shows tank pressure; the **application** gauge shows the pressure going to the brakes. (p. 5-3)
- **Trap:** Put the front brake limiting valve in "slippery" on icy roads. → **Correct:** Keep it in **"normal."** Front wheel braking is good in **all** conditions, and front skids are not likely **even on ice**. (p. 5-4)
- **Trap:** Wait for the spring brakes to stop you when air pressure is low. → **Correct:** **Stop safely right away** when the low-air warning first comes on, while you can still control the brakes. (p. 5-4)
- **Trap:** Spring brakes work fine even if the brakes are out of adjustment. → **Correct:** If brakes are out of adjustment, **neither** the regular brakes **nor** the parking/emergency brakes work right. (p. 5-4)
- **Trap:** Push the yellow knob in to set the parking brakes. → **Correct:** **Pull** it out to set them; **push** in to release. (p. 5-4)
- **Trap:** Pressing the brake pedal while the spring brakes are on gives extra holding power. → **Correct:** **Never** do it — the springs plus air pressure together can **damage the brakes**. (p. 5-4)
- **Trap:** ABS gives you more braking power and always shorter stops. → **Correct:** ABS does **not** add or remove braking power and does **not necessarily** shorten stops; it helps you **keep control**. (p. 5-5)
- **Trap:** An ABS lamp that lights at start-up means the ABS is broken. → **Correct:** A quick light at start-up is the **bulb check**. It is a problem if it **stays on** afterward or comes on while driving. (p. 5-5)

## Handbook review questions — answered
**TYK 5.1 #1** Why do you need to drain the air tanks?
→ Compressed air carries some water and compressor oil, which collect in the bottom of the tanks and harm the brake system. The water can freeze in cold weather and make the brakes fail. (p. 5-2)

**TYK 5.1 #2** What is a supply pressure gauge for?
→ It tells you how much air pressure is in the air tanks. Every air-brake vehicle has one; a dual system has a gauge for each half (or one gauge with 2 needles). (p. 5-3)

**TYK 5.1 #3** True or false: every vehicle with air brakes must have a low air pressure warning signal.
→ True. It must come on (a signal you can see, usually a red light) when tank pressure falls between 55 and 75 psi. (p. 5-3)

**TYK 5.1 #4** What are spring brakes?
→ Brakes put on by powerful springs. Air pressure holds the springs back while you drive; when the air is let out (with the parking brake control, or by a leak), the springs put the brakes on. They serve as the parking and emergency brakes and come fully on at 20–45 psi (typically 20–30 psi). (p. 5-4)

**TYK 5.1 #5** True or false: front wheel brakes are good under all conditions.
→ True. Tests show front wheel skids from braking are not likely even on ice, so keep any front brake limiting control in "normal." (p. 5-4)

**TYK 5.1 #6** How can you tell whether your vehicle has anti-lock brakes?
→ Check the certification label for the date of manufacture (tractors built on or after March 1, 1997, and other air-brake vehicles on or after March 1, 1998, must have ABS), and look for the yellow ABS malfunction lamp. On older towed units, look under the vehicle for the ECU and wheel speed sensor wires. (p. 5-5)

   b) Service and parking brakes only
   c) Foot, hand and trailer brakes

   a) 100 psi
   b) 150 psi
   c) 125 psi

   a) 55 psi
   b) 100 psi
   c) 75 psi

   a) To lower the pressure so the compressor keeps running
   b) To let the safety valve reset
   c) Water and oil collect in them, and the water can freeze and cause brake failure

   a) At the end of each day of driving
   b) Once a week
   c) Only when the weather is below freezing

   a) You no longer need to drain the air tanks
   b) You should check and fill the alcohol container every day in cold weather
   c) It keeps the compressor cool in hot weather

   a) 150 psi
   b) 125 psi

## One-minute recap
- Air brakes = **3 systems**: service, parking, emergency. To count for your CDL, the vehicle needs **air gauges + low-air warning**, or you get the **"L"** restriction.
- **Compressor** fills the tanks; **governor** stops it at **~125 psi** (cut-out) and restarts it at **~100 psi** (cut-in).
- **Drain manual tanks every day** — water and oil collect, and frozen water causes brake failure. The alcohol evaporator helps with ice but does **not** replace draining.
- **Safety valve** in the first tank opens at **~150 psi**; if it pops, get a mechanic.
- Don't pump the pedal needlessly — you can use air faster than the compressor makes it.
- **S-cam**: air → brake chamber → push rod → **slack adjuster** → camshaft → S-cam spreads shoes against the drum.
- **Low-air warning: 55–75 psi** (buses often 80–85). **Spring brakes come on at 20–45 psi** — stop as soon as the warning comes on. Keep front limiting valves in **"normal."**
- **Yellow diamond knob: pull = park.** Never press the pedal with spring brakes on. **ABS** required on tractors from **March 1, 1997**, others from **March 1, 1998**; yellow lamp; ABS helps **control**, not braking power.
