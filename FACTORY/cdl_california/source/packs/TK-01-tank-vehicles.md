# TK-01 · Tank vehicles

> **Handbook:** Section 8.1–8.3, pages 8-1–8-3 · **Test:** Tank Vehicles · **Exam weight:** High · **Study time:** ~35 min

## What you'll be able to answer
- Exactly when you need an **"N" (tank) endorsement** — the gallon numbers — and what a learner (CLP) may do.
- What to check when you inspect a tank vehicle, and why leaks are the most important item.
- Why tankers roll over so easily: **high center of gravity** and **liquid surge**.
- The difference between **bulkheads**, **baffles**, and **smooth bore** (unbaffled) tanks.
- What **outage** is, and the things that decide how much liquid you may load.
- How to drive a tanker: smooth starts and stops, braking to control surge, curves, stopping distance, and skids.

## Learn it

A **tank vehicle** (tanker) carries liquid or gas in a tank. Liquid moves and sits high, so a tanker handles very differently from a truck with boxes on it. You should also study Sections 2, 5, 6, and 9 (Driving Safely, Air Brakes, Combination Vehicles, Hazardous Materials). **Inspect the tanker before loading, unloading, or driving it** — to make sure it is safe to carry the load and safe to drive. (p. 8-1)

**Key words**
- **N endorsement** = the tank vehicle endorsement on a CDL.
- **Rated capacity** = how many gallons a tank is built to hold. **Individual** = one tank. **Aggregate** = all the tanks on the vehicle added together.
- **HazMat** = hazardous materials (dangerous goods such as fuel or acid).
- **CLP** = commercial learner permit (the permit you drive on while learning, with a CDL holder).
- **Purge** = clean out a tank completely so nothing of the old load is left.
- **Center of gravity** = the balance point of all the weight. The higher it is, the easier the vehicle tips over.
- **Surge** = the liquid sloshing back and forth (or side to side) in a tank that is not full.
- **Bulkhead** = a solid wall that divides one big tank into smaller separate tanks.
- **Baffle** = a wall inside a tank **with holes** in it, so liquid can flow through but more slowly.
- **Smooth bore** tank = an **unbaffled** tank — nothing inside to slow the liquid.
- **Outage** = empty space you leave at the top of a tank so the liquid has room to expand.
- **Manhole cover** = the lid on top of a tank that you open to get inside or load.
- **Vent** = an opening that lets air in and out of the tank.
- **Stab braking / controlled braking** = emergency stopping methods from Section 2 (see GK-12).

### 8.0 Who needs the "N" endorsement (p. 8-1)
You need an **"N" endorsement** when **all** of these are true:
- your vehicle needs a **Class A, B, or C** CDL, **and**
- you haul a **liquid or liquid gas**, **and**
- it is in a tank or tanks with an **individual** rated capacity of **more than 119 gallons** **and** an **aggregate** (total) rated capacity of **1,000 gallons or more**, **and**
- the tank is attached to the vehicle or chassis **permanently or temporarily**.

The liquid or gas **does not have to be HazMat**. Milk or water counts too. (p. 8-1)

*Worked example:* a truck (needs a CDL) carries **4 tanks of 300 gallons** each, bolted to the bed. Each tank is more than 119 gallons, and 4 × 300 = **1,200 gallons**, which is 1,000 or more → **N endorsement needed**. With **3 tanks of 300** (900 gallons total), the total is under 1,000 → no N endorsement for this rule.

**[CA] Special cases (p. 8-1):**
- **Not a tank vehicle:** a CMV hauling an **empty storage tank** that is **not designed for transportation**, with a rated capacity of at least **1,000 gallons**, **temporarily attached to a flatbed trailer** (CVC §15210(r)).
- **CLP holder with an N endorsement:** may drive a tank vehicle **only if it is empty**. If it last held **HazMat**, it must also be **purged** (FMCSR §383.25).
- **Class C CDL with only an N endorsement** (new applicant): a **skills test is required**, in a representative Class C tank vehicle that meets the gallon rule above.
- **Adding N to an existing Class C CDL:** pass the knowledge tests; **no skills test** needed.

### 8.1.1 Inspection — leaks come first (p. 8-1)
- Tankers come in many types and sizes. **Check the operator's manual** to learn how to inspect yours.
- On **all** tank vehicles, the **most important thing to check for is leaks**. Look **under and around** the vehicle.
- **Never carry liquid or gas in a leaking tank.** It is a **crime**: you will be **cited** and **stopped from driving further**, and you may have to **pay for the cleanup** of any spill.
- Check:
  - the tank **body (shell)** for **dents or leaks**;
  - the **intake, discharge, and cut-off valves** — they must be in the **correct position** before loading, unloading, or moving;
  - **pipes, connections, and hoses** for leaks, **especially around joints**;
  - **manhole covers and vents** — covers must have **gaskets** and **close correctly**; keep vents **clear** so they work.

### 8.1.2 Special purpose and emergency equipment (p. 8-1)
If your tanker has any of these, make sure it **works**:
- **Vapor recovery kits**
- **Grounding and bonding cables**
- **Emergency shutoff systems**
- **Built-in fire extinguisher**

**Never drive a tank vehicle with open valves or open manhole covers.**

**Emergency equipment (8.1.3):** find out what emergency equipment your vehicle is **required** to carry, and make sure you **have it and it works**.

### 8.2.1 High center of gravity (p. 8-2)
- Hauling liquid takes special skill for **2 reasons**: the **high center of gravity** and the **movement of the liquid (surge)**.
- High center of gravity = much of the load's weight rides **high above the road**. The vehicle is **top-heavy** and **easy to roll over**. Liquid tankers are **especially** easy to roll over.
- A tanker's center of gravity is **much higher** than a car's or pickup truck's (Figure 8.1 shows a tanker at about 60–78 inches and a pickup at about 18–24 inches).
- **Tests show tankers can turn over at the speed limits posted for curves.** So take highway curves and on/off-ramp curves **well below** the posted speed.

### 8.2.2 Danger of surge (p. 8-2)
- **Surge** comes from liquid moving in a **partially filled** tank. It hurts handling.
- *Example:* when you stop, the liquid surges back and forth. When the wave hits the end of the tank, it **pushes the truck in the direction the wave is moving**.
- On a **slippery** road (like ice), the wave can **shove a stopped truck out into an intersection**.
- A tanker driver must know the vehicle's handling **very well**.

### 8.2.3 Bulkheads, baffles, and smooth bore tanks (p. 8-2)
| Tank type | What's inside | Surge | What to watch |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Bulkheads** | Solid walls that **divide** the tank into several **smaller tanks** | Each small tank is separate | When loading and unloading, watch **weight distribution** — don't put **too much weight on the front or rear** |
| **Baffled** | Bulkheads **with holes** that let liquid flow through | Controls **forward-and-back** surge; **side-to-side surge can still happen** and cause a rollover | Side-to-side surge in turns and lane changes |
| **Unbaffled ("smooth bore")** | **Nothing** inside to slow the liquid | **Forward-and-back surge is very strong** | Be **extremely cautious** (slow and careful), **especially when starting and stopping** |

- Smooth bore tanks usually haul **food products** (for example, **milk**). *Why no baffles?* **Sanitation rules forbid baffles** because they make the inside of the tank **hard to clean**. (p. 8-2)

### 8.2.6 Outage and how much to load (p. 8-2)
- **Never load a cargo tank totally full.** Liquids **expand as they warm**, so you must leave room. That room is called **outage**.
- Different liquids expand by **different amounts**, so they need **different amounts of outage**. You must **know the outage requirement** when you haul liquid in bulk.
- **How much to load (8.2.7):** a full tank of a **dense (heavy) liquid**, such as some **acids**, may be **over the legal weight limit**. So heavy liquids are often loaded only **part way**.
- The amount to load depends on **4 things**:
  1. how much the liquid will **expand** in transit,
  2. the **weight** of the liquid,
  3. **legal weight limits**,
  4. the **temperature** of the load.

> Note — handbook conflict: the review question on p. 8-3 asks for "3 things" that decide how much you can load, but 8.2.7 lists **4** (expansion, weight, legal weight limits, temperature). Learn all 4; on a "3 things" question, **expansion, weight, and legal weight limits** are the safest three (temperature is what causes the expansion).

### 8.2.8 [CA] Speeding and hours of service in a tank vehicle (p. 8-3)
- **Speeding (CVC §22406.5):** if you drive a tanker carrying **more than 500 gallons of flammable liquid** above the speed limit, or with **willful or wanton disregard** for people's or property's safety:
  - **1st offense:** a **fine** (on top of any other penalty);
  - **2nd or later offense within 2 years:** a **fine** and a **suspension of up to 6 months** of your **HazMat** and/or **"N"** endorsement.
- **Hours of service (CVC §34501.2(b)(1)):** a driver of a tank vehicle with a capacity **greater than 500 gallons** hauling **flammable liquid** may drive **at most 10 hours** in a work period.

### 8.3 Safe driving rules for tankers (p. 8-3)
- **Drive smoothly (8.3.1):** because of the high center of gravity and surge, **start, slow down, and stop very smoothly**. Make **smooth turns and lane changes**.
- **Control surge (8.3.2):**
  - Keep a **steady pressure** on the brakes. **Don't release the brakes too soon** when coming to a stop. *Why:* letting off early lets the wave push the truck forward.
  - **Brake far ahead** of a stop, and **increase your following distance**.
  - If you must stop **quickly** to avoid a crash, use **controlled or stab braking** (Section 2). Remember: **steering quickly while braking can roll the tanker over**.
- **Curves (8.3.3):** **slow down before** the curve, then **accelerate slightly through** it. The **posted speed for a curve may be too fast** for a tanker.
- **Stopping distance (8.3.4):** know how much room you need. **Wet roads double** the normal stopping distance. **Empty tankers may take longer to stop than full ones.**
- **Skids (8.3.5):** don't **over-steer, over-accelerate, or over-brake** — any of these can cause a skid. On tank **trailers**, if the **drive or trailer wheels** skid, the rig may **jackknife**. When any vehicle starts to skid, act to **get traction back** to the wheels.

## Exam traps
- **Trap:** You need an N endorsement only if the liquid is hazardous. → **Correct:** The liquid or gas **does not have to be HazMat** — any liquid in tanks over the gallon limits counts. (p. 8-1)
- **Trap:** The N endorsement rule is 119 gallons total. → **Correct:** **More than 119 gallons** per **individual** tank **and** **1,000 gallons or more** **aggregate**. (p. 8-1)
- **Trap:** A tank that is only temporarily attached doesn't count. → **Correct:** Tanks attached **permanently or temporarily** both count (the one exception is an empty storage tank not designed for transport on a flatbed). (p. 8-1)
- **Trap:** A CLP holder with an N endorsement may drive a loaded tanker if a CDL holder rides along. → **Correct:** **[CA]** A CLP holder may drive a tank vehicle **only if it is empty** (and purged if it held HazMat). (p. 8-1)
- **Trap:** The posted speed on a curve is safe for a tanker. → **Correct:** Tankers **can roll over at the posted curve speed**. Take curves and ramps **well below** it. (pp. 8-2, 8-3)
- **Trap:** Baffles stop all surge. → **Correct:** Baffles control **forward-and-back** surge; **side-to-side** surge can still happen and roll the tanker over. (p. 8-2)
- **Trap:** Bulkheads and baffles are the same thing. → **Correct:** **Bulkheads** divide the tank into separate **smaller tanks**; **baffles** are bulkheads **with holes** that let liquid flow through. (p. 8-2)
- **Trap:** Smooth bore tanks are safer because the liquid flows evenly. → **Correct:** Smooth bore (unbaffled) tanks have the **strongest forward-and-back surge**; be extremely careful when starting and stopping. (p. 8-2)
- **Trap:** Food tankers have baffles for safety. → **Correct:** Sanitation rules **forbid baffles** in food tanks (like milk), because baffles are hard to clean. (p. 8-2)
- **Trap:** Fill the tank to the top so the liquid cannot surge. → **Correct:** **Never load totally full** — leave **outage** for the liquid to expand as it warms. (p. 8-2)
- **Trap:** A full tank is always legal as long as it doesn't leak. → **Correct:** A full tank of **dense liquid** (like some acids) may be **over legal weight limits**, so you may only partly fill it. (p. 8-2)
- **Trap:** Ease off the brakes just before you stop, for a smooth stop. → **Correct:** Keep **steady pressure** and **do not release the brakes too soon** — surge can push the truck forward. (p. 8-3)
- **Trap:** In an emergency stop, brake hard and steer around the hazard quickly. → **Correct:** Use **controlled or stab braking**; **steering quickly while braking can roll the tanker over**. (p. 8-3)
- **Trap:** An empty tanker stops faster than a full one. → **Correct:** An **empty** tank vehicle **may take longer** to stop than a full one. (p. 8-3)
- **Trap:** Brake through a curve to keep speed down. → **Correct:** **Slow down before** the curve, then **accelerate slightly** through it. (p. 8-3)

## Handbook review questions — answered
**TYK 8 #1** How are bulkheads different from baffles?
→ Bulkheads are solid walls that divide a tank into several smaller tanks (so you must watch weight distribution when loading and unloading). Baffles are bulkheads with holes in them that let the liquid flow through; they slow forward-and-back surge, but side-to-side surge can still happen. (p. 8-2)

**TYK 8 #2** Should a tank vehicle take curves, on-ramps, or off-ramps at the posted speed limits?
→ No. Tests show tankers can turn over at the posted curve speed. Take highway curves and ramp curves well below the posted speed: slow down before the curve, then accelerate slightly through it. (pp. 8-2, 8-3)

**TYK 8 #3** How are smooth bore tankers different to drive than those with baffles?
→ Smooth bore (unbaffled) tanks have nothing inside to slow the liquid, so forward-and-back surge is very strong. Be extremely cautious — slow and careful — especially when starting and stopping. (p. 8-2)

**TYK 8 #4** What 3 things determine how much liquid you can load?
→ How much the liquid will expand in transit, the weight of the liquid, and legal weight limits. (The handbook's list on p. 8-2 also adds the temperature of the load.) (p. 8-2)

**TYK 8 #5** What is outage?
→ The empty space you must leave in a tank because liquids expand as they warm. Never load a cargo tank totally full; different liquids need different amounts of outage, and you must know the requirement for your load. (p. 8-2)

**TYK 8 #6** How can you help control surge?
→ Keep steady pressure on the brakes and don't release them too soon when stopping; brake far ahead of the stop and increase following distance; in a quick stop, use controlled or stab braking and don't steer quickly while braking. (p. 8-3)

**TYK 8 #7** What 2 reasons make special care necessary when driving tank vehicles?
→ The high center of gravity (top-heavy, easy to roll over) and the movement of the liquid (surge). (p. 8-2)

## One-minute recap
- **N endorsement:** CDL vehicle + any **liquid or liquid gas** (not just HazMat) + a tank **over 119 gal** each and **1,000 gal or more** total, attached **permanently or temporarily**.
- **[CA]** CLP with N → **empty** tanks only (purged if HazMat). New Class C + N only → skills test; adding N to a Class C → knowledge only.
- Inspect **before loading, unloading, or driving**. **Leaks** are #1 — hauling in a leaking tank is a **crime**. Check shell, valves, pipes (joints), manhole covers (gaskets), vents. Never drive with **open valves or manhole covers**.
- Two dangers: **high center of gravity** (top-heavy; can roll **at the posted curve speed**) and **surge**.
- **Bulkheads** = separate tanks (watch weight distribution). **Baffles** = walls with holes; stop forward-back surge, **not side-to-side**. **Smooth bore** = no baffles (food/milk), strongest surge — careful starting and stopping.
- **Outage** = room for expansion; never fill totally full. Load depends on **expansion, weight, legal limits, temperature**.
- Drive **smoothly**; **steady brake pressure**, don't release too soon; brake early, more following distance; emergency = **controlled/stab braking**, no quick steering.
- Slow **before** curves, accelerate slightly through. Wet roads **double** stopping distance; **empty tankers may take longer** to stop. **[CA]** Flammable >**500 gal**: max **10 hours** driving; repeat speeding within **2 years** → up to **6 months** suspension.
