# HM-03 · Loading, unloading and bulk packages

> **Handbook:** Section 9.4–9.5, pages 9-11–9-15 · **Test:** Hazardous Materials · **Exam weight:** High · **Study time:** ~45 min

## What you'll be able to answer
- The general rules for loading any HazMat: parking brake, heat, leaks, bracing, and never opening or transferring packages on the trip.
- Around which hazard classes you must never smoke, which classes have cargo heater rules, and which classes cannot ride as overhang or tailgate loads.
- The special loading precautions for explosives, flammable solids, oxidizers, corrosives, compressed gases, poisons and radioactive materials.
- Which products you must never load in the same vehicle (the Do Not Load table), including what goes with battery acid and cyanide.
- What cargo tanks and portable tanks are, and how bulk packages must be marked (ID number size, letter size, which sides).
- Who must watch a cargo tank being loaded, and the engine, grounding and hose rules for flammable liquids and compressed gas.

## Learn it

This lesson is about the moment HazMat goes onto or comes off your vehicle. HazMat can hurt people when a package is damaged, leaks, catches fire, or is loaded next to something it reacts with. Protect the containers: **don't use tools that could damage them**, and **don't use hooks**. (p. 9-11)

**Key words**
- **HazMat** = hazardous materials: products that can hurt people, property or the environment while they are being moved.
- **Hazard class** = the category a HazMat belongs to (for example Class 3, flammable liquids). Each material is assigned only one class.
- **Division** = a smaller group inside a hazard class, written with a decimal point (Division 1.1 is one kind of Class 1 explosive).
- **Placard** = a large diamond-shaped sign on the outside of the vehicle that warns of the HazMat inside.
- **Cargo heater** = a heater (or heater/air-conditioner unit) that warms the cargo space.
- **Overhang or tailgate load** = cargo that sticks out past the end of the bed or rides on an open tailgate.
- **Nonferrous metal** = any metal that has no iron or iron alloys in it.
- **Carboy** = a large bottle (often in a crate) used to carry corrosive liquids such as acids.
- **Cylinder** = a strong metal bottle that holds compressed gas.
- **Relief valve** = a safety valve that lets gas out if the pressure inside gets too high.
- **Transport index** = a number the shipper prints on some radioactive labels. It tells how much control the package needs in transit.
- **Bulk packaging** = one container with a capacity of more than 119 gallons for a liquid (for example a cargo tank or a dump truck body).
- **Cargo tank** = bulk packaging that is permanently attached to a vehicle and stays on it while it is loaded and unloaded.
- **Portable tank** = bulk packaging that is not permanently attached; it is filled or emptied off the vehicle, then put on a vehicle to be moved.
- **Intermediate bulk container (IBC)** = a rigid or flexible portable package, built to be handled by machines, that counts as bulk packaging.
- **Freight container** = a reusable container of 64 cubic feet or more that can be lifted with its contents inside.
- **Outage (ullage)** = the empty space left at the top of a tank so the liquid has room to expand when it warms up.
- **Consignee** = the business or person the shipment is delivered to.

> Note: the handbook says you will not be tested on the glossary itself (p. 9-24). The key words above are here so the rules make sense.

### 9.4.1 General loading requirements (p. 9-11)
- **Before** loading or unloading, **set the parking brake** and make sure the vehicle **cannot move**.
- **Keep HazMat away from heat sources.** *Why:* many products get **more dangerous when they are heated**.
- **Watch for leaking or damaged containers.** Leaks spell trouble. **Do not transport leaking packages** — it is **illegal to move a vehicle with leaking HazMat**. *Why:* depending on the product, you, your truck and other people could all be in danger.
- **Brace** every HazMat container so it cannot move during the trip.

**No smoking.** Keep fire away while loading or unloading, and don't let anyone smoke nearby. **Never smoke** around these **5** classes:
- **Class 1** (Explosives)
- **Division 2.1** (Flammable Gas)
- **Class 3** (Flammable Liquids)
- **Class 4** (Flammable Solids)
- **Class 5** (Oxidizers)

**Secure against movement.** Brace containers so they **cannot fall, slide or bounce**. Take extra care with containers that have **valves or other fittings**. All HazMat packages must be secured for the trip.

**Hands off during the trip.**
- Once loaded, keep every package **closed** for the whole trip.
- **Never move HazMat from one package into another** on the road.
- A **cargo tank** may be emptied on the vehicle; **no other package** may be.

### 9.4.1 Cargo heaters and closed cargo space (p. 9-12)
**Cargo heater rules** apply to **3** classes:
- **Class 1** (Explosives)
- **Division 2.1** (Flammable Gas)
- **Class 3** (Flammable Liquids)

The rules **usually forbid cargo heaters**, and that includes **automatic cargo heater/air-conditioner units**. Unless you have read all the related rules, **don't load these products in a cargo space that has a heater**. *Why:* heat plus fuel-like cargo can start a fire or explosion.

**Use a closed cargo space.** **No overhang or tailgate loads** of these **3** classes:
- **Class 1** (Explosives)
- **Class 4** (Flammable Solids)
- **Class 5** (Oxidizers)

They must go in a **closed cargo space**, unless **every** package is **fire- and water-resistant** or covered with a **fire- and water-resistant tarp**.

Memory hook: heaters = **1, 2.1, 3** (things that burn from heat); closed space = **1, 4, 5** (things that react to fire and water).

### 9.4.1 Class 1 (Explosives) precautions (p. 9-12)
- **Turn the engine off** before loading or unloading **any** explosives. Then check the cargo space. You must:
  - **Disable cargo heaters**: disconnect their power and **drain heater fuel tanks**.
  - Make sure there are **no sharp points** — bolts, screws, nails, broken side panels or broken floorboards — that could damage cargo.
  - For **Division 1.1, 1.2 or 1.3**, use a **floor lining**. The floor must be tight, and the liner must be **nonmetallic** or a **nonferrous metal** (no iron).
- **Never use hooks or other metal tools.** **Never drop, throw or roll** explosive packages. Protect them from other cargo that could damage them.
- **Don't transfer** Division 1.1, 1.2 or 1.3 explosives from one vehicle to another **on a public road**, except in an **emergency**. For an emergency transfer, set out **red warning reflectors, flags or electric lanterns** to warn other drivers.
- **Never transport damaged packages** of explosives. **Don't accept** a package with **any dampness or oily stain**.

**Division 1.1 or 1.2 in a combination vehicle.** Don't haul them in a combination if:
- the combination has a **marked or placarded cargo tank**, or
- the other vehicle in the combination carries:
  - **Division 1.1 A** (Initiating Explosives),
  - Class 7 packages labeled **"Yellow III"**,
  - **Division 2.3** (Poisonous Gas) or **Division 6.1** (Poisonous) materials, or
  - HazMat in a portable tank on a **DOT Spec 106A or 110A** tank.

### 9.4.1 Classes 4, 5 and 8 precautions (pp. 9-12–9-13)
**Class 4 (Flammable Solids) and Class 5 (Oxidizers)** (p. 9-12)
- Class 4 solids can **react — even catch fire or explode —** with **water, heat or air**, or **all by themselves**.
- Class 4 and 5 materials must be **completely enclosed** in the vehicle or **covered securely**.
- Those that become dangerous **when wet** must be **kept dry** during the trip and during loading and unloading.
- Materials that can **heat up or catch fire on their own** (spontaneous combustion) need a vehicle with **enough ventilation**.

**Class 8 (Corrosives)** (pp. 9-12–9-13)
- Loading by hand: load **breakable containers of corrosive liquid one by one**, **right side up**. Don't drop or roll them. Load them onto an **even floor**.
- **Stack carboys** only if the **lower tiers can safely hold the weight** of the upper tiers.
- **Nitric acid** must **never be loaded above any other product**.
- **Charged storage batteries**: load so the liquid can't spill, **right side up**, and make sure other cargo **can't fall against or short-circuit** them.
- **Never load corrosive liquids next to or above:** Division **1.4** (Explosives C), Division **4.1** (Flammable Solids), Division **4.3** (Dangerous When Wet), **Class 5** (Oxidizers), Division **2.3 Zone B** (Poisonous Gases).
- **Never load corrosive liquids with:** Division **1.1 or 1.2**, Division **1.2 or 1.3**, Division **1.5** (Blasting Agents), Division **2.3 Zone A** (Poisonous Gases), Division **4.2** (Spontaneously Combustible), Division **6.1, PGI, Zone A** (Poison Liquids).

### 9.4.1 Classes 2, 6 and 7 precautions (pp. 9-13, 9-22)
**Class 2 (Compressed gases, including cryogenic liquids)** (p. 9-13)
- If the vehicle has **no racks** for cylinders, the cargo floor must be **flat**.
- Cylinders must be **held upright**, in **racks attached to the vehicle** or in **boxes** that keep them from turning over.
- A cylinder may lie **horizontal (lying down)** only if it is **designed so the relief valve is in the vapor space**.

**Division 2.3 (Poisonous Gas) and Division 6.1 (Poisonous)** (p. 9-13)
- **Never** carry them in containers with **interconnections**.
- **Never** load a package labeled **POISON** or **POISON INHALATION HAZARD** in the **driver's cab or sleeper**, or with **food for people or animals**.
- Loading and unloading Class 2 materials in **cargo tanks** has special rules, and you need **special training** to do it.

**Class 7 (Radioactive)** (pp. 9-13, 9-22)
- Some packages show a **transport index**. The shipper labels them **Radioactive II or III** and prints the index on the label.
- Radiation passes through **all nearby packages**. So the rules control **how many** packages you load together and **how close** they are to **people, animals and unexposed film**.
- The **total transport index** of all packages in one vehicle **must not exceed 50**.
- Example from the handbook: a package with a transport index of **1.1** can't be left **within 2 feet** of people or cargo-space walls.

The handbook's radioactive separation table (Table A, Figure 9.10) gives minimum distances in feet. Do not leave Yellow II or Yellow III packages near people, animals or film longer than it allows. (p. 9-22)

### 9.4.1 Mixed loads: the Do Not Load table (p. 9-14)
Some products **cannot ride in the same cargo space**. Figure 9.9 (the **Do Not Load table**) gives examples; the full federal **Segregation Table** lists more.

| Do not load… | …in the same vehicle with |
|---|---|
| **Division 6.1 or 2.3** (POISON or poison inhalation hazard labeled) | **Animal or human food**, unless the poison package is overpacked in an approved way. Food means anything you swallow — but **mouthwash, toothpaste and skin creams are not food**. |
| **Division 2.3 gas Zone A** or **Division 6.1 liquids, PGI, Zone A** | Division 1.1, 1.2, 1.3 explosives; 5.1 oxidizers; Class 3 flammable liquids; Class 8 corrosive liquids; 5.2 organic peroxides; 1.5 blasting agents; 2.1 flammable gases; Class 4 flammable solids |
| **Charged storage batteries** | **Division 1.1** |
| **Class 1 detonating primers** | **Any other explosives**, unless in authorized containers or packages |
| **Division 6.1 cyanides or cyanide mixtures** (e.g. silver cyanide, sodium cyanide) | **Acids, corrosive materials** or other acidic materials that could release **hydrocyanic acid** |
| **Nitric acid (Class 8)** | Other materials, **unless the nitric acid is not loaded above** any other material |

*Worked example:* you have **silver cyanide** on board and the shipper hands you **battery acid**. Battery acid is a corrosive (an acid). Cyanides must not be loaded with acids, so **you cannot load them in the same vehicle**. (pp. 9-14, 9-23)

### 9.5 Cargo tanks and portable tanks (p. 9-15)
- A **cargo tank** is bulk packaging **permanently attached** to a vehicle. It **stays on the vehicle** while you load and unload it.
- A **portable tank** is bulk packaging that is **not permanently attached**. The product is loaded or unloaded **while the tank is off the vehicle**; then the tank is put on a vehicle to be moved.
- The **most common** cargo tanks: **MC306 for liquids** and **MC331 for gases**.

### 9.5.1 Markings (p. 9-15)
- You must show the **ID number** of the HazMat on **portable tanks, cargo tanks and other bulk packaging** (such as **dump trucks**). Look up the ID number in **Column 4** of the Hazardous Materials Table.
- The numbers are **black**, **100 mm (3.9 inches)** tall, on **orange panels**, on **placards**, or on a **white diamond-shaped background** if no placards are required.
- **Specification cargo tanks** must show **retest date** markings.
- **Portable tanks** must show:
  - the **lessee's or owner's name**;
  - the **shipping name** of the contents on **two opposite sides**, in letters at least **2 inches** tall if the tank holds **more than 1,000 gallons**, or **1 inch** tall if it holds **less than 1,000 gallons**;
  - the **ID number** on **each side and each end** if it holds **1,000 gallons or more**, or on **2 opposite sides** if it holds **less than 1,000 gallons**.
- The ID numbers must stay **visible when the portable tank is on the vehicle**. If they can't be seen, display the ID number on **both sides and both ends of the vehicle**.
- **IBCs** are bulk packaging, but they **don't need the owner's name or shipping name**.

*Worked example:* a 1,200-gallon portable tank needs shipping-name letters at least 2 inches tall, and the ID number on both sides and both ends. An 800-gallon portable tank needs 1-inch letters and the ID number on 2 opposite sides.

### 9.5.2 Tank loading (p. 9-15)
The person in charge of loading or unloading a cargo tank must make sure a **qualified person is always watching**. That person must:
- be **alert**;
- have a **clear view** of the cargo tank;
- be **within 25 feet** of the tank;
- **know the hazards** of the materials;
- **know the emergency procedures**;
- be **authorized to move** the cargo tank **and able to do so**.

There are **special attendance rules** for cargo tanks carrying **propane** and **anhydrous ammonia**.

**Close all manholes and valves** before moving a tank of HazMat — **no matter how small the amount or how short the distance**. *Why:* open manholes and valves leak. Moving a cargo tank with any valve or cover open is **illegal** unless the tank is **empty** as defined in 49 CFR §173.29.

### 9.5.3–9.5.4 Flammable liquids and compressed gas (p. 9-15)
**Flammable liquids (9.5.3)**
- **Turn off your engine** before loading or unloading. Run it **only if needed to run a pump**.
- **Ground** the cargo tank correctly before filling it through an **open filling hole**: ground it **before opening** the filling hole and keep the ground on **until after closing** it. *Why:* grounding drains static electricity that could spark and ignite the vapor.

**Compressed gas (9.5.4)**
- Keep **liquid discharge valves closed** except when loading and unloading.
- Turn the engine **off** when loading or unloading, **unless it runs a pump** for the product transfer.
- If you use the engine for the pump, turn it off **after product transfer** and **before you unhook the hose**.
- **Unhook all loading/unloading connections** before **coupling, uncoupling or moving** a cargo tank.
- **Always chock** trailers and semi-trailers when they are uncoupled from the power unit, so they can't roll.

## Exam traps
- **Trap:** You can move a vehicle with a slow leak to a better spot to deal with it. → **Correct:** do **not** transport leaking packages; it is **illegal to move a vehicle with leaking HazMat**. (p. 9-11)
- **Trap:** You may move HazMat into a better package during the trip if one is damaged. → **Correct:** **never transfer** HazMat between packages in transit, and don't open packages; only a **cargo tank** may be emptied. (p. 9-11)
- **Trap:** The no-smoking classes and the cargo-heater classes are the same list. → **Correct:** never smoke around **1, 2.1, 3, 4 and 5**; cargo heater rules cover only **1, 2.1 and 3**. (pp. 9-11–9-12)
- **Trap:** Oxidizers can ride as a tailgate load if tied down. → **Correct:** **Class 1, 4 and 5** need a **closed cargo space** unless all packages are fire- and water-resistant or covered by such a tarp. (p. 9-12)
- **Trap:** A stainless steel floor liner is fine for Division 1.1 or 1.2 explosives. → **Correct:** the liner must be **nonmetallic or nonferrous**; stainless steel contains **iron**. (p. 9-12)
- **Trap:** You may transfer explosives between vehicles on a public road if you are careful. → **Correct:** only in an **emergency**, and then you set out red reflectors, flags or electric lanterns. (p. 9-12)
- **Trap:** A slightly damp explosives package is OK to accept. → **Correct:** **don't accept** any explosives package with **dampness or an oily stain**. (p. 9-12)
- **Trap:** Nitric acid should go on the top tier so nothing crushes it. → **Correct:** **never load nitric acid above** any other product. (p. 9-12)
- **Trap:** Compressed gas cylinders may always be laid flat to keep them from tipping. → **Correct:** keep them **upright** in racks or boxes; lay one down only if it is **designed with the relief valve in the vapor space**. (p. 9-13)
- **Trap:** A small POISON package can ride in the sleeper berth. → **Correct:** **never** in the **cab or sleeper**, and never with food. (p. 9-13)
- **Trap:** Toothpaste counts as food, so it can't ride with poison. → **Correct:** **mouthwash, toothpaste and skin creams are not foodstuffs**. (p. 9-14)
- **Trap:** You may load as many radioactive packages together as fit in the cargo space. → **Correct:** the **total transport index** in one vehicle **must not exceed 50**. (p. 9-13)
- **Trap:** A portable tank stays on the vehicle while it is filled. → **Correct:** that is a **cargo tank**; a portable tank is filled or emptied **off** the vehicle. (p. 9-15)
- **Trap:** The person watching a cargo tank being loaded can be anywhere on the property. → **Correct:** within **25 ft**, alert, with a **clear view**, and able and authorized to move the tank. (p. 9-15)
- **Trap:** You can leave a valve open for a short move across the yard. → **Correct:** close **all manholes and valves** before moving, **no matter how small the amount or how short the distance**. (p. 9-15)
- **Trap:** With a pump-driven compressed gas delivery, unhook the hose first, then shut off the engine. → **Correct:** turn the engine off **after product transfer** and **before unhooking** the hose. (p. 9-15)

## Handbook review questions — answered
**TYK 9.4 #1** Around which hazard classes must you never smoke?
→ **Class 1** (Explosives), **Division 2.1** (Flammable Gas), **Class 3** (Flammable Liquids), **Class 4** (Flammable Solids) and **Class 5** (Oxidizers). (p. 9-11)

**TYK 9.4 #2** Which 3 hazard classes should not be loaded into a trailer that has a heater/air-conditioner unit?
→ **Class 1** (Explosives), **Division 2.1** (Flammable Gas) and **Class 3** (Flammable Liquids). The cargo heater rules usually forbid heaters, including automatic heater/AC units. (p. 9-12)

**TYK 9.4 #3** Should the floor liner required for Division 1.1 or 1.2 materials be stainless steel?
→ **No.** The liner must be **nonmetallic** or a **nonferrous** metal (one with no iron). Stainless steel contains iron. (p. 9-12)

**TYK 9.4 #4** At the shipper's dock you are handed a paper for 100 cartons of battery acid. You already have 100 pounds of dry silver cyanide on board. What precautions must you take?
→ **Don't load the battery acid in the same vehicle.** Silver cyanide is a Division 6.1 cyanide, and the Do Not Load table says cyanides must not ride with acids or corrosives, which could release hydrocyanic acid. (p. 9-14)

**TYK 9.4 #5** Name a hazard class that uses transport indexes to decide how much can be loaded in one vehicle.
→ **Class 7 (Radioactive).** The total transport index in one vehicle must not exceed 50. (p. 9-13)

**TYK 9.5 #1** What are cargo tanks?
→ Bulk packaging that is **permanently attached to a vehicle** and stays on the vehicle while it is loaded and unloaded. (p. 9-15)

**TYK 9.5 #2** How is a portable tank different from a cargo tank?
→ A portable tank is **not permanently attached** to a vehicle. It is loaded or unloaded **while off the vehicle**, then put on a vehicle to be moved. A cargo tank stays on the vehicle. (p. 9-15)

**TYK 9.5 #3** Your engine runs a pump used to deliver compressed gas. Do you turn the engine off before or after unhooking the hoses?
→ **Before.** Turn the engine off **after the product transfer** is done and **before you unhook** the hose. (p. 9-15)

## One-minute recap
- Before loading: **parking brake on**, keep HazMat **away from heat**, **no hooks**, brace everything. **Never move leaking HazMat.**
- Never smoke around **1, 2.1, 3, 4, 5**. Cargo heaters: **1, 2.1, 3**. Closed cargo space (no tailgate loads): **1, 4, 5**.
- Explosives: **engine off**, heaters disabled, **nonmetallic or nonferrous** liner, no damaged, **damp or oily** packages, emergency transfers only.
- Corrosives: one by one, **right side up**; **nitric acid never on top**; batteries upright and protected.
- Cylinders **upright** (flat only if the relief valve is in the vapor space). **POISON never in the cab or sleeper or with food.**
- Radioactive: total transport index **50 max**; distance from people grows with the index.
- Do Not Load: **cyanides + acids**, **batteries + Division 1.1**, poison + food (toothpaste is not food).
- Tanks: cargo tank **stays on**, portable tank comes **off**. ID numbers **black, 100 mm**. Watcher **within 25 ft**. Close valves before **any** move. Ground **before opening, until after closing**. Engine off **before unhooking** the hose.
