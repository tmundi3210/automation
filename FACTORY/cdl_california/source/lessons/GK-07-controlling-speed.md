# GK-07 · Controlling speed and stopping distance

> **Handbook:** Section 2.6–2.6.8, pages 2-15–2-18 · **Test:** General Knowledge · **Exam weight:** High · **Study time:** ~45 min

## What you'll be able to answer
- Name the three parts of total stopping distance and give each one's value at 55 mph (142 + 61 + 216 = 419 ft).
- Say how much braking distance and impact grow when you double, triple or quadruple your speed (4×, 9×, 16×).
- Explain why an empty truck needs MORE room to stop than a loaded one.
- Pick a safe speed for wet roads, packed snow, ice, curves, fog or low beams, heavy traffic, downgrades and work zones.
- Recognize slippery surfaces (black ice, bridges, shade, first rain) and say what to do when hydroplaning.
- **[CA]** Recall the brake-performance distances from 20 mph, the rule for passing slow vehicles on a grade, the 300-foot following rule and the 100-foot caravan gap.

## Learn it

### 2.6 Why speed matters (p. 2-15)
The handbook calls driving too fast a major cause of fatal crashes. Your speed must fit five things:
- **Traction** — how well your tires grip the road.
- **Curves.**
- **Visibility** — how far ahead you can see.
- **Traffic.**
- **Hills.**

### 2.6.1 Stopping distance (pp. 2-15 – 2-16)

**The formula to memorize:**

> **Perception distance + Reaction distance + Braking distance = Total stopping distance**

A **hazard** is anything on or near the road that could become dangerous (a car braking, a person, debris).

| Part | What happens during it | Average time | Distance at **55 mph** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Perception distance** | Your eyes see the hazard → your brain understands "this is a hazard" | **1¾ seconds** (alert driver) | **142 ft** |
| **Reaction distance** | Your brain has understood → your foot actually presses the brake | **¾ second to 1 second** | **61 ft** |
| **Braking distance** | The brakes are working → the vehicle stops | — | about **216 ft** (dry road, good brakes) |
| **Total stopping distance** | All three added together | — | **at least 419 ft** |

**Why it matters:** during perception and reaction you are still rolling at full speed. The truck has not slowed at all yet.

Important details:
- All these numbers are for **ideal conditions** — they are minimums.
- Perception can take much longer. Poor visibility, a hazard that is hard to see, or your mental and physical condition (tired, sick, distracted) can stretch it a lot.

**Worked example 1 — add it up at 55 mph**
- Perception 142 ft + reaction 61 ft = **203 ft** traveled before the brakes even begin to work. That is almost half the total.
- 203 ft + braking 216 ft = **419 ft** total.

**Worked example 2 — the same math at other speeds (data from Figure 2.11, p. 2-16)**

| Speed | Perception | Reaction | Braking | Total | What to notice |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 15 mph | 39 ft | 16 ft | 17 ft | **72 ft** | At low speed braking is tiny — about the same as reaction |
| 25 mph | 65 ft | 28 ft | 47 ft | **140 ft** | 65 + 28 + 47 = 140 |
| 35 mph | 91 ft | 39 ft | 92 ft | **222 ft** | Braking now about equals perception |
| 45 mph | 117 ft | 50 ft | 152 ft | **319 ft** | Braking is now the biggest part |
| 55 mph | 142 ft | 61 ft | 216 ft | **419 ft** | Braking alone is longer than perception + reaction together |

Look at the columns from 25 mph to 55 mph: perception and reaction a little more than double (65 → 142, 28 → 61). Braking grows more than four times (47 → 216). That is why speed is so dangerous.

The handbook uses an American football field to picture these distances: at **60 mph**, your stopping distance is **longer than a football field**.

> **Beyond the handbook — not on the test:** at 55 mph you cover roughly 80 feet every second (142 ft ÷ 1¾ s). Each second you are slow to notice a hazard costs you about that much road.

> **Note from Air Brakes (p. 5-12):** you already learned that air brakes add a fourth part, *brake lag* (about 32 ft at 55 mph), so an air-brake vehicle needs over 450 ft. A General Knowledge question about the three parts at 55 mph expects **419 ft**.

**Effect of speed (p. 2-16).** Speed increases both the **impact** (how hard you hit) and the **braking distance** — much faster than the speed itself:

| Speed change | Speed is… | Impact and braking distance are… |
|---|---|---|
| 20 → 40 mph | 2 times faster | **4 times** greater |
| 20 → 60 mph | 3 times faster | **9 times** greater |
| 20 → 80 mph | 4 times faster | **16 times** greater |

**Memory trick:** multiply the speed factor by itself. 2 × 2 = 4, 3 × 3 = 9, 4 × 4 = 16.
**Why:** the energy of a moving vehicle grows with the square of its speed, and the brakes must absorb all of it as heat.
The fix is simple: **slowing down is how you shorten braking distance.**

**Effect of vehicle weight (p. 2-16).**
- A heavier vehicle makes the brakes work harder, and they absorb more heat.
- BUT the brakes, tires, springs and shock absorbers of a heavy vehicle are designed to work best when it is **fully loaded**.
- **Empty trucks need LONGER stopping distances**, because an empty vehicle has **less traction**.
- **Why:** with little weight pressing the tires onto the road, they grip less, so the wheels cannot use full braking force.

**[CA] Control and stopping requirements — CVC §26454 (p. 2-16).** (CVC = California Vehicle Code.) **GVWR** (gross vehicle weight rating) = the maximum loaded weight the manufacturer says the vehicle is built for.
- The **service brake** (your regular foot brake) must hold the vehicle or combination **still on any grade** it drives on, whether **loaded or empty**.
- The service brakes must stop the vehicle from **20 mph** within a **maximum stopping distance (MSD)**:

| Vehicle type | MSD from 20 mph |
|---|---|
| Passenger vehicle (car) | **25 ft** |
| Single motor vehicle, GVWR **under 10,000 lb** | **30 ft** |
| Single motor vehicle, GVWR **10,000 lb or more**, or **any bus** | **40 ft** |
| Car, or any motor vehicle with GVWR under 10,000 lb, **towing** a trailer, semitrailer or trailer coach | **40 ft** |
| **All other combinations** (for example, a tractor-semitrailer) | **50 ft** |

**Worked examples:**
- A tractor pulling a semitrailer → "all other combinations" → **50 ft**.
- A large straight truck (GVWR 10,000 lb or more, no trailer) → **40 ft**.
- A school bus or city bus → "any bus" → **40 ft**.
- A pickup with a GVWR under 10,000 lb towing a travel trailer → **40 ft**.
- Pattern: bigger and heavier vehicles are allowed more feet.

### 2.6.2 Matching speed to the road surface (pp. 2-16 – 2-17)
**Traction** = the friction between your tires and the road. Without traction you **cannot steer or brake**.

**Slippery surfaces** make stopping take longer and make skids in turns more likely:

| Road surface | What the handbook says to do |
|---|---|
| Wet | Wet roads can **double** stopping distance. Cut speed by about **one-third** — for example **55 → about 35 mph**. |
| Packed snow | Cut speed by **half or more**. |
| Ice | Slow to a **crawl**. **Stop driving** at the first place where it is safe to do so. |

**Why:** on a slippery road, only a lower speed lets you stop in the distance you would need on dry pavement.

**Worked examples:**
- Dry-road speed 45 mph, road now wet: one-third of 45 is 15 → about **30 mph**.
- Dry-road speed 50 mph, packed snow: half of 50 is 25 → **25 mph or slower**.

**Identifying slippery surfaces (p. 2-17).** Warning signs:
- **Shaded areas** — shady parts stay icy long after sunny parts have melted.
- **Bridges** — they freeze **before** the road does. Take extra care when the temperature is near **32°F**. (Why: cold air flows both above and below a bridge.)
- **Melting ice** — a little melting makes ice wet, and **wet ice is much more slippery** than dry ice.
- **Black ice** — a thin layer of ice so clear that you can see the road through it. The road just **looks wet**. Whenever it is **below freezing and the road looks wet**, suspect black ice.
- **Vehicle icing** — easy check: open your window and touch the **front of the mirror, the mirror support, or the antenna**. If there is ice on them, the road is probably starting to ice too.
- **Just after rain begins** — the first rain mixes with oil left on the road by vehicles, making it very slippery. If the rain keeps falling, it washes the oil away.

**Hydroplaning (p. 2-17).**
- **What it is:** water or slush on the road lifts your tires off the pavement, like water skiing. The tires have **little or no traction**, so you may be **unable to steer or brake**.
- **What to do:** **release the accelerator** and **push in the clutch**. This slows the vehicle and lets the wheels turn freely. **Do not use the brakes** to lose speed. If the drive wheels begin skidding, press the clutch in.
- **Speed:** it can happen at speeds **as low as 30 mph** when there is a lot of water. It does not take much water.
- **More likely when:** tire pressure is **low** or tread is **worn**. (Why: tire grooves carry water away; shallow grooves cannot.)
- **Signs of standing water:** clear **reflections** on the road, **tire splashes**, and **raindrops** visible on the road surface.

### 2.6.3 Speed and curves (p. 2-17)
Too fast in a curve, and one of **two** things happens:
1. The tires **lose traction** and keep going straight → you **skid off the road**.
2. The tires **keep traction** → the vehicle **rolls over**.

Rules:
- **Slow down BEFORE you enter the curve.** Why: braking inside a curve can easily lock the wheels and start a skid.
- **Never exceed the posted speed** for the curve.
- Be in a gear that lets you **accelerate slightly** through the curve. Why: gentle power helps you keep control.

### 2.6.4 Speed and distance ahead (p. 2-17)
**Rule:** your stopping distance must always fit **inside the stretch of road you can see ahead**.
- Fog, rain or other conditions can shorten how far you see → slow down.
- At night, **low beams** show you less road than high beams → **slow down when using low beams**.

**Worked example:** Figure 2.11 shows that even at 35 mph you need about **222 ft** to stop in ideal conditions. If fog lets you see only about 200 ft ahead, 35 mph is already too fast. Slow down more.

### 2.6.5 Speed and traffic flow (p. 2-17)
- In **heavy traffic**, your safest speed is **the same speed as the vehicles around you** — as long as it is legal and safe. Why: vehicles going the same way at the same speed rarely hit each other.
- **[CA]** Speed limits for trucks and buses are **lower** than for cars — the difference can be **as much as 15 mph**. Take extra care when changing lanes or passing, because cars are coming up behind you faster. (California's 55 mph limit for certain vehicles is covered in GK-03, p. 1-25.)
- Keep a safe following distance (see GK-08).
- **Speeding to save time does not work.** Drivers mostly speed to save time, but they save very little. Going faster than traffic means passing again and again. That raises crash risk and makes you more tired — and **fatigue** raises crash risk too. Going with the flow is safer and easier.

### 2.6.6 Speed on downgrades (p. 2-18)
A **downgrade** is a road going downhill. **Gravity** makes your speed increase.

Your **most important goal** is to choose, and keep, a speed that is not too fast for:
- the **total weight** of vehicle and cargo,
- the **length** of the grade,
- the **steepness** of the grade,
- **road conditions**,
- **weather**.

Rules:
- Never go faster than a posted speed limit or a **"Maximum Safe Speed"** sign. Watch for signs that give the grade's length and steepness.
- Use **engine braking** as your **main** way to control speed. (Engine braking = the slowing force of the engine when you are in gear with your foot off the accelerator.)
- Engine braking is **strongest** when the engine is near its **governed rpm** (the highest engine speed the governor allows) and the transmission is in a **lower gear**.
- **Save your service brakes** so they are ready when road or traffic makes you slow or stop.
- **Shift to a low gear BEFORE you start down the grade.** Why: you want full engine braking from the top of the hill.
- Full mountain-driving technique is in Section 2.16 (module GK-11).

### 2.6.7 Roadway work zones (p. 2-18)
- The **number one cause** of work-zone injuries and deaths is **speeding traffic**.
- Obey the posted speed limit when approaching and driving through a work zone.
- Watch your speedometer — do not let your speed **creep up** in long construction areas.
- Slow down for bad weather or road conditions.
- Slow down **even more** when a worker is close to the road.

### 2.6.8 Overtaking or following another vehicle — [CA] (p. 2-18)

**Passing on a grade (CVC §21758) [CA].** Outside a business or residential district (built-up town or city areas), you may **not** pass a vehicle going **less than 20 mph** on a grade unless **both** are true:
1. you pass it going **at least 10 mph faster** than it, **and**
2. you can **finish the pass within ¼ mile**.

*Worked example:* a truck is climbing a grade at 12 mph. You may pass only if you can do at least 22 mph and complete the pass within a quarter mile.

**300-foot following rule (CVC §21704) [CA].** Keep at least **300 feet** behind these vehicles:
- a truck or truck tractor with **3 or more axles**,
- a truck or truck tractor **towing** any vehicle,
- a passenger vehicle or bus **towing** any vehicle,
- a **school bus** carrying any pupil,
- a **farm labor vehicle** carrying passengers,
- a vehicle carrying **explosives**,
- a **trailer bus**.

The 300-foot rule does **NOT** apply:
- while you are **overtaking and passing**,
- where there are **2 or more lanes in each direction**,
- in a **business or residential district**.

> **Beyond the handbook — not on the test:** the law itself (CVC §21704) is aimed at drivers of these listed vehicles following one another. On the test, answer with the handbook's wording above.

**Caravans (CVC §21705) [CA].** When large vehicles travel together in a line (a **caravan**) on the open highway, leave at least **100 feet** between them so other vehicles can pass.

*Do not mix them up:* **300 ft** = following one of the listed vehicles · **100 ft** = gap between vehicles in a caravan.

## Exam traps
- **Trap:** "An empty truck stops in a shorter distance because it is lighter." → **Correct:** empty trucks need a **longer** stopping distance. Lighter *feels* easier to stop, but an empty truck has less traction. (p. 2-16)
- **Trap:** "About 300 ft" for total stopping distance at 55 mph. → **Correct:** **419 ft**. 300 ft sounds like "a football field" and the 300-ft following rule. (p. 2-16)
- **Trap:** 216 ft as the total at 55 mph. → **Correct:** 216 ft is only the **braking** part; the total is **419 ft**. (p. 2-16)
- **Trap:** "Double the speed = double the braking distance." → **Correct:** **4 times**. Speed and distance feel like they should grow together, but braking distance grows with speed × speed. (p. 2-16)
- **Trap:** Perception time = ¾ second. → **Correct:** ¾ to 1 second is **reaction** time; perception is **1¾ seconds**. (p. 2-15)
- **Trap:** Brake hard when hydroplaning. → **Correct:** release the accelerator and push in the clutch; **do not brake**. Braking is instinct, but the tires have no grip. (p. 2-17)
- **Trap:** "Hydroplaning only happens at highway speeds." → **Correct:** it can happen **as low as 30 mph** with a lot of water. (p. 2-17)
- **Trap:** "Black ice is easy to see." → **Correct:** it is **clear** and makes the road look **wet**. (p. 2-17)
- **Trap:** "A road is most slippery after hours of rain." → **Correct:** it is very slippery **just after rain begins** (water + oil); steady rain washes the oil away. (p. 2-17)
- **Trap:** Slowing for a curve while in the curve. → **Correct:** slow down **before** entering; braking in the curve can lock wheels. (p. 2-17)
- **Trap:** Packed snow → reduce speed by one-third. → **Correct:** one-third is for **wet** roads; packed snow needs **half or more**. (p. 2-16)
- **Trap:** "Use the service brakes as the main way to control speed downhill." → **Correct:** **engine braking** in a low gear is the main way; save the brakes. (p. 2-18)
- **Trap:** "Driving faster than traffic saves a lot of time." → **Correct:** it saves little and raises crash risk and fatigue; match the flow of traffic. (p. 2-17)
- **Trap [CA]:** 40 ft as the MSD for a tractor-semitrailer. → **Correct:** **50 ft** ("all other combinations"). 40 ft is for single heavy vehicles, buses and light vehicles towing. (p. 2-16)
- **Trap [CA]:** Mixing the 300-ft following rule with the 100-ft caravan gap. → **Correct:** 300 ft behind listed vehicles; 100 ft between caravan vehicles. (p. 2-18)

## Handbook review questions — answered
**TYK 2.4–2.6 #6** Which three distances add together to make total stopping distance?
→ Perception distance + reaction distance + braking distance. At 55 mph that is 142 + 61 + 216 = at least 419 ft under ideal conditions. (pp. 2-15 – 2-16)

**TYK 2.4–2.6 #7** If your speed doubles, does your stopping distance grow 2 times or 4 times?
→ **4 times.** The handbook explains that going from 20 to 40 mph makes the braking distance and the impact 4 times greater (tripling to 60 mph makes them 9 times greater). (p. 2-16)

**TYK 2.4–2.6 #8** True or false: empty trucks brake best.
→ **False.** Empty trucks need longer stopping distances because they have less traction. Heavy-vehicle brakes, tires, springs and shock absorbers work best when the vehicle is fully loaded. (p. 2-16)

**TYK 2.4–2.6 #9** What is hydroplaning?
→ When water or slush on the road makes the tires lose contact with the pavement (like water skiing), leaving little or no traction, so you may not be able to steer or brake. It can happen as low as 30 mph with a lot of water and is more likely with low tire pressure or worn tread. To regain control, release the accelerator and push in the clutch; do not brake. (p. 2-17)

**TYK 2.4–2.6 #10** What is black ice?
→ A thin layer of ice clear enough that you can see the road through it, so the road only looks wet. Watch for it whenever it is below freezing and the road looks wet. (p. 2-17)

## One-minute recap
- Total stopping distance = perception (1¾ s, 142 ft) + reaction (¾–1 s, 61 ft) + braking (216 ft) = **419 ft at 55 mph**.
- Double speed → **4×** braking distance and impact; triple → **9×**; 20 → 80 mph → **16×**. Slow down to stop shorter.
- **Empty trucks stop LONGER** — less traction.
- Wet: slow **⅓** (55 → 35). Packed snow: **half or more**. Ice: crawl, then stop driving.
- Black ice looks like a wet road; bridges freeze first; first rain is slickest. Hydroplaning (from **30 mph**): off the gas, clutch in, **no brakes**.
- Slow **before** curves; always be able to stop within your sight distance; match traffic speed.
- Downgrades: **low gear before the top**, **engine braking** first, save the brakes. Work zones: speeding is the **#1 killer**.
- **[CA]** MSD from 20 mph: 25 / 30 / 40 / 40 / **50 ft** (combinations). Grade passing: vehicle under 20 mph → **10 mph faster, within ¼ mile**. Follow listed vehicles at **300 ft**; caravans **100 ft**.
