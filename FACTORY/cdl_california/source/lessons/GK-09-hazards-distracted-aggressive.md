# GK-09 · Seeing hazards, distracted and aggressive drivers

> **Handbook:** Section 2.8–2.10, pages 2-21–2-28 · **Test:** General Knowledge · **Exam weight:** Medium · **Study time:** ~35 min

## What you'll be able to answer
- What a **hazard** is, how it differs from an **emergency** and a **conflict**, and why you plan as soon as you see one.
- What the **move-over law** asks you to do **[CA]**, and how to handle work zones, drop-offs, objects in the road and freeway ramps.
- Which clues show that another driver, pedestrian or vehicle may do something dangerous.
- The hand-held phone and texting rules: definitions, the **60-day / 120-day** disqualifications, the emergency exception, and the research numbers.
- How to avoid distraction, and how to spot and handle a distracted driver.
- The difference between **aggressive driving** and **road rage**, and what to do when an aggressive driver confronts you.

## Learn it

### 2.8.1 Importance of seeing hazards (p. 2-21)
- **Hazard** = any road condition, or any other road user (driver, motorcyclist, bicyclist, pedestrian), that *could* become a danger.
- **Emergency** = the danger is happening *now*; you must act at once.
- **Example:** a car ahead heads for a freeway exit, then brakes hard. The driver may be unsure and swing back onto the freeway — a **hazard**. If it actually cuts in front of you, it is an **emergency**.
- **Why see hazards early:** it gives you time to check mirrors, signal, slow down or change lanes calmly. A driver who notices only at the last second must brake hard or swerve, and sudden moves are more likely to cause a crash.
- Hazards often come with **clues**. You get better at reading them with experience.

### 2.8.2 Hazardous roads (p. 2-21 – 2-22)
**Move-over laws**
- **Why:** police, ambulance crews, firefighters and road workers are being hit at the roadside more and more often.
- **Rule:** when you approach an **authorized emergency vehicle stopped at the roadside**, or a **work zone**: slow down **and** move to a lane **not next to it**, if safety and traffic allow. If a lane change is not safe: slow down and pass carefully at a speed safe for traffic.
- **[CA]** Breaking the move-over law is an **infraction** (minor traffic offense) punished by a **fine** — **CVC §21809** (California Vehicle Code). (p. 2-22)

**Other road hazards — slow down and be careful (p. 2-22)**

| Hazard | Why it is dangerous | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| **Work zones** | Narrow lanes, sharp turns, rough surface; distracted drivers; workers and equipment in your path | Drive slowly; warn drivers behind with **4-way flashers or brake lights** |
| **Drop-off** (pavement edge drops sharply) | Truck tilts toward the roadside — the **top** can hit signs or tree branches; steering is hard when crossing the drop | Keep away from the edge |
| **Foreign objects** (things fallen on the road) | Damage tires, rims, electrical and brake lines; can wedge **between dual tires** (two tires side by side). A box or sack may hold something heavy | See them early and avoid them **without sudden moves** |
| **Off/on ramps** | Posted ramp speed may be fine for cars but **too fast for large or heavily loaded vehicles**. **Downhill + curve** is especially dangerous: hard to slow, and braking while turning is risky | Be slow enough **before** the curved part |

### 2.8.3 Drivers who are hazards (p. 2-22 – 2-24)
Learn the clues that someone may do something dangerous.

**They can't see or hear you (p. 2-22)**

| Clue | What to expect |
|---|---|
| **Blocked vision:** vans, loaded station wagons, cars with a blocked rear window; **rental trucks** (drivers not used to limited side/rear view); **frosted, icy or snowy windows** in winter | They may move into you without seeing you |
| **Partly hidden vehicle** at a blind intersection or alley | If you can see part of the vehicle but **not the driver**, assume the driver can't see you. Be ready to stop |
| **Delivery trucks** (step vans, postal, local delivery) | Packages or doors block the driver's view; rushed drivers may jump out or pull into your lane |
| **Parked vehicles** | Doors open, people step out, car pulls out. Clues: movement inside, **brake lights, backup lights, exhaust** |
| **Stopped bus** | Passengers may walk around the front or back of it, and often can't see you |
| **Pedestrians and bicyclists** | Back to traffic (can't see you), headphones (can't hear you); in rain, hats and umbrellas block their view and they hurry |
| **Distracted people** | Watch where they look. Even if they look at you, they may think they have the right of way |

**Their attention is elsewhere (p. 2-23):** **children** (move quickly without checking, especially when playing) · **talkers** (people chatting with each other) · **workers** (distract drivers and may not see you) · **ice cream trucks** (children nearby) · **disabled vehicles** (people fixing them ignore traffic; clues: **jacked-up wheels, raised hoods**) · **accidents** (people don't look, passing drivers stare, people run across, cars stop suddenly) · **shoppers** (looking at stores; run across parking lots; cars stop suddenly).

**They may act unpredictably (p. 2-23)**
- **Confused drivers** turn or stop without warning — common near **freeway interchanges and major intersections**. Clues:
  - tourists: **car-top luggage, out-of-state plates**;
  - odd actions: stopping mid-block, changing lanes for no reason, backup lights suddenly on;
  - hesitation: very slow driving, frequent braking, stopping in the middle of an intersection;
  - looking at street signs, maps or house numbers.
- **Slow drivers** can't keep normal speed. Some vehicles are slow by nature — **mopeds, farm and construction machinery, tractors**. Some carry the **slow-moving vehicle (SMV) emblem: a red triangle with an orange center**. **[CA]** Such vehicles are **not designed to exceed 25 mph** (**CVC §385.5**).
- **Drivers signaling a turn** may brake harder than you expect, or stop: tight turns into alleys/driveways are slow, pedestrians may block them, left-turners wait for oncoming traffic.
- **Drivers in a hurry** may pass without enough room in oncoming traffic and cut in close, or pull out in front of you to avoid being stuck behind your truck — forcing you to brake.
- **Impaired drivers** (sleepy, drunk, on drugs, or ill). Clues:
  - weaving or drifting side to side;
  - leaving the road (right wheels dropping onto the shoulder, bumping over a curb while turning);
  - stopping when they shouldn't (at a **green light**, or sitting too long at a stop);
  - **window open in cold weather**;
  - sudden speed changes; too fast or too slow.
  - Watch for drunk and sleepy drivers especially **late at night**.
- **Body movement:** people look toward where they will turn, so head and body movement can warn of a turn with no signal. An **over-the-shoulder check** may mean a lane change. Easiest to see in **motorcyclists and bicyclists**.

**Conflicts (p. 2-24)**
- **Conflict** = to avoid hitting someone, you have to change your **speed, your direction, or both**.
- Where: intersections, merges (on-ramps), forced lane changes (lane ends), slow or stalled traffic, crash scenes.
- **Why watch other drivers' conflicts:** the move they make to escape may create a conflict with **you**.

### 2.8.4 Always have a plan (p. 2-24)
You look for hazards because they can turn into emergencies. When you see one: picture the emergency it could cause, decide what you would do, and be ready to do it. That is **defensive driving**.

### 2.9 Distracted driving (p. 2-24)
**Driver distraction** = anything that pulls your attention off the job of driving. It puts you, passengers, other drivers and pedestrians at risk of injury, death or property damage.
- **Inside the vehicle:** talking with passengers; adjusting the radio, CD player or heat/AC; eating, drinking or smoking; reading maps; picking up dropped items; cell phone or **CB** (citizens band) radio; texting; **telematics** and electronic devices (built-in systems like navigation, plus pagers, PDAs, computers); daydreaming.
- **Outside the vehicle:** traffic and pedestrians; a police stop, crash scene or construction; sunlight/sunset; objects in the road; billboards.

### 2.9.1 The crash problem (p. 2-24)
- **LTCCS** (Large Truck Crash Causation Study): **8%** of large-truck crashes — driver distracted by something **outside**; **2%** — by something **inside**.
- **NHTSA** (National Highway Traffic Safety Administration): distracted-driving crashes kill about **5,500** and injure about **448,000** people a year in the U.S.
- A phone conversation — **even hands-free** — takes **39%** of the brain energy you normally use for driving.
- Hand-held device users are more likely to have injury crashes.

### 2.9.2 Effects (p. 2-25)
1. **Slowed perception** — you notice an important event late, or never.
2. **Delayed decision making** — you are slow to choose the right action.
3. **Improper action** — wrong steering, gas or brake input.

### 2.9.3 Types of distractions (p. 2-25)

| Type | What it does | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Physical** | Takes your hands from the wheel or your eyes from the road | Reaching for an object |
| **Mental** | Mind off the road | Talking with a passenger; thinking about your day |
| **Both** | **Greatest** crash risk | Phone calls; sending or reading texts |

### 2.9.4 Cell/mobile phones (p. 2-25)
- **Federal rule (49 CFR):** CMV (commercial motor vehicle) drivers may not use **hand-held** phones while driving. **Motor carriers (employers)** may not require or allow it, and can also be fined.
- **"Hand-held use" means any of:**
  1. holding the phone with **at least one hand** for a voice call;
  2. dialing by pressing **more than a single button**;
  3. moving out of your **seat-belted**, seated position to reach the phone.
- **Allowed:** a **hands-free** phone placed close to you so you can use it while buckled in.
- **Penalties:** CDL **disqualification** (loss of the right to drive a CMV) after **2 or more convictions**: **2nd** within **3 years** → **60 days**; **3rd or more** within **3 years** → **120 days**. Every violation, including the **1st**, can also bring **civil penalties** (fines).
- **Emergency exception:** hand-held use is allowed to contact **law enforcement or emergency services**.
- Section 1.3.9 (p. 1-16, module GK-02) gives the same 60/120 days for California's hands-free/texting law, in any vehicle, plus 1 point on your record.
- **Research — dialing:** a **safety-critical event** (crash, near-crash, or drifting out of your lane by accident) is **6 times** more likely. Eyes leave the road for **3.8 seconds** on average. Worked example: 55 mph = **80.7 ft per second**; 80.7 × 3.8 ≈ **306 feet** — about a football field, blind.
- **Hands-free is NOT safer:** hands-free devices are **no less** distracting than hand-held ones — both pull your attention away.
- **[CA]** While driving, electronic devices are legal only in **hands-free** mode — and even that is unsafe.
- **If you must use a device while driving:** keep it **short**, no social chatting; **hang up** in difficult traffic; don't use equipment or devices when approaching **severe weather, road construction, heavy traffic or crowds of pedestrians**; **never type or read messages**.

### 2.9.5 Texting (p. 2-26)
- **Federal ban:** CMV drivers may not text while driving in **interstate commerce** (trade or transport that crosses a state line, or is part of a trip that does — defined on p. 1-13). Carriers may not require or allow it.
- **Texting** = entering text into an electronic device by hand, or reading text from it: text messages, email, instant messages, opening a web page, any other text entry or reading.
- **Electronic device** = cell phone, PDA, pager, computer, or anything used to write, send, receive or read text.
- **Disqualification:** same as phones — **60 days** (2nd in 3 years), **120 days** (3rd+ in 3 years).
- **Civil penalty:** **up to $2,750** per violation, first one included. (Beyond the handbook — not on the test: the federal amount is adjusted for inflation over time. On the test, use **$2,750**.)
- **Emergency exception:** only to contact **law enforcement or emergency services**.
- **Why texting is the worst:** eyes on a small screen **and** hands on keys — physical and mental distraction at once.
- **Research — texting:** safety-critical event **23.2 times** more likely; eyes off the road **4.6 seconds**; 80.7 × 4.6 ≈ **371 feet** at 55 mph — a whole football field.

| Don't mix these up | Dialing | Texting |
|---|---|---|
| Higher odds of a safety-critical event | **6 ×** | **23.2 ×** |
| Average eyes-off-road time | **3.8 s** | **4.6 s** |
| Distance at 55 mph | **306 ft** | **371 ft** |

### 2.9.6 Do not drive distracted (p. 2-26 – 2-27)
- **Goal:** remove **all** in-vehicle distractions **before** you drive. Check for possible distractions, make a plan to reduce them, expect them, and talk through situations in advance.
- **Why:** if distraction makes drivers react just **half a second** slower, crashes **double**.
- **Tips:**
  - **Turn off** communication devices. If you must use a phone: keep it **within reach while buckled in**, use an **earpiece or speakerphone**, **voice-activated dialing** and the **hands-free** feature. Reaching unsafely for the phone is a violation **even if you meant to use hands-free**.
  - Never type or read texts; don't read or write while driving.
  - **Before driving:** learn the vehicle's features; adjust controls and mirrors; preset radio stations and load CDs; clear loose objects and secure cargo; check maps, set the GPS and plan your route.
  - Avoid smoking, eating and drinking — leave early so you can stop to eat.
  - Avoid complex or emotional conversations; ask passengers to help you stay focused.

### 2.9.7 Watch out for other distracted drivers (p. 2-27)
- **Signs:** drifting over lane lines or within the lane; changing speeds; busy with maps, food, cigarettes, phones or other objects; talking with passengers.
- **Response:** give **plenty of room** and keep your **safe following distance**. **Pass very carefully** — they may not know you're there and could drift in front of you.

### 2.10.1 Aggressive driving and road rage (p. 2-27)
Heavy, slow traffic and tight schedules make drivers angry. Packed roads leave almost no margin for mistakes, so drivers grow suspicious and hostile and take others' mistakes personally.

| | Aggressive driving | Road rage |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning | Driving in a **selfish, bold or pushy** way, ignoring others' rights and safety | Driving with **intent to harm** others, or **physically attacking** a driver or their vehicle |
| Example sign | **Frequent, sudden lane changes without signaling** | — |

### 2.10.2 Do not be an aggressive driver (p. 2-27 – 2-28)
- Your **mood before you start** strongly affects how stress hits you on the road. Lower stress before and during the drive — e.g., calm "easy listening" music.
- Give driving your **full attention** (no phone calls, eating, etc.).
- Be **realistic about travel time**; expect delays from traffic, construction or weather. If you'll be late, **accept it** and take a deep breath.
- Give others the **benefit of the doubt** — whatever their reason, it isn't about you.
- **Slow down**; keep a reasonable following distance.
- If you are going slower than traffic, **stay out of the left lane**.
- **Avoid gestures**, even small ones like shaking your head; keep your hands on the wheel.
- Be courteous: if someone wants in front of you, let them — "**Be my guest**." It becomes a habit and other drivers will bother you less.

### 2.10.3 When an aggressive driver confronts you (p. 2-28)
1. **First and most important: get out of their way.**
2. **Put your pride aside** — don't speed up or refuse to give up your lane.
3. **Avoid eye contact.**
4. **Ignore gestures**; don't react.
5. **Report** them: **license number**, what the vehicle looks like, the location and, if you can, which way it was heading. Call police if you can use your phone safely.
6. If they crash farther on: **stop a safe distance away**, wait for police, and report what you saw.

## Exam traps
- **Trap:** "Hands-free phones are safe." → **Correct:** hands-free is **no less** distracting; **[CA]** it is the only legal way, but still unsafe. "Free" sounds harmless. (p. 2-25)
- **Trap:** The move-over law only means "slow down." → **Correct:** slow down **and** change to a lane not next to it when safe; slowing alone is the fallback. (p. 2-21)
- **Trap:** The posted ramp speed is safe for your truck. → **Correct:** it may be too fast for large or heavy vehicles; slow down **before** the curve. A posted sign feels official. (p. 2-22)
- **Trap:** An empty-looking box in the road is harmless. → **Correct:** it may hold something heavy; avoid it early and smoothly. (p. 2-22)
- **Trap:** A pedestrian looking at you will wait. → **Correct:** stay alert — they may think they have the right of way. (p. 2-22)
- **Trap:** SMV emblem vehicles go up to 35 or 45 mph. → **Correct:** not designed to exceed **25 mph [CA]**. (p. 2-23)
- **Trap:** 1st phone/texting conviction = disqualification, or 120 days for the 2nd. → **Correct:** 1st = civil penalty; **2nd in 3 years = 60 days**; **3rd+ = 120 days**. (p. 2-25, 2-26)
- **Trap:** Mixing up the research numbers. → **Correct:** dialing **6× / 3.8 s / 306 ft**; texting **23.2× / 4.6 s / 371 ft**; **39%** is brain energy, not a risk multiplier. (p. 2-24 – 2-26)
- **Trap:** Reaching for the phone is fine if you then use hands-free. → **Correct:** unsafe reaching is itself a violation. (p. 2-26)
- **Trap:** You may use a hand-held phone to tell dispatch you're late. → **Correct:** the exception covers only law enforcement or emergency services. (p. 2-25)
- **Trap:** Pass a distracted driver quickly. → **Correct:** give room, keep following distance, pass with great care. (p. 2-27)
- **Trap:** Aggressive driving = intent to harm. → **Correct:** that is **road rage**. The two terms appear together, so they get swapped. (p. 2-27)
- **Trap:** Speed up or hold your lane against an aggressive driver. → **Correct:** get out of their way; avoid eye contact. (p. 2-28)

## Handbook review questions — answered
**TYK 2.7 & 2.8 #5** What is a hazard?
→ Any road condition, or any other road user (driver, motorcyclist, bicyclist, pedestrian), that could be a danger. (p. 2-21)

**TYK 2.7 & 2.8 #6** What is the point of planning for an emergency once you spot a hazard?
→ Hazards can become emergencies. Planning early gives you time to act calmly (mirrors, signal, slow down, change lanes) instead of braking hard or swerving, which more often causes crashes. Always be ready to act on your plan. (p. 2-21, 2-24)

**TYK 2.9 & 2.10 #1** What tips help you avoid becoming a distracted driver?
→ Turn off devices; if you must use a phone, keep it in reach while belted and use an earpiece/speakerphone, voice dialing and hands-free; never text; before driving, learn the controls, adjust controls and mirrors, preset the radio, clear loose objects, secure cargo, and plan the route/program the GPS; don't read or write; avoid eating, drinking and smoking; avoid complex or emotional talks; get passengers' help. (p. 2-26 – 2-27)

**TYK 2.9 & 2.10 #2** How can you use phones and other communication equipment in the cab with care?
→ Hands-free only **[CA]**, within reach while belted. Keep it short, no social chatting; hang up in difficult traffic; don't use it near severe weather, construction, heavy traffic or crowds of pedestrians; never type or read messages. (p. 2-25 – 2-26)

**TYK 2.9 & 2.10 #3** What signs show that another driver is distracted?
→ Drifting over lane lines or within the lane, inconsistent speed, being busy with maps, food, cigarettes, phones or other objects, or talking with passengers. (p. 2-27)

**TYK 2.9 & 2.10 #4** How is road rage different from aggressive driving?
→ Aggressive driving is selfish, bold or pushy driving without regard for others' rights or safety. Road rage is driving with intent to harm others, or physically attacking a driver or their vehicle. (p. 2-27)

**TYK 2.9 & 2.10 #5** An aggressive driver confronts you. How should you respond?
→ Get out of their way first; don't challenge them; avoid eye contact; ignore gestures; report them (vehicle description, license number, location, direction), calling police only if you can do so safely; if they crash ahead, stop a safe distance away, wait for police and report what you saw. (p. 2-28)

**TYK 2.9 & 2.10 #6** Name ways to lower your stress level before and during a drive.
→ Start calm; listen to relaxing music; give driving full attention; expect delays and accept being late; give others the benefit of the doubt; slow down and keep a reasonable following distance; keep out of the left lane if you're slow; avoid gestures; be courteous ("Be my guest"). (p. 2-27 – 2-28)

## One-minute recap
- **Hazard** = possible danger; **emergency** = danger now; **conflict** = you must change speed or direction to avoid a hit. See hazards early and **always have a plan**.
- **Move-over [CA]:** slow down and move to a lane not next to a stopped emergency vehicle or work zone; if you can't, slow down and pass carefully. Violation = infraction + fine (CVC §21809).
- Road hazards: work zones (flashers/brake lights), drop-offs, debris (boxes may be heavy), ramps (posted speed may be too fast — slow before the curve).
- People hazards: can't see the driver = they can't see you; parked cars (exhaust, lights), buses, children, tourists, SMV emblem (**25 mph [CA]**), hurried and impaired drivers (weaving, green-light stops, open window in cold).
- Phone + texting = physical **and** mental distraction. Reacting **½ second** slower **doubles** crashes.
- Hand-held/texting: **60 days** (2nd in 3 years), **120 days** (3rd+); texting fine **up to $2,750**; exception only for police/emergency services. **[CA]** hands-free only — still just as distracting.
- Dialing **6× / 3.8 s / 306 ft**; texting **23.2× / 4.6 s / 371 ft** (55 mph = 80.7 ft/s).
- Aggressive driving = selfish, bold, pushy; road rage = intent to harm. When confronted: get out of the way, no eye contact, ignore gestures, report.
