# GK-10 · Night, fog, winter and hot-weather driving

> **Handbook:** Section 2.11–2.14, pages 2-28–2-34 · **Test:** General Knowledge · **Exam weight:** High · **Study time:** ~50 min

## What you'll be able to answer
- When to use high beams, when to switch to low beams, and the **500-foot** dimming rule.
- How far you can see with low and high beams, and why your speed must let you stop inside that distance.
- The warning signs of fatigue (tiredness), who is most at risk, and the only real cure.
- What to do if you must drive in fog.
- What to check before winter driving, how to spot ice, why to turn off the retarder, and how to dry wet brakes.
- How to handle hot tires, a hot radiator cap, loose belts and "bleeding tar" in very hot weather.

## Learn it

### 2.11.1 Why night driving is more dangerous (p. 2-28)
- At night you see hazards later, so you have **less time to react**. A driver who is surprised is less able to avoid a crash.
- Night driving problems come from three places: **the driver, the roadway, and the vehicle**. Each one is covered below.

### 2.11.2 Driver factors (pp. 2-28–2-30)

**Vision.** You steer, brake and accelerate based on what you see. If you can't see clearly, you notice traffic, road conditions and trouble too late.
- Get **regular** eye exams from an eye specialist. Weak vision can go unnoticed until you are tested.
- If you need glasses or contact lenses ("corrective lenses"):
  - Wear them **every time** you drive, even for a short trip. If your license has a corrective-lens restriction, it is illegal to move a vehicle without them.
  - Keep a **spare pair** in the vehicle in case your normal pair breaks or gets lost.
  - **Do not wear dark or tinted lenses at night**, even if they seem to cut glare. Why: they block the light you need to see.

**Glare.** Glare is bright light that dazzles your eyes. It can blind you for a short time, and recovery can take **several seconds**. Even **2 seconds** of glare blindness is dangerous: at **55 mph** you travel **more than half the length of a football field** in that time.

**Fatigue and lack of alertness.** Fatigue means being tired in body or mind. It can come from physical or mental strain, doing the same task over and over, illness, or not enough sleep. Like alcohol and drugs, fatigue harms your **vision and judgment**. A tired driver:
- makes mistakes judging speed and distance,
- sees and reacts to hazards more slowly,
- makes poor decisions,
- may fall asleep at the wheel. Even sleep "bursts" of a few seconds make a crash much more likely.

Fatigue facts the handbook gives (p. 2-29):

| Fact | Number |
|---|---|
| Police-reported crashes per year caused by drowsy driving (NHTSA estimate) | about **100,000** |
| Americans who have driven while sleepy | **60%** |
| Americans who admit they fell asleep at the wheel | more than **1/3** (36%) |
| Heavy truck crashes that involve fatigue | at least **15%** |
| Average daily sleep in a study of 80 long-haul drivers | **less than 5 hours** |
| Truck-driver deaths where drowsy driving was probably the cause (NTSB) | **more than half** |
| Other people killed for each truck driver killed | **3 to 4** |

**When is the risk highest?** When people are naturally sleepiest: **at night** (especially **after midnight**) and in the **mid-afternoon**. Many heavy-vehicle crashes happen between **midnight and 6 a.m.** Risk climbs further if you have already been driving a long time.

**Who is most at risk?** Research points to: shift workers; young men; commercial drivers, above all **long-haul** drivers; people whose sleep disorder is not being treated; and anyone who is short on sleep, whether for a few days or for a long time.

**Are you at risk? Ask yourself before you drive.** Your risk is higher if you:
- had **6 hours of sleep or less** (this **triples** your risk), or are otherwise short on sleep;
- have insomnia (trouble sleeping) or poor-quality sleep;
- are driving long distances without proper rest breaks;
- are driving through the night, in the mid-afternoon, or when you would normally be asleep;
- take medicine that makes you sleepy (sedating), such as antidepressants, cold tablets or antihistamines (allergy pills);
- work **more than 60 hours a week** (raises your risk by **40%**);
- have more than one job and your main job is shift work;
- are driving by yourself, or on a long, dark, boring country road;
- have flown and changed time zones.

**Warning signs — stop and rest if you notice any of these:**
- hard to focus, blinking a lot, heavy eyelids;
- yawning again and again, rubbing your eyes;
- daydreaming, wandering thoughts;
- can't remember the last few miles; missed an exit or a traffic sign;
- hard to hold your head up;
- drifting out of your lane, following too closely, or hitting a rumble strip on the shoulder;
- feeling restless and irritable.

"Pushing on" while tired is far riskier than most drivers believe, and it is behind many deadly crashes. **What to do:** stop driving and sleep for the night, or take a **15–20 minute nap**.

**Before a trip — prevent drowsiness (p. 2-30):**
- Get enough sleep. Adults need **8–9 hours** to stay alert.
- Plan your route: total distance, where you will stop, and other details.
- Plan to drive during your usual waking hours, not overnight.
- Bring a passenger along.
- Stay away from medicines that make you drowsy.
- Talk to your doctor if you feel sleepy during the day, have trouble sleeping at night, or nap a lot.
- Exercise regularly — it gives you more energy.

**While driving — stay alert (p. 2-30):**
- In daytime, wear sunglasses to prevent glare and eye strain. (Never at night — see 2.11.5.)
- Keep the cab cool: open a window or use the air conditioner.
- Don't eat heavy meals.
- Know your low-energy times of the day.
- Ride with another driver and take turns driving.
- Take a break about every **100 miles or 2 hours** on long trips.
- Stop and rest, or nap, when you need to.
- Caffeine raises alertness for only **a few hours**, then wears off. Don't drink too much and **don't rely on it** to prevent fatigue.
- Avoid drugs. They may keep you awake for a while, but they **do not make you alert**.

**Bottom line:** for drowsiness, **nothing is safe except leaving the road and sleeping**.

### 2.11.3 Roadway factors (p. 2-30)
**Poor lighting.** Many roads have little or no lighting at night, so your headlights may be your only light.
- People and animals without lights are hard to see. Many night crashes involve **pedestrians, bicyclists and animals**.
- Bright areas can be confusing too: traffic signals and hazards get lost against signs, shop windows and other lights.
- **Rule:** slow down when lighting is poor or confusing. Drive slowly enough that you can **stop within the distance you can see ahead**.

**Drunk drivers.** Drivers on alcohol or drugs endanger themselves and you. Be extra alert around **bar and tavern closing times**. Watch for drivers who can't stay in their lane, can't hold a steady speed, stop for no reason, or show other signs of being impaired.

### 2.11.4 Vehicle factors (pp. 2-30–2-31)
**Headlights** are usually your main light at night — for seeing and for being seen. You see much less with them than in daylight.

| Beam | How far ahead you can see |
|---|---|
| Low beams | about **250 ft** |
| High beams | about **350–500 ft** |

- **Rule:** keep your stopping distance **within your sight distance** — drive slowly enough to stop inside the reach of your headlights. Why: if you are too fast, by the time a hazard shows up in your lights you have no room to stop.
- **Worked example:** the stopping-distance chart on p. 2-16 shows a truck at **55 mph** needs at least **419 ft** to stop — far more than the ~250 ft you see on low beams. At **35 mph** it needs about **222 ft**, which fits inside low-beam range.
- **Dirty headlights** may give only **half** the light they should. You see less, and others see you less. Keep them clean and working.
- **Headlights out of adjustment** point the wrong way: you don't see the road well, and you can blind other drivers. Have a qualified person adjust them.

**[CA] When your headlights must be on** (CVC §§280 and 24400):
- from **½ hour after sunset to ½ hour before sunrise**;
- whenever snow, rain, fog or other bad weather makes you use your **windshield wipers**;
- whenever you **cannot clearly see a person or vehicle 1,000 ft** away.

**[CA] Parking lights:** you may **never drive with only parking lights on**. They may be used as signals, or together with the headlights (CVC §24800).

**Other lights** that must be clean and working so others can see you: **reflectors, marker lights, clearance lights, taillights, identification lights**.

**Turn signals and brake lights** matter even more at night. They are how other drivers learn what you plan to do. Keep them clean and working.

**Windshield and mirrors.** At night, bright lights make dirt on glass glow and create glare of its own. (Think of driving toward a low sun through a windshield that looked clean at noon.) Clean the windshield on the **inside and outside**.

### 2.11.5 Night driving procedures (p. 2-31)
**Before you drive:**
- Be rested. If you are drowsy, **sleep first** — even a nap can save lives.
- Glasses must be clean and not scratched. **No sunglasses at night.**
- Do a complete vehicle inspection. Pay special attention to **all lights and reflectors**, and clean the ones you can reach.

**Dim your lights so you don't blind others.** Your high beams dazzle drivers coming toward you. They also bother drivers ahead of you, because your light hits their mirrors. Dim **before** you cause glare:
- when a vehicle **coming toward you** is within **500 ft**, and
- when a vehicle **you are following** is within **500 ft**.

> Note: the California car-driver handbook uses 300 ft for following. The CDL test follows this handbook — answer **500 ft** for both oncoming and following.

**Handling glare from oncoming vehicles.**
- Don't look straight into oncoming headlights. Look **slightly to the right**, at the right lane line or edge line if there is one.
- If another driver keeps their high beams on, **don't "get back at them"** with your own high beams. That adds glare for them and raises the chance of a crash.

**Use high beams when you can.** Driving on low beams all the time is a mistake — it badly shortens how far ahead you see. Use high beams whenever it is **safe and legal** — for example, when you are **not within 500 ft** of an oncoming vehicle or a vehicle you are following.

**Keep the cab dark.** A bright cab makes it harder to see outside. Keep the **interior (dome) light off**. Turn the instrument lights down as low as you can while still reading the gauges.

**Getting sleepy? Stop at the nearest safe place.** Drivers often have no idea they are about to doze off — even while their eyes are drooping shut. When it is safe, check your face in a mirror. Sleepy-looking eyes, or just feeling sleepy, mean **stop driving now** — this is a very dangerous state. Only sleep fixes it.

### 2.12 Driving in fog (p. 2-32)
Fog can form at any time, often without warning, and visibility can drop quickly. Watch for it and be ready to slow down. **Don't assume the fog will thin out** once you are in it.

**Best advice: don't drive in fog.** Pull off into a **rest area or truck stop** and wait until you can see better. If you must drive:
- Obey every fog warning sign.
- **Slow down before** you enter the fog.
- Use **low beams and fog lights**, even in daytime. Watch for drivers who forgot to turn on their lights. (Why: bright high beams reflect off the fog back into your eyes.)
- Turn on your **4-way emergency flashers**, so drivers coming up behind you notice you sooner.
- Watch for vehicles on the side of the road. Lights ahead of you **may not show where the road is** — that vehicle might not be on the road at all.
- Use **roadside reflectors** to see how the road curves ahead.
- **Listen** for traffic you can't see.
- **Avoid passing.**
- **Don't stop on the side of the road** unless you absolutely must. (Why: drivers behind may not see you in time.)

### 2.13.1 Winter: vehicle checks (pp. 2-32–2-33)
Do your normal inspection, and pay extra attention to these items:

| Item | What to check | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Coolant and antifreeze | System is full, with enough antifreeze to protect against freezing (use a special coolant tester) | Protects the cooling system against freezing |
| Defrosters and heater | Defrosters work; heater works and you know how to use it; check any other heaters you rely on (mirror, battery box, fuel tank heaters) | You need defrosters to see safely |
| Wipers and washers | Blades in good condition and press hard enough on the glass; washer works; reservoir has fluid; use **washer antifreeze** | Weak blades won't clear snow; plain washer fluid can freeze |
| Tires | **Front** tires: **4/32 inch** or deeper in each major groove. **All other** tires: **2/32 inch** or deeper. Deeper is better; use a tread gauge | Drive tires need grip to push the rig through snow and water; steering tires need grip to steer |
| Tire chains | Carry the right number of chains plus **extra cross-links**; make sure they fit your **drive tires**; look for broken hooks, worn or broken cross-links, bent or broken side chains; **practice putting them on** before you need to | Some roads can't be driven without chains — even to reach a safe place |
| Lights and reflectors | Clean and working; recheck them now and then during bad weather | They matter most in bad weather |
| Windows and mirrors | Remove ice and snow **before starting** (scraper, snow brush, defroster) | You must see out |
| Hand holds, steps, deck plates | Remove all ice and snow | Prevents slips and falls |
| Radiator shutters and winterfront | Clear any ice off the shutters; don't set the winterfront **too tight** (too closed) | Iced-shut shutters or an over-closed winterfront block air, so the engine can **overheat and quit** |
| Exhaust system | Look and listen for loose parts and leaks | Leaks can let **carbon monoxide** into the cab, especially with windows up. It makes you **sleepy** and in large amounts can **kill** |

Terms: **radiator shutters** are slats in front of the radiator that open and close to control air flow. A **winterfront** is a cover over the front grille that blocks cold air in winter. **Cross-links** are the short chain pieces that go across the tire tread.

If your view gets poor on the road — say the wipers quit — **pull over safely and fix it** before going on.

### 2.13.2 Driving on slippery surfaces (p. 2-33)
On a slippery road, keep everything **slow and smooth**. If the road is extremely slippery, don't drive — **pull off at the first safe spot**.
- **Start off slowly and gently.** Feel how much grip the road has. Take your time.
- **Check for ice**, especially on **bridges and overpasses**.
  - If other vehicles' tires are throwing **no spray**, ice has formed.
  - Check your **mirrors and wiper blades**. Ice on them means the road is probably icy too.
  - (More ice clues, such as black ice, are in GK-07, p. 2-17.)
- **Turning and braking:** turn as gently as possible. Don't brake harder than you need to. **Don't use the engine brake or speed retarder** — they can make the **drive wheels skid** on a slippery road.
- **Speed:** don't pass slower vehicles unless you must. Go slowly and look far ahead so you can keep a steady speed — avoid slowing down and speeding up. Take curves slower and **don't brake in a curve**. When the temperature rises to the point where **ice starts to melt**, the road gets **even more slippery** — slow down more.
- **Space:** don't drive alongside other vehicles. Keep a **longer following distance**. If traffic is jammed up ahead, ease off or stop until it clears. Plan stops early and slow down gradually. Give **snowplows, salt trucks and sand trucks** plenty of room.

**Wet brakes.** Heavy rain or deep standing water can soak your brakes. Wet brakes may be **weak**, apply **unevenly**, or **grab**. Results:
- less braking power,
- wheels locking up,
- the vehicle pulling to one side,
- a **jackknife** if you pull a trailer (the trailer swings around and the rig folds like a pocketknife).

Avoid deep puddles and flowing water if you can. If you can't:
1. Slow down and shift to a **low gear**.
2. Put the brakes on **gently**. This presses the linings against the drums or discs and keeps mud, silt, sand and water out.
3. **Speed up the engine (higher rpm)** and drive through with a **light, steady touch** on the brake.
4. Once you are through the water, keep a light foot on the brake for a **short distance** so the heat dries them out.
5. When it is safe, make a **test stop**: first make sure nobody is following you, then brake and see if they work properly. If they don't, repeat the drying steps.

**Caution:** don't press hard on the brake and the accelerator at the same time — you can **overheat** the brake drums and linings.

### 2.14.1 Very hot weather: vehicle checks (p. 2-34)
Do your normal inspection, with special attention to:

**Tires.** Check how they are mounted and their air pressure. In very hot weather, inspect tires **every 2 hours or every 100 miles**.
- Air pressure **goes up** as tires get hot. **Never let air out** — when the tire cools, the pressure will be too low.
- If a tire is **too hot to touch**, stay stopped until it cools. Otherwise it may **blow out or catch fire**.

**Engine oil** does two jobs: it **cools** the engine and **lubricates** it. Make sure there is enough. If you have an oil temperature gauge, keep the reading in the proper range while driving.

**Engine coolant.** Before you start, make sure the cooling system has enough water and antifreeze, following the engine maker's directions. (Antifreeze helps in **heat as well as cold**.) While driving, check the water/coolant temperature gauge now and then. If it goes **above the highest safe temperature**, something may be wrong that could cause engine failure or even fire — **stop as soon as you safely can** and find out what is wrong.

**Checking coolant on a hot engine.** On some trucks you can read the coolant level with the engine still hot, using a **sight glass**, a **see-through overflow container**, or a **coolant recovery container**. When that container sits **outside the pressurized system**, its cap may be opened and coolant added safely, even at normal running temperature.

**The radiator cap — danger.** Keep the radiator cap, and every other part of the pressurized cooling system, **closed until the system has cooled**. Open it hot and the pressure can blast out steam and boiling coolant, causing severe burns. Simple test: a cap that is cool enough to **touch bare-handed** is probably safe to open.

Adding coolant to a system **without** a recovery or overflow tank:
1. Shut the engine off.
2. Wait until the engine has cooled.
3. Protect your hands with gloves or a thick cloth.
4. Turn the cap **slowly to the first stop** — this releases the pressure seal.
5. **Step back** while the pressure escapes.
6. Once the pressure is fully gone, push the cap down and keep turning to take it off.
7. Look at the coolant level and add coolant if needed.
8. Put the cap back and turn it **all the way closed**.

**Engine belts.** Know how to test V-belt tension on your truck: push on each belt. A **slack (loose) belt** can't drive the **water pump and/or fan** the way it should, so the engine **overheats**. Also look for cracking and other wear.

**Hoses.** Coolant hoses must be in good shape. If one bursts on the road, it can **wreck the engine or even start a fire**.

### 2.14.2 Driving in the heat (p. 2-34)
- **Watch for bleeding tar.** In very hot weather, tar in the road often rises to the surface. These "bleeding" spots are **very slippery**.
- **Go slowly enough to prevent overheating.** Higher speed makes more heat in the tires and the engine. In desert heat this can build up to a dangerous level, raising the chance of **tire failure, fire, and engine failure**.

## Exam traps
- **Trap:** "Use low beams whenever you can." → **Correct:** use **high beams whenever it is safe and legal**. Low beams feel polite, but they cut your view to about 250 ft. (p. 2-31)
- **Trap:** Dim when following at 300 ft. → **Correct:** **500 ft** for following and for oncoming. The 300-ft figure comes from the car handbook. (p. 2-31)
- **Trap:** Flash your high beams at a driver who won't dim. → **Correct:** never retaliate; look slightly right at the lane or edge line. Payback feels fair, but it adds glare for the oncoming driver and raises the chance of a crash. (p. 2-31)
- **Trap:** Tinted glasses or sunglasses help with night glare. → **Correct:** **never** wear them at night — they cut the light you need. (pp. 2-28, 2-31)
- **Trap:** Coffee, cold air or the radio will fix drowsiness. → **Correct:** the **only safe cure is sleep** (stop for the night or nap 15–20 minutes). The other tricks help a little, so they seem like answers. (pp. 2-29–2-30)
- **Trap:** In fog, high beams help you see farther. → **Correct:** **low beams and fog lights**, plus 4-way flashers. (p. 2-32)
- **Trap:** In fog, follow the lights ahead of you. → **Correct:** that vehicle may be off the road; use **roadside reflectors** to find the curve. (p. 2-32)
- **Trap:** In fog, stop on the shoulder and wait. → **Correct:** pull into a **rest area or truck stop**; don't stop at the roadside unless absolutely necessary. (p. 2-32)
- **Trap:** Front tires need 2/32 inch. → **Correct:** front **4/32**, other tires **2/32**. The numbers are easy to swap. (p. 2-32)
- **Trap:** A retarder or engine brake gives extra control on ice. → **Correct:** **turn it off** on slippery roads — it can skid the drive wheels. The name sounds like a safety device. (p. 2-33)
- **Trap:** Lots of road spray means ice. → **Correct:** **no spray** means ice has formed. (p. 2-33)
- **Trap:** Melting ice is safer. → **Correct:** when ice starts to melt the road is **even more slippery**. (p. 2-33)
- **Trap:** Dry wet brakes by pressing hard on brake and gas together. → **Correct:** use **light** brake pressure; hard brake plus gas overheats drums and linings. (p. 2-33)
- **Trap:** Let air out of hot tires to bring pressure back to normal. → **Correct:** **never** — they'll be underinflated once cool. It sounds like "relieving" pressure. (p. 2-34)
- **Trap:** The radiator cap is safe to remove if the engine isn't overheated. → **Correct:** only when the system has **cooled** (cap cool enough to touch bare-handed), then turn slowly to the **first stop**. A "normal" hot engine is still under pressure. (p. 2-34)
- **Trap:** You can never open any coolant cap on a hot engine. → **Correct:** a see-through overflow/recovery container that is **not part of the pressurized system** can be opened hot. (p. 2-34)

## Handbook review questions — answered
**TYK 2.11–2.14 #1** True or false: you should use your low beams whenever you can.
→ **False.** Always using low beams cuts how far ahead you can see (about 250 ft instead of 350–500 ft). Use **high beams whenever it is safe and legal** — when you are not within 500 ft of an oncoming vehicle or a vehicle you are following. (p. 2-31)

**TYK 2.11–2.14 #2** If you are drowsy before a trip, what should you do?
→ **Get some sleep first, then drive.** Even a short nap may save a life — yours or another person's. Drowsiness has only one safe cure: sleep. To avoid being drowsy in the first place, get 8–9 hours of sleep, schedule driving for your normal waking hours, plan the route, avoid drowsy-making medicines, and drive with a passenger. (pp. 2-30–2-31)

**TYK 2.11–2.14 #3** What can wet brakes do, and how do you prevent the problems?
→ Wet brakes can be weak, apply unevenly, or grab. This causes less braking power, wheel lockup, pulling to one side, and a jackknife if you pull a trailer. Avoid deep puddles and flowing water when possible. If you must go through: slow down and use a low gear; apply the brakes gently; raise engine rpm and cross while keeping light brake pressure; after the water, keep light pressure for a short distance to heat and dry them; then make a test stop when no one is behind you (dry them more if needed). Don't use heavy brake and gas together — it overheats the drums and linings. (p. 2-33)

**TYK 2.11–2.14 #4** True or false: when tires are hot, you should release some air to bring the pressure back down to normal.
→ **False.** Pressure rises with heat and drops again as the tire cools. If you let air out, the tire will be underinflated when it cools. If a tire is too hot to touch, stay stopped until it cools, or it may blow out or catch fire. (p. 2-34)

**TYK 2.11–2.14 #5** True or false: it is safe to remove the radiator cap as long as the engine isn't overheated.
→ **False.** Wait until the system has cooled before you open the radiator cap or any other part of the pressurized system — while it is hot, steam and boiling coolant can shoot out and badly burn you. A cap you can touch bare-handed is probably cool enough. Then: engine off, protect your hands, turn the cap slowly to the first stop, step back while pressure escapes, and only then remove it. (p. 2-34)

## One-minute recap
- Night: you see hazards later. Drive slow enough to **stop within your headlight range** (low ~**250 ft**, high ~**350–500 ft**).
- Use **high beams whenever safe and legal**; dim within **500 ft** of oncoming **and** following vehicles. Never retaliate; look slightly right.
- [CA] Headlights on **½ hr after sunset to ½ hr before sunrise**, when wipers are needed, or when you can't see **1,000 ft**. Never parking lights alone.
- Fatigue: watch for yawning, drifting, missed exits. The **only cure is sleep** (or a **15–20 min** nap). Breaks every **100 miles / 2 hours**. No sunglasses at night.
- Fog: best not to drive. If you must: slow **before** entering, **low beams + fog lights + 4-way flashers**, follow reflectors, don't pass, don't stop at the roadside.
- Winter: tread **4/32** front, **2/32** others; chains with extra cross-links; winterfront not too tight; watch for **no spray** = ice; **retarder off**; melting ice = **more** slippery.
- Wet brakes: low gear, **light** brake pressure through the water and a short distance after, then a test stop.
- Heat: check tires every **2 hours / 100 miles**; **never let air out**; radiator cap only when cool enough to **touch bare-handed**, turn to **first stop**; bleeding tar is slippery.
