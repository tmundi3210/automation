# GK-08 · Managing space: following distance, turns, clearance

> **Handbook:** Section 2.7–2.7.7, pages 2-19–2-21 · **Test:** General Knowledge · **Exam weight:** High · **Study time:** ~35 min

## What you'll be able to answer
- Work out the right following distance, in seconds, for any truck length at any speed.
- Measure the following distance you actually have, using a landmark and a count.
- Say what to do — and what NOT to do — when someone tailgates you.
- Explain why you avoid driving beside other vehicles, and what strong wind changes.
- Judge clearance above and below your vehicle, including why an empty van can hit a bridge that a loaded one cleared.
- Make right and left turns safely: button hook vs jug handle, oncoming traffic, and which lane to use when there are two turn lanes.

## Learn it

### 2.7 Why space matters (p. 2-19)
Space around your vehicle is time. When something goes wrong, space gives you time to think and act.
A truck needs more space than a car for two reasons: it **takes up more room**, and it needs **more room to stop and to turn**.
So you manage the space on every side of you — ahead, behind, the sides, above, below — plus the space you need for turns and for crossing traffic.

### 2.7.1 Space ahead (p. 2-19)
**The space ahead is the most important space of all** — it is the space you are driving into.

**Why you need it.** If you must stop suddenly, you need room. A smaller vehicle ahead of you can usually stop faster than your truck can. If you follow too closely, you may crash into it.

**The following-distance rule** (the handbook calls it the heavy vehicle formula, Figure 2.12):

| Speed | Rule |
|---|---|
| **Below 40 mph** | At least **1 second for every 10 feet** of your vehicle's length |
| **Above 40 mph** | Use the same rule, then **add 1 second** |

Why the extra second? At higher speed you cover more ground before you can react and stop, so you need a bigger cushion.

**Quick method:** take your length in feet, drop the last zero — that is your seconds. Going over 40 mph? Add 1.

**Worked examples**

| Vehicle length | Below 40 mph | Above 40 mph |
|---|---|---|
| 30 ft | 3 seconds | **4 seconds** |
| 40 ft | **4 seconds** | **5 seconds** |
| 50 ft | 5 seconds | **6 seconds** |
| 60 ft | **6 seconds** | **7 seconds** |
| 70 ft | 7 seconds | 8 seconds |

The handbook itself gives: a 40-ft truck = **4 s** (5 s over 40 mph); a 60-ft rig = **6 s** (7 s over 40 mph); a 50-ft truck above 40 mph = **6 s**. The other rows use the same rule.

Notice: you add **one** second in total above 40 mph — not one second for every extra 10 mph.

**Slippery roads:** you need **more** space than the rule gives, because it takes longer to stop.

**How to measure your following distance** (p. 2-19)
1. Pick a fixed landmark ahead: a shadow on the road, a pavement marking, a sign.
2. When the vehicle ahead passes it, start counting: "one thousand-and-one, one thousand-and-two…"
3. Stop counting when the front of your vehicle reaches the same spot.
4. Compare your count with the rule.

*Example:* you drive a 40-ft truck and only reach "two." That is too close. Fall back, then count again. Keep adjusting until the count reaches **4 seconds** (**5 seconds** above 40 mph). With practice you learn how far back that is.

### 2.7.2 Space behind (pp. 2-19 – 2-20)
You cannot stop people from following you too closely, but you can make it safer.

**Stay to the right.** Trucks get tailgated when they cannot keep up with traffic — very often on an **uphill** climb. When a heavy load holds your speed down, keep to the right lane where possible. On a climb, pass another slow vehicle only if you can get by it quickly and safely.

**When tailgating usually happens:**
- When you are **traveling slowly** — drivers stuck behind slow vehicles often follow closely.
- In **bad weather** — many car drivers stay close behind a truck, especially when they can't see far down the road.

**If you are being tailgated — the four responses:**

| Do this | Why |
|---|---|
| **Avoid quick changes.** Signal early and slow down very gradually. | A sudden slowdown is how the tailgater hits you. |
| **Increase your following distance** (open up more space in front of you). | You will not need to brake or swerve suddenly, and it is easier for the tailgater to pass you. |
| **Do not speed up.** | Being tailgated at a low speed is safer than at a high speed. |
| **Avoid tricks** — no switching on your taillights, no blinking your brake lights at the tailgater. | Tricks can confuse or provoke the other driver. Just use the steps above. |

The most-tested point: when someone follows **you** too closely, you make the space **in front of you** bigger — not smaller.

### 2.7.3 Space to the sides (p. 2-20)
Commercial vehicles are wide and fill most of a lane, so there is little spare room.

**Stay centered in your lane.** This keeps some clearance on both sides.

**Avoid traveling next to other vehicles.** There are **2 dangers**:
1. A driver next to you may suddenly swerve into your lane and hit you.
2. You may be boxed in, with no way out, at the moment you need to change lanes.

Find an open spot away from other traffic. In heavy traffic that can be hard. If you must travel near others, keep as much space as you can, and drop back or pull forward so the other driver can see you.

**Strong winds** can push you out of your lane. Wind usually causes **more trouble for lighter vehicles**, and it can be especially bad **as you come out of a tunnel**. Avoid driving alongside other vehicles if you can.

### 2.7.4 Space overhead (p. 2-20)
Hitting something overhead is a real danger. Always make sure you have room above you.
- **Do not trust posted heights** at bridges and overpasses. **Repaving or packed snow** may have lowered the real clearance since the sign was put up.
- **Weight changes height.** An **empty** van sits **higher** than a loaded one, because the springs are not pressed down. If you cleared a bridge loaded, you might **not** clear it empty.
- **In doubt?** Go slowly. **Not sure you will fit?** Take another route. Low bridges and underpasses often have warning signs — but not always.
- **Tilted roads.** Some roads tilt your vehicle, so the top of it leans toward signs, trees or bridge supports at the road edge. Where this happens, **move a little toward the center of the road**.
- **Before backing**, get out and look for overhanging objects such as tree branches or electric wires. They are easy to miss from the seat. Check for other hazards at the same time.

### 2.7.5 Space below (p. 2-20)
Many drivers forget the space **under** their vehicle. It can be very small when the vehicle is heavily loaded.
- **Dirt roads and unpaved yards** are common places to get hung up.
- **Drainage channels** across a road can make the ends of some vehicles drag. Cross them carefully.
- **Railroad tracks** are a problem, especially with trailers that have **low clearance underneath**. Do not risk getting hung up halfway across.

### 2.7.6 Space for turns (pp. 2-20 – 2-21)
Trucks hit other vehicles and objects in turns because they turn wide and because of **off-tracking** — the rear wheels follow a shorter path than the front wheels, cutting inside the turn. (Off-tracking is covered in detail in CV-01.)

**Right turns** (p. 2-20; Figure 2.13 on p. 2-21)
- **Turn slowly.** It gives you and other road users more time to avoid problems.
- If your truck needs part of another lane to get around a right-hand corner, **turn wide as you complete the turn**, and hold the **back of the vehicle near the curb**. This blocks other drivers from sneaking past you on the right. The handbook calls this shape the **button hook — CORRECT**.
- **Do not swing out to the left at the start of the turn.** The handbook calls this the **jug handle — INCORRECT**. A driver behind you may believe you are going left and pull up on your right side. Then you may hit that vehicle as you finish your turn.
- If the turn takes you into the **oncoming lane**, look out for traffic heading toward you. Leave those vehicles room to get by, or stop and wait. But **do not back up** to make room — you could hit someone behind you.

**Left turns** (pp. 2-20 – 2-21)
- **Don't begin a left turn until you are at the center of the intersection.** If you turn too soon, off-tracking can make the left side of your vehicle hit another vehicle.
- **If there are 2 left-turn lanes, use the right-hand one** (the outside lane). If you start from the inside (left) lane you may have to swing right to complete the turn, into the other lane's traffic. Drivers on your left are also easier for you to see. (Figure 2.14)

### 2.7.7 Space to cross or enter traffic (p. 2-21)
Keep your size and weight in mind whenever you cross or join traffic.
- Trucks pick up speed slowly and take up a lot of room, so joining traffic can take a **much larger gap** than a car would need.
- Acceleration **depends on the load**. When heavily loaded, allow **even more room**.
- Only start across a road when you are sure you can get **all the way across** before any traffic gets to you.

**[CA] Related California rule:** keeping at least **300 feet** behind certain vehicles outside business and residence districts is a separate state law, taught in **GK-07** (p. 2-18). Do not mix it up with the seconds rule above.

## Exam traps
- **Trap:** When tailgated, slow down or flash your brake lights to warn the driver. → **Correct:** **Increase your following distance**, signal early and slow very gradually. No taillight or brake-light tricks. Warning the driver feels natural, but sudden signals invite a rear-end crash. (pp. 2-19 – 2-20)
- **Trap:** Speed up to get away from the tailgater. → **Correct:** **Do not speed up** — being tailgated at low speed is safer. (p. 2-20)
- **Trap:** "Decrease your following distance so the tailgater has room behind you." → **Correct:** False — you **increase** the space ahead. (p. 2-19)
- **Trap:** Above 40 mph, add 1 second for every extra 10 mph. → **Correct:** Add **1 second, once**. A 40-ft truck at 65 mph still needs 5 s. (p. 2-19)
- **Trap:** A 30-ft truck at 55 mph needs 3 seconds. → **Correct:** **4 seconds** (3 + 1, because it is over 40 mph). People forget the extra second. (p. 2-19)
- **Trap:** The height on the bridge sign is exact. → **Correct:** Clearance may be **lower** than posted (repaving, packed snow). (p. 2-20)
- **Trap:** If you fit under a bridge loaded, you will fit empty. → **Correct:** An **empty** van rides **higher**. (p. 2-20)
- **Trap:** Swing left before a tight right turn to make room. → **Correct:** No — that is the **jug handle**. Turn wide **as you complete** the turn (button hook). (p. 2-20)
- **Trap:** Back up to let an oncoming car pass during your turn. → **Correct:** **Do not back up** — give room or stop. (p. 2-20)
- **Trap:** With two left-turn lanes, take the inside (left) one so you have more room. → **Correct:** Take the **right-hand** lane. (p. 2-21)
- **Trap:** Start a left turn early so your trailer clears the corner. → **Correct:** Reach the **center** of the intersection first; turning early can make off-tracking swing the left side of your vehicle into another vehicle. (p. 2-20)
- **Trap:** Strong wind is mainly a problem for heavy, loaded trucks. → **Correct:** It is usually worse for **lighter** vehicles. (p. 2-20)
- **Trap:** On a road that tilts you toward roadside signs, move toward the edge to stay out of traffic. → **Correct:** Move a little toward the **center** of the road. (p. 2-20)

## Handbook review questions — answered
**TYK 2.7–2.8 #1** How can you tell how many seconds of following distance you have?
→ Wait until the vehicle ahead passes a fixed landmark (a shadow, pavement marking or sign). Count "one thousand-and-one, one thousand-and-two…" until the front of your vehicle reaches the same spot. Then compare your count with the rule: 1 second for each 10 ft of your vehicle's length, plus 1 second above 40 mph. (p. 2-19)

**TYK 2.7–2.8 #2** How much following time, in seconds, does a 30-foot vehicle need at 55 mph?
→ **4 seconds**: 3 seconds for the length (30 ÷ 10), plus 1 second because you are going faster than 40 mph. (p. 2-19)

**TYK 2.7–2.8 #3** True or false: if someone follows you too closely, you should shorten your own following distance.
→ **False.** You should **increase** your following distance. The extra space ahead means you will not have to stop or swerve suddenly, and it gives the tailgater an easier chance to pass you. (p. 2-19)

**TYK 2.7–2.8 #4** True or false: if you swing wide to the left before a right turn, the driver behind you may try to pass on your right.
→ **True.** The driver may think you are turning left and move up on your right side, and you could hit them as you finish the turn. Instead, keep the rear near the curb and turn wide as you complete the turn. (p. 2-20)

## One-minute recap
- **Following distance:** 1 second per 10 ft below 40 mph; add **1** second above 40 mph. 40 ft → 4 s / 5 s · 60 ft → 6 s / 7 s · 30 ft at 55 → 4 s.
- Measure it with a **landmark and a count**; slippery roads need more.
- **Tailgated?** Increase the space ahead, signal early, slow gradually, don't speed up, no light tricks.
- Stay centered; don't ride beside other vehicles (lane-change danger, getting trapped). Wind is worse for light vehicles and at tunnel exits.
- **Overhead:** posted heights can be wrong; an empty van rides higher; tilted road → move toward the center; look up before backing.
- **Below:** dirt roads, yards, drainage channels and railroad tracks can hang you up.
- **Right turn:** button hook (wide at the end), never jug handle; don't back up for oncoming cars.
- **Left turn:** start at the center of the intersection; with two left-turn lanes, use the right-hand one. Entering traffic needs a much bigger gap.
