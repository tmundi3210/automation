# GK-14 · Transporting cargo safely

> **Handbook:** Section 3.1–3.4, pages 3-1–3-5 · **Test:** General Knowledge · **Exam weight:** High · **Study time:** ~45 min

## What you'll be able to answer
- The four cargo duties every driver has, even when someone else loaded the truck.
- When to inspect and re-check cargo during a trip (first 50 miles; every 3 hours or 150 miles; every break).
- What GVW, GCW, GVWR, GCWR, axle weight and tire load mean, and what a bridge formula does.
- How a high center of gravity or badly balanced weight affects steering, traction and rollover.
- How many tie-downs a load needs, how strong they must be, and what blocking, bracing and header boards do.
- How to handle special cargo (sealed, dry bulk, hanging meat, livestock, oversized) and **[CA]** California's rules for spills, wide loads and projecting loads.

## Learn it

Cargo questions are one of the biggest topics on the General Knowledge test, and "weight distribution" is one of the most-missed areas. Learn this module well.

**Words you need first**
- **Cargo / load:** the goods your vehicle carries.
- **Secure (verb):** to fasten cargo so it cannot move.
- **Axle:** the bar that connects a pair of wheels. **Steering axle** = the front axle you steer with. **Drive axles** = the axles the engine turns.
- **Center of gravity (CG):** the balance point of all the weight. A **high CG** means the weight sits high above the road.
- **Flatbed:** a trailer with a flat deck and no sides or roof.

### Section 3 introduction: why cargo safety matters (p. 3-1)
Bad loading or bad securing puts people at risk:
- Cargo that falls off can block traffic and injure or kill other people.
- Loose cargo can injure or kill **you** in a quick stop or a crash.
- Too much weight (an overload) can damage your vehicle.
- How the vehicle is loaded can change the steering and make the vehicle harder to control.

**Your four responsibilities.** Whether or not you loaded and secured the cargo yourself, **you** are responsible for:
1. Inspecting the cargo.
2. Recognizing overloads and poorly balanced weight.
3. Knowing the cargo is properly secured and does **not block your view** ahead or to the sides.
4. Knowing the cargo does **not block your access to emergency equipment**.

**HazMat.** To haul hazardous materials that require **placards** (the diamond-shaped warning signs on the vehicle), you also need an **"H" endorsement**. Section 9 of the handbook covers the HazMat test.

### 3.1 Inspecting cargo (p. 3-1)
Your pre-trip vehicle inspection includes the cargo: confirm the truck **isn't carrying too much weight** (not overloaded) and that the load is **balanced** and **properly secured**.

| When | What to do |
|---|---|
| Before the trip | Check cargo as part of the vehicle inspection |
| Within the first **50 miles** | Check the cargo and the devices securing it again; adjust as needed |
| After **3 hours or 150 miles** of driving | Inspect again |
| After **every break** | Inspect again |
| Any time it is needed | Re-check as often as necessary to keep the load secure |

*Why:* once the truck starts moving, cargo settles and shifts, and straps or chains can loosen.

Federal, state and local rules about weight, securing cargo, covering loads, and where large vehicles may drive are **different from place to place**. Learn the rules for every area you will drive through.

### 3.2.1 Weight terms you should know (pp. 3-1 to 3-2)
A simple way to remember: **"W" = the actual weight** (what the scale says). **"WR" = a rating** (a value the manufacturer sets).

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **GVW** (Gross Vehicle Weight) | Actual total weight of a single vehicle **plus its load** |
| **GCW** (Gross Combination Weight) | Actual total weight of a **combination** (for example tractor + trailer) **plus the load** |
| **GVWR** (Gross Vehicle Weight Rating) | The **manufacturer's** stated loaded weight for a single vehicle |
| **GCWR** (Gross Combination Weight Rating) | The **manufacturer's** stated loaded weight for a combination (articulated, meaning it bends at a joint) vehicle |
| **Axle weight** | The weight one axle, or one set of axles, puts on the ground |
| **Tire load** | The most weight a tire can safely carry at a stated air pressure; it is printed on the side of each tire |
| **Suspension systems** | Carry a weight capacity rating set by the manufacturer |
| **Coupling device capacity** | Coupling devices (the parts that connect vehicles) are rated for the most weight they can pull and/or carry |

**GCWR with no manufacturer value.** If the manufacturer gives no GCWR, you find it by adding: the **GVWR of the power unit** (the vehicle with the engine) + the **total weight of the towed unit** + **any load on it**.
*Worked example:* power unit GVWR 30,000 lb + trailer weight 15,000 lb + load on the trailer 30,000 lb = GCWR of **75,000 lb**.

### 3.2.2 Legal weight limits (p. 3-2)
- You must stay within legal limits. States set maximums for **GVWR, GCWR and axle weights**.
- Maximum axle weights are often set by a **bridge formula**. A bridge formula allows **less** weight on axles that are **closer together**.
  *Why:* the same weight packed into a short space presses harder on one part of a bridge or road. The formula prevents overloading bridges and roadways.

**What overloading does:**
- Steering, braking and speed control get worse.
- On **upgrades** (uphill), the truck must go very slowly.
- On **downgrades** (downhill), it can gain too much speed. This is worse.
- **Stopping distance increases.**
- **Brakes can fail** when they are forced to work too hard.

**Legal is not always safe.** In **bad weather** or in the **mountains**, driving at the legal maximum weight may not be safe. Think about this before you start driving.

### 3.2.3 Do not be top-heavy (p. 3-2)
How **high the center of gravity** sits has a big effect on how safely the vehicle handles.
- A **high CG** (cargo piled high, or heavy cargo on top) **raises your risk of tipping over**.
- The danger is **greatest in curves**, or when you must **swerve** to avoid a hazard.
- Load the cargo **as low as possible**. The **heaviest** items belong **under the lightest** ones.

*Why:* in a turn, weight that sits high pushes the vehicle sideways over its outside wheels.

### 3.2.4 Balance the weight (p. 3-2)
Poor balance makes handling unsafe. Learn each problem and its result:

| Loading problem | What can happen |
|---|---|
| **Too much** weight on the steering (front) axle | **Hard steering**; damage to the steering axle and tires |
| **Too little** weight on the front axle (weight shifted too far to the rear) | Steering axle is **too light to steer safely** |
| Drive axles carry **too little** weight | **Poor traction**; drive wheels spin easily; in bad weather the truck may get stuck and be unable to keep moving |
| Cargo loaded with a **high CG** | **Greater chance of rollover** |
| High-CG load on a **flatbed** | Load is also more likely to **shift to the side or fall off** |

**Figure 3.1 (p. 3-2), in words:** the "wrong" pictures show cargo stacked tall and bunched at the front of the trailer, or bunched at the back. The "right" picture shows cargo kept low and centered, wide at the bottom and narrower on top. Seen from behind, a load leaning to one side is wrong; a level, centered load is right.

### 3.3.1 Blocking and bracing (p. 3-2)
- **Blocking** is placed against the **front, back and/or sides** of a cargo item so it cannot **slide**. It is shaped to fit tightly against the cargo and is fastened to the cargo deck (floor).
- **Bracing** also stops cargo from moving. Each brace starts at the cargo's **upper part** and runs down to the **floor and/or walls** of the compartment.

Memory hook: **blocking** sits snug against the cargo at deck level (front, back or sides); **bracing** reaches from high on the cargo down to the floor or walls.

### 3.3.2 Cargo tie-down (p. 3-3)
- Cargo on **flatbeds** or trailers with no sides has to be tied down so it cannot shift or fall off.
- Tie-downs can matter in **closed vans** too: cargo that shifts can change how the vehicle handles.
- Tie-downs must be the **right type and strength**.

**Strength rule (working load limit).** The **working load limit (WLL)** is the rated safe working strength of a tie-down. The **aggregate** WLL means all the tie-down ratings added together. Federal rules say the aggregate WLL of the securement system must be **at least ½ (one-half) the weight** of the cargo it holds.
*Worked example:* a 10,000-lb machine needs tie-downs whose WLLs add up to at least **5,000 lb**. Four straps rated 1,500 lb each = 6,000 lb → strong enough.

> **Note:** Some practice-question banks say "1½ times the weight." The handbook says **½ the weight**. Use the handbook answer on the test.

**Equipment.** Use proper tie-down equipment: ropes, straps, chains, and tensioning devices (winches, ratchets, clinching components). Attach tie-downs to the vehicle the right way, with hooks, bolts, rails or rings (Figure 3.2).

**How many tie-downs.** At least **1 tie-down for every 10 feet** of cargo, and **never fewer than 2**, no matter how small the cargo is.

| Cargo length | Minimum tie-downs |
|---|---|
| 4 ft | 2 (the minimum) |
| 10 ft | 2 (the minimum) |
| 20 ft | **2** |
| 25 ft | 3 — the handbook does not say how to count a leftover 5 ft; adding one for it is the safe choice |
| 30 ft | 3 |
| 40 ft | 4 |

**Special cargo.** Heavy pieces of metal have special securing rules; find out what they are before you haul them. Federal rules in **49 CFR §393** set loading and securing rules for: logs and dressed lumber; metal coils and paper rolls; concrete pipe; large boulders; containers (intermodal and roll-on/roll-off); and vehicles (automobiles, heavy vehicles, and flattened or crushed vehicles).

### 3.3.3 Header boards (p. 3-3)
A **front-end header board**, also called a **"headache rack,"** is a strong wall at the front of the cargo area. It **protects you from your cargo** in a crash or emergency stop.
- Make sure it is in good condition.
- It should **block cargo from moving forward**.

*Why:* in a hard stop, cargo keeps moving forward, straight toward the cab where you sit.

### 3.3.4 Covering cargo (p. 3-3)
There are **2 basic reasons** to cover cargo:
1. To **protect people** from spilled cargo.
2. To **protect the cargo** from weather.

Many states require spill protection for safety; learn the laws of each state you drive in.
Check your cargo covers **in your mirrors** now and then while driving. A cover that **flaps** can rip loose. Then the cargo is uncovered, and the cover may block your view or another driver's view.

### 3.3.5 Sealed and containerized loads (p. 3-3)
- **Containerized loads** are usually used when freight travels part of the way by **rail or ship**. A truck carries it at the start and/or end of the trip.
- Certain containers come with **built-in tie-down devices or locks** that fasten directly to a special frame. Others go onto flatbed trailers and must be **secured like any other cargo**.
- **Sealed loads:** you cannot open them to inspect, but you **must check** that you are not over the **gross weight and axle weight limits**.

### 3.3.6 [CA] Spilling loads and damage to the highway (p. 3-4)
- It is **illegal** to drive a vehicle that is covered, built or loaded so badly that any of its load **spills, drops, leaks, blows, sifts** or escapes in any other way.
  **Exceptions:** **clear water** and **feathers from live birds** (CVC §§23114 and 23115).
- Vehicles hauling **garbage, trash, rubbish, ashes** and similar loads must keep the load **covered** so nothing spills onto the road.
- **Aggregate material** (loose material such as sand, gravel or crushed rock):
  - must ride in the cargo area and stay **6 inches below the upper edge** of the cargo area;
  - the cargo area must have **no holes, cracks or openings** the material could escape through;
  - the vehicle needs **fenders**, **seals** on any openings used to unload, and **splash flaps** behind each tire or set of tires.
  - More rules are in CVC §23114. Vehicles hauling wet, waste fruit or vegetable matter, or food-processing-plant waste are **exempt** from these rules.
- **You pay for road damage.** Anyone who damages a street or highway on purpose or through carelessness must pay to repair the road and any damaged sign, signal, guard rail or other facility. This can include the cost of clearing debris.

*Why:* falling rocks and debris break windshields and cause crashes.

### 3.4.1 Dry bulk (p. 3-4)
**Dry bulk tanks** have a **high CG**, and the **load can shift**. Be extremely careful (go slowly) around **curves** and in **sharp turns**.

### 3.4.2 Hanging meat (p. 3-4)
**Hanging meat** (beef, pork or lamb hung from rails in a refrigerated truck) is a **very unstable** load with a **high CG**. Take special care on **sharp curves** such as **on-ramps and off-ramps**. Go slowly.
*Why:* hanging meat can swing toward the outside of a curve and shift the weight suddenly.

### 3.4.3 Livestock (p. 3-4)
- Live animals move around in a trailer, which makes handling unsafe.
- With a **partial load** (less than a full trailer), put in **false bulkheads** (movable inside walls) so the animals stay **bunched together**.
- Even when bunched, the animals can **lean in a curve**; that moves the center of gravity and raises the chance of a **rollover**.

### 3.4.4 Oversized loads (p. 3-4)
Loads that are **over-length, over-width and/or overweight** need **special transit permits** (**[CA]** issued by **Caltrans**, the California Department of Transportation).
- Driving is usually allowed only at **certain times**.
- Special equipment may be needed: **"wide load" signs, flashing lights, flags**, and so on.
- The load may need a **police escort** or **pilot vehicles** (escort cars) with warning signs and/or flashing lights.
- These loads need special driving care.

### 3.4.5 [CA] Special markings for wide loads (p. 3-5)
If a vehicle and its load are **over 80 inches wide**, then on top of the normal vehicle lights, the side of the load that sticks out (the projection) needs:
- an **amber** combination clearance and side-marker lamp at the **front** of the projection, and
- a **red** combination clearance and side-marker lamp at the **rear** of the projection.

(A **clearance lamp** shows how wide a vehicle is; a **side-marker lamp** is on the side. A "combination" lamp does both jobs.)

**Short projection option.** If the wide part is **no more than 3 feet long** (front to rear):
- Projection close to the vehicle's **front** → a minimum of **one amber** combination clearance lamp at the widest point, visible from the **front, side and rear**.
- Projection close to the **rear** → a minimum of **one red** combination side clearance lamp (CVC §25100).

Memory hook: **amber faces forward, red faces back.**

### 3.4.6 [CA] Projecting loads (p. 3-5)

**Load sticking out the back.** If the load extends **4 feet (48 inches) or more** past the rear of the body:
- Put a flag at the very end of the load. It must be **solid red or fluorescent orange** and at least **12 inches square**.
- If you drive in **darkness**, the end of the load also needs **2 lit red lights** that can be seen from **500 feet** to the side and rear (CVC §24604).

**Load sticking out to the left.** If the load extends **1 foot or more** to the left:
- In darkness, the load needs an **amber light** at its far left edge that can be seen from at least **300 feet** ahead and behind.
- If the load extends **more than 120 inches** (10 feet), it needs two lamps — **amber at the front** and **red at the rear** — each of which can be seen from at least **300 feet**.

**Wide vehicle in daylight.** A vehicle **wider than 102 inches** must show a **red or fluorescent flag**, at least **12 inches square**, at the **left front and left rear** (CVC §25104).

| Situation | What you need |
|---|---|
| Load 4 ft or more past rear | Solid red or fluorescent orange flag, at least 12 in square, at end of load |
| Same, in darkness | Also 2 lit red lights, visible 500 ft to side and rear |
| Load 1 ft or more to the left, in darkness | Amber light on far left, visible 300 ft front and rear |
| Load extends more than 120 in | Amber lamp front + red lamp rear, each visible 300 ft |
| Vehicle wider than 102 in, daylight | Red or fluorescent flag, at least 12 in square, left front and left rear |

*Worked example:* after dark you haul pipe that sticks out **5 feet** past the rear. You need the **12-inch flag** at the end of the load **and 2 lit red lights** visible from **500 feet**.
*Why:* other drivers must be able to see a load that sticks out past the normal shape of your vehicle.

### 3.4.7 [CA] Piggyback trailers (p. 3-5)
When a trailer is carried on top of another vehicle ("**piggyback**") on the highway, it must be **tied down securely** so it cannot **shift, tip over, or become unstable**.

## Exam traps
- **Trap:** The shipper loaded and sealed it, so the cargo is not your responsibility → **Correct:** you are responsible whether or not you loaded it. It tempts because someone else did the work. (p. 3-1)
- **Trap:** First re-check after 100 miles (or 25 miles) → **Correct:** within the first **50 miles**. (p. 3-1)
- **Trap:** Re-check every 2 hours or 100 miles → **Correct:** every **3 hours or 150 miles**, and after **every break**. (p. 3-1)
- **Trap:** GVWR is what the loaded truck weighs today → **Correct:** that is **GVW**. GVWR is the manufacturer's value. The letters look almost the same. (p. 3-1)
- **Trap:** A bridge formula allows more weight on axles that are close together → **Correct:** **less** weight. Close axles concentrate the weight on one spot. (p. 3-2)
- **Trap:** If the weight is legal, it is safe → **Correct:** in bad weather or mountains, legal maximum weight may not be safe. (p. 3-2)
- **Trap:** Put heavy items on top to hold lighter items down → **Correct:** heaviest **under** the lightest, as low as possible. (p. 3-2)
- **Trap:** Too little weight on the front axle causes hard steering → **Correct:** **too much** front weight = hard steering; **too little** = too light to steer safely. The two are easy to mix up. (p. 3-2)
- **Trap:** Too little weight on the drive axles causes hard steering → **Correct:** it causes **poor traction** (wheels spin). (p. 3-2)
- **Trap:** Bracing is fastened snug against the cargo at deck level → **Correct:** that is **blocking**. Bracing reaches down from high on the cargo to the floor and/or walls. (p. 3-2)
- **Trap:** A 20-foot load needs 3 tie-downs (one each end plus middle) → **Correct:** **2** (1 per 10 ft, minimum 2). (p. 3-3)
- **Trap:** A very small load needs just 1 tie-down → **Correct:** **at least 2**, no matter how small. (p. 3-3)
- **Trap:** Aggregate WLL must be 1½ times the cargo weight → **Correct:** at least **½** the weight (handbook). (p. 3-3)
- **Trap:** Header boards protect the cargo → **Correct:** they protect **you** from the cargo in a crash or emergency stop. (p. 3-3)
- **Trap:** You cannot check anything on a sealed load → **Correct:** check that you are within **gross weight and axle weight** limits. (p. 3-3)
- **Trap:** Hanging meat is stable because it is fastened to rails → **Correct:** it is **very unstable** with a **high CG**. (p. 3-4)
- **Trap:** With a partial livestock load, spread the animals out → **Correct:** use **false bulkheads** to keep them **bunched together**. (p. 3-4)
- **Trap:** Rear flag needed at 3 feet of overhang, or flag must be 18 inches → **Correct:** **4 feet** or more; flag at least **12 inches** square. (p. 3-5)
- **Trap:** Night lights on a rear overhang must be visible 300 feet → **Correct:** **500 feet** for the 2 red rear lights; 300 feet is the left-side amber light. (p. 3-5)

## Handbook review questions — answered
**TYK Section 3 #1** Name the four cargo-related things a driver is responsible for.
→ (1) Inspecting the cargo; (2) recognizing overloads and poorly balanced weight; (3) knowing the cargo is properly secured and does not block your view ahead or to the sides; (4) knowing the cargo does not block your access to emergency equipment. This is true even if you did not load it yourself. (p. 3-1)

**TYK Section 3 #2** During a trip, how often do you need to stop and check your cargo?
→ Inspect the cargo and securing devices within the first **50 miles**, then again after every **3 hours or 150 miles** of driving and after **every break**, and as often as needed to keep the load secure. (p. 3-1)

**TYK Section 3 #3** What is the difference between gross combination weight rating (GCWR) and gross combination weight (GCW)?
→ **GCW** is the actual total weight of the combination vehicle including its load. **GCWR** is the value the **manufacturer** sets as the loaded weight of the combination. (If there is no maker's value, GCWR = power unit's GVWR + weight of the towed unit + its load.) (p. 3-1)

**TYK Section 3 #4** Give 2 situations where driving at the legal maximum weight may not be safe.
→ **Bad weather** and **mountains**. (p. 3-2)

**TYK Section 3 #5** What can happen when the front axle does not carry enough weight?
→ The steering axle becomes **too light to steer safely** (this happens when weight is shifted too far to the rear). (p. 3-2)

**TYK Section 3 #6** What is the smallest number of tie-downs any flatbed load may have?
→ **2.** Use at least 1 tie-down per 10 feet of cargo, but never fewer than 2, however small the cargo. (p. 3-3)

**TYK Section 3 #7** What is the smallest number of tie-downs for a load 20 feet long?
→ **2** (20 ft ÷ 10 ft = 2, which also meets the 2-tie-down minimum). (p. 3-3)

**TYK Section 3 #8** What are the 2 basic reasons to cover cargo on an open bed?
→ To **protect people from spilled cargo** and to **protect the cargo from weather**. (p. 3-3)

**TYK Section 3 #9** Before hauling a sealed load, what must you check?
→ That you will **not exceed the gross weight and axle weight limits** (you cannot inspect the cargo inside). (p. 3-3)

## Practice test (real-exam style)
1. Whether or not you loaded the cargo yourself, you are responsible for all of these EXCEPT:
   a) Knowing the cargo does not block your view to the sides
   b) Recognizing overloads and poorly balanced weight

## One-minute recap
- You are responsible for the cargo even if you did not load it: inspect it, spot overloads and poor balance, make sure it is secured, and make sure it does not block your view or your emergency equipment.
- Check cargo within the first **50 miles**, then every **3 hours or 150 miles** and after **every break**.
- "W" = actual weight (GVW, GCW); "WR" = manufacturer's rating (GVWR, GCWR). Bridge formula = **less** weight on axles that are **closer together**. Legal max weight may be unsafe in **bad weather** or **mountains**.
- Keep the center of gravity low: heaviest cargo **under** the lightest. Too much front weight = hard steering; too little = can't steer safely; too little on drive axles = poor traction.
- Tie-downs: at least **1 per 10 ft**, **never fewer than 2**; combined working load limit at least **½** the cargo weight. Header board protects **you**.
- Cover cargo to protect **people** from spills and **cargo** from weather. Sealed load: check **gross and axle weights**.
- Dry bulk and hanging meat have a **high CG**: go slowly on curves and ramps. Livestock: **false bulkheads**. Oversized: permits (**Caltrans** in CA), set hours, signs, escorts.
- **[CA]** Nothing may escape except clear water and live-bird feathers; aggregate **6 in** below the edge; rear overhang **4 ft+** = **12-in** red/orange flag, and at night **2 red lights** seen from **500 ft**.
