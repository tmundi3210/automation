# GK-13 · Accidents, fires, alcohol and drugs, hazmat rules for all drivers

> **Handbook:** Section 2.20–2.23, pages 2-44–2-49 · **Test:** General Knowledge · **Exam weight:** High · **Study time:** ~45 min

## What you'll be able to answer
- The five basic steps at any accident scene, what to do first, and what information to collect.
- The main causes of vehicle fires (including tire fires) and how to prevent them.
- How to fight a truck fire: where to stop, engine vs. cargo fires, where to stand, where to aim, and which extinguisher works on which fire.
- How alcohol works in the body, the CMV blood alcohol limit, the 4-hour rule, and why coffee doesn't sober you up.
- Which drugs and medicines are a problem, and what to do if you get sick while driving.
- What a HazMat placard is, why it is used, how many are needed and how big they are, and when you need an "H" endorsement.

## Learn it

### 2.20 Accident procedures (p. 2-44)
When you are in a crash but **not badly hurt**, act to prevent more damage and injuries. The **5 basic steps** at any accident:
1. **Protect** the area.
2. **Notify** the authorities.
3. **Care** for the injured.
4. **Collect** the required information.
5. **Report** the accident.

Think of it in time order: make the scene safe → get help coming → help people → do the paperwork (collect, then report).

### 2.20.1 Protect the area (p. 2-44)
**The first job at any accident scene is to stop a second accident from happening in the same place.**
- **Your truck is involved:** try to move it to the **side of the road**. Why: it prevents another crash and lets traffic keep moving.
- **You stopped to help at someone else's crash:** park **away from** the wreck. Why: emergency vehicles need the space right around the crash.
- Switch on your **4-way emergency flashers**.
- Place **reflective triangles** so other drivers see them **in time** to avoid the crash. (Where to place triangles is in GK-06, section 2.5.)

### 2.20.2 Notify authorities (p. 2-44)
- **Have a cell phone or CB radio?** Call for help **before you get out** of your truck.
- **No phone or radio?** First **protect the scene**, then phone the police or send someone to phone.
- Figure out **exactly where you are**, so you can tell them the precise location.

### 2.20.3 Care for the injured (p. 2-44)
- Someone **trained** (a medic, for example) is already treating people? **Let them work** — help only if they ask.
- If no one qualified is there, do what you can:
  - **Do not move a badly injured person** unless there is danger from **fire** or **passing traffic**. Why: moving them can make injuries worse.
  - Control **heavy bleeding** with **direct pressure** on the wound.
  - Keep hurt people **warm**.

### 2.20.4 Gather information (p. 2-44)
If you were in the accident, you must file an **accident report**. Collect:
- Each driver's **name, address, and driver license (DL) number**.
- **License plate numbers** and **types** of the vehicles.
- For any other vehicle whose **owner is not the driver**: the **owner's name and address**.
- A **description of the damage** to other vehicles or property.
- **Names and addresses** of anyone **injured or involved**.
- The investigating peace officer's **name, badge number, and agency**.
- **Names and addresses of witnesses**.
- The **exact location** of the accident.
- The **direction of travel** of each vehicle.

**[CA]** The California SR 1 report to DMV (within 10 days if there is injury, death, or damage over $1,000) is taught in GK-03 (p. 1-26).

### 2.21 Fires (p. 2-44)
This section covers what starts truck fires, how to keep them from starting, and how to fight one.

### 2.21.1 Causes of fire (p. 2-44)
| Source | Causes |
|---|---|
| **After accidents** | **Spilled fuel**; **improper use of flares** |
| **Tires** | **Under-inflated tires**; **dual tires that touch** each other |
| **Electrical system** | **Short circuits** from damaged insulation; **loose connections** |
| **Fuel** | **Driver smoking**; **improper fueling**; **loose fuel connections** |
| **Cargo** | **Flammable cargo**; cargo **not properly sealed or loaded**; **poor ventilation** |

Why tires burn: a soft (under-inflated) tire or two duals rubbing together build up heat through friction and flexing.

### 2.21.2 Fire prevention (p. 2-44 to 2-45)
- **Vehicle inspection (pre-trip):** fully inspect the **electrical, fuel, and exhaust systems**, plus the **tires and cargo**. Make sure the **fire extinguisher is charged**.
- **En route inspection:** every time you stop on a trip, look over the **tires, wheels, and truck body** for any **sign of heat**.
- **Follow safe procedures** for fueling, using brakes, handling flares, and anything else that can start a fire.
- **Monitoring:** check **instruments and gauges** often for overheating, and use your **mirrors** to look for **smoke** from tires or the vehicle.
- **Caution:** be careful with anything **flammable**.

### 2.21.3 Fire fighting (p. 2-45)
Untrained drivers have turned small fires into **bigger ones**. **Learn how your extinguisher works now — read the instructions printed on it before you ever need it.**

**Step 1 — Pull off the road and stop.**
- Choose an **open spot** with nothing nearby that could catch fire — no **buildings, trees, brush, or other vehicles**.
- **Never pull into a service station!** Why: a gas station is full of fuel that can catch fire and explode.
- **Tell emergency services** what is wrong and where you are.

**Step 2 — Keep the fire from spreading** (before you try to put it out).
- **Engine fire:** **shut the engine off** right away. **Avoid opening the hood** if at all possible — opening it feeds the fire fresh air. Spray the extinguisher (the handbook says foam) **through the louvers, the radiator, or from under** the vehicle.
- **Cargo fire inside a van or box trailer:** leave the **doors shut**, above all when the cargo is HazMat. Why: opening the doors gives the fire **oxygen**, and it can burn very fast.

**Step 3 — Put out the fire.**
- Keep the **greatest distance** you can between you and the fire.
- Point the nozzle low, at the **source or base** — what is actually burning — **not at the flames** above it.
- Put the wind at your back (**stand upwind**) so it pushes the spray toward the fire.
- Don't stop until the burning material has **cooled** down. A fire with **no smoke or flame can still flare up again**.
- **[CA]** The handbook points to California Code of Regulations (CCR) Title 13 §1242 for more on this.

**Use the right extinguisher**
- **B:C type** → **electrical fires** and **burning liquids**.
- **A:B:C type** → electrical and liquids **plus burning wood, paper, and cloth**.
- **Water** → OK on **wood, paper, or cloth**. **Never on an electrical fire** (you can get a **shock**) or a **gasoline fire** (it **spreads** the flames).
- **Burning tire** → it must be **cooled**; it may take **a lot of water**.
- **Unsure which extinguisher is right** — above all with a **HazMat** fire? Then **wait for firefighters.**

**Classes of fire (Figure 2.20)**
| Class | What is burning | How it is put out |
|---|---|---|
| **A** | Wood, paper, ordinary materials that burn | Cooling and soaking — water or dry chemical |
| **B** | Gasoline, oil, grease, other greasy liquids | Smothering, cooling or heat shielding — carbon dioxide or dry chemical |
| **C** | Electrical equipment | Agents that don't carry electricity — carbon dioxide or dry chemical. **No water.** |
| **D** | Metals that burn | Special extinguishing powders |

**Extinguisher types grouped by class (from Figure 2.21)**
- **B or C:** regular dry chemical, KCL dry chemical, carbon dioxide (dry), halogenated agent (gas).
- **A only:** water; water with antifreeze. **A or B:** water in a "loaded stream." **Foam:** B, and some A.
- **D:** dry powder special compound; the handbook's chart also lists Purple K dry chemical as D.
- **Multi-purpose dry chemical:** listed for A, B, C, or D — the widest range.

Note: the handbook's Figure 2.20 misprints the first class as "Z"; from its contents (wood, paper) it is **Class A**. Beyond the handbook — not on the test: Purple K is usually rated for B and C fires; for the test, use the handbook chart.

### 2.22 Alcohol, other drugs, and driving (p. 2-46)

### 2.22.1 Alcohol and driving (p. 2-46 to 2-47)
Alcohol and driving are a deadly mix: crashes involving drinkers kill **more than 20,000 people every year**. Alcohol harms **reaction time, muscle coordination, depth perception, and night vision**. It also dulls the brain areas in charge of **judgment and inhibition** (self-restraint). For some people, **just 1 drink** causes signs of impairment.

**The on-duty rules**
- **Never drink while on duty.**
- **Do not drink any intoxicating beverage — no matter how little alcohol it has — within 4 hours before going on duty.**

**BAC (blood alcohol concentration)** = how much alcohol is in your blood, measured in **milligrams of alcohol per 100 milliliters of blood**. A BAC of **.10** means **1/10 of 1%** (1/1000) of your blood is alcohol.

**The CMV limit [CA]**
- It is **illegal to drive a CMV with a BAC of 0.04% or more.**
- Doing so brings an **immediate administrative license action ("Admin Per Se")** under CVC §13353.2, and you may also be **convicted of DUI** (driving under the influence) under CVC §23152(d).
- **A BAC below 0.04% does NOT mean it is safe or legal to drive.** (Even a detectable amount under .04 puts you out of service for 24 hours — see GK-02, p. 1-14.)
- Note: Figure 2.23 labels **.08** the "drunk driving limit." Do not confuse it with the CMV rule: **for a CMV the limit is .04.** (Beyond the handbook — not on the test: .08 is the general limit for adult drivers of ordinary cars.)

**How alcohol works**
- Alcohol goes **straight into the bloodstream** and travels to the **brain**.
- After the brain, a **small amount** leaves through **urine, sweat, and breath**; the **rest goes to the liver**.
- The liver can process only about **1/3 ounce of alcohol per hour** — much **less** than the alcohol in one standard drink.
- This rate **never changes**. Coffee and cold showers do nothing — **only waiting (time) lowers your BAC**.
- If you drink faster than your body can remove it, alcohol builds up and your driving gets worse.
- Note: the Figure 2.23 box rounds this to "about 1 drink per hour," and the Figure 2.22 chart says to **subtract .01% for each 40 minutes** of drinking. Either way, the lesson is the same: **only time lowers your BAC**.

**What is "one drink"?** Each of these has the **same amount of alcohol**:
| Drink | Size |
|---|---|
| Beer (5% alcohol) | **12 oz** |
| Wine (12% alcohol) | **5 oz** |
| 80-proof liquor | **1½ oz** shot |

Beer, wine, or liquor — your body only "sees" the **alcohol**, so one standard serving of each affects you the same.

**What sets your BAC — 3 things**
1. **How much** you drink — more alcohol → higher BAC.
2. **How fast** you drink — faster → higher BAC.
3. **Your weight** — a smaller person reaches the same BAC with fewer drinks.

**Worked examples (from Figure 2.22, approximate):**
- A **100-lb** person after just **1 drink** is at about **.04** — already at the CMV limit.
- A **180-lb** person after **2 drinks** is at about **.04**.
- A **160-lb** person has **3 drinks** → about **.07**. If 2 hours have passed since the drinking started: 120 minutes ÷ 40 = 3, so subtract .03 → about **.04**. Still at the CMV limit two hours later.
- The chart's key sums it up: **0 drinks is the "only safe driving limit,"** and **impairment begins with 1 drink**.

**How rising BAC affects you (Figure 2.23, simplified)**
| BAC | Body | Driving |
|---|---|---|
| .02 | Mellow, slightly warm | Less inhibited |
| .05 | Clearly relaxed | Less alert; coordination starts to suffer |
| .08 | Definite impairment of coordination and judgment | The chart's "drunk driving limit" (the CMV limit is lower: .04) |
| .10 | Loud, mood swings, embarrassing behavior | Slower reaction time |
| .15 | Poor balance, clearly drunk | Unable to drive |
| .30–.50 | .30: many pass out · .40: most pass out and some die · .50: breathing stops; many die | — |

**Alcohol and the brain**
- The higher the BAC, the more of the brain is affected.
- The **first** part affected controls **judgment and self-control**. Why this is dangerous: drinkers **can't tell they are getting drunk** — and safe driving needs good judgment.
- Next, **muscle control, vision, and coordination** get worse.
- Driving signs: **straddling lanes**, **quick, jerky starts**, **failing to signal or use lights**, **running red lights and stop signs**, **passing improperly**.
- Drinking drivers are much more likely to crash and to lose their license.

**How alcohol affects driving**
**Every** driver is affected. Alcohol harms **judgment, vision, coordination, and reaction time**, causing serious mistakes such as:
- Reacting to hazards more slowly
- A speed that is **too fast or too slow**
- **Weaving**
- **Running over the curb**
- Drifting into the **wrong lane**

### 2.22.2 Other drugs (p. 2-47)
- Laws ban having or using many drugs **on duty**.
- It is illegal to be **under the influence** of:
  - any **"controlled substance"** (a drug regulated by law),
  - **amphetamines** — street names **"pep pills," "uppers," "bennies"**,
  - **narcotics**,
  - **any other substance** that can make you an unsafe driver.
- That can include **prescription and over-the-counter (OTC) drugs**, such as **cold medicines**, that make you **drowsy** or affect driving.
- **Exception:** you **may** use a drug your **doctor** gave you **if the doctor tells you it will not affect safe driving**.
- Read **warning labels** and follow your **doctor's orders**. Avoid **illegal drugs** completely.
- **Never take a drug to cover up tiredness. Rest is the only thing that fixes fatigue.**
- **Alcohol makes other drugs much stronger.** The safest rule: **don't mix drugs and driving at all.**
- Drug use can cause crashes (death, injury, damage) and lead to **arrest, fines, jail**, and the **end of your driving career**.

### 2.22.3 Illness (p. 2-47)
- **Too sick to drive safely? Then don't drive.**
- **Emergency exception:** drive only as far as the **closest place where it is safe to stop**.

### 2.23 Hazardous materials rules for all commercial drivers (p. 2-48)
Every CMV driver — not only HazMat drivers — must be able to **spot hazardous cargo** and decide whether their license lets them haul it **without an "H" (HazMat) endorsement**.

**TSA background check for the H endorsement**
- Anyone applying for an **original or renewal "H"** endorsement must pass a **Transportation Security Administration (TSA)** federal **security threat assessment** (a background records check).
- You **start** it **after** you: apply for your CDL at DMV, **pass all required knowledge tests**, and turn in a **valid medical form**.
- You give a TSA agent your **fingerprints, a fee**, and any other required information, plus a **copy of your CLP** (commercial learner's permit) and **one ID**:
  - a California DL or ID card,
  - an out-of-state DL, or
  - your CLP with a DMV photo receipt.
- TSA agent locations: universalenroll.dhs.gov.

### 2.23.1 What are hazardous materials? (p. 2-48)
**HazMat** = anything that could **harm people's health or safety, or damage property**, while it is being shipped.

**Hazard classes (Figure 2.24)**
| Class | Name | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Explosives | Ammunition, dynamite, fireworks |
| 2 | Gases | Propane, oxygen, helium |
| 3 | Flammable (liquids) | Gasoline, acetone |
| 4 | Flammable solids | Matches, fuses |
| 5 | Oxidizers | Ammonium nitrate, hydrogen peroxide |
| 6 | Poisons | Pesticides, arsenic |
| 7 | Radioactive | Uranium, plutonium |
| 8 | Corrosives | Hydrochloric acid, battery fluid |
| 9 | Miscellaneous hazardous materials | Formaldehyde, asbestos |
| No number | ORM-D (Other Regulated Material–Domestic) | Hair spray, charcoal |
| No number | Combustible liquids | Fuel oils, lighter fluid |

### 2.23.2 Why are there rules? (p. 2-48)
The HazMat rules have **3 goals**:
1. **Contain the product.**
2. **Communicate the risk.**
3. **Ensure safe drivers and equipment.**

**Contain the product** — some of these products can **hurt or kill a person who touches them**. So the rules tell **shippers** how to **package** them safely, and tell **drivers** how to **load, haul, and unload bulk tanks**. These are the **containment rules**.

**Communicate the risk** — the **shipper** warns dockworkers and drivers with a **shipping paper** and **diamond-shaped hazard labels**.
- Why it matters: after a crash or spill, **you may be hurt and unable to speak**. Firefighters and police can limit damage and injuries if they **know what HazMat is on board** — lives may depend on finding the papers fast.
- So HazMat shipping papers must be **marked (identified)** or kept **on top of** other shipping papers.
- Keep the shipping papers:
  - in a **pouch on the driver's door**, or
  - in **clear view, within reach**, while driving, or
  - on the **driver's seat** when you are **out of the vehicle**.

**Ensure safe drivers and equipment** — this is why drivers of placarded vehicles must be trained and hold the **H endorsement** (next section).

### 2.23.3 Placards, ID numbers, and who may drive (p. 2-48 to 2-49)
**Placards** are **warning signs mounted outside the vehicle**; each shows which **hazard class** the cargo belongs to. Their purpose: **to warn others** that HazMat is on board.
- A placarded vehicle needs **at least 4 identical placards**: **front, rear, and both sides**.
- Someone looking from **any of the 4 directions** must be able to read one.
- Size and shape: **at least 9.84 inches (250 mm) square**, turned **upright on a point** — a **diamond**.
- Note: some older manuals and websites say 10¾ inches. The test follows this handbook: **9.84 in (250 mm)**.
- **Cargo tanks and other bulk packaging** carry the **ID number** of what is inside them, shown on **placards or orange panels** (**[CA]** CVC §27903).

**ID numbers**
- **4-digit numbers** that let **first responders** (police, firefighters, medics) look up what is in the load.
- The same number can cover **several different chemicals** on shipping papers.
- It comes after the letters **"UN" or "NA."**
- The chemicals and the ID numbers assigned to them are listed in the U.S. DOT **Emergency Response Guidebook (ERG)**.

**Do you need an "H" endorsement? (p. 2-49)**
- **Not every** vehicle carrying HazMat needs placards. The placard rules are in Section 9.
- **Placards NOT required → you may drive it without an "H."**
- **Placards required → you may NOT drive it unless you have an "H"** endorsement. To get it, you pass a knowledge test on Section 9.
- Drivers of placarded vehicles must learn how to **load and transport** hazardous products safely.
- **Don't know if your load needs placards? Ask your employer.**
- **Driving a vehicle that needs placards without an "H" is a crime.** If stopped, you will be **cited** and **not allowed to keep driving** your truck — costing time and money.
- **Failing to placard** when required can cost lives in a crash: **emergency crews won't know** about the hazardous cargo.
- H drivers must also know **which products can and cannot be loaded together** (Section 9). Before loading more than one type of product, be sure it is safe; if unsure, **ask your employer and check the regulations**.

**Tank vehicles**
- A tank endorsement is needed for a CMV carrying **liquid or gas** in one or more tanks when **each tank holds more than 119 gallons** (individual rated capacity) and **all the tanks together hold 1,000 gallons or more** (aggregate rated capacity), whether the tanks are attached permanently or temporarily.
- **Not a tank vehicle:** an **empty storage tank** that is not built for transport, rated at 1,000 gallons or more, and only **temporarily fastened to a flatbed trailer** (49 CFR §383.5).
- The tank ("N") endorsement is **not needed** for vehicles that **don't require a CDL**.
- Note: page 2-49 names this the "X" endorsement, but Section 1 of the handbook calls the plain tank endorsement **"N"** (p. 1-4) and uses **"X"** for a **tank carrying placarded HazMat** (tank + HazMat, p. 1-5). On the test: tank only → **N**; tank with placarded HazMat → **X**.

## Exam traps
- **Trap:** The first thing to do at a crash is help the injured. → **Correct:** first **protect the area** so no second crash happens. Helping people feels most urgent, but a second crash can hurt everyone. (p. 2-44)
- **Trap:** Park right next to the crash to help. → **Correct:** park **away** from it; emergency vehicles need that space. (p. 2-44)
- **Trap:** Move injured people off the road right away. → **Correct:** don't move a badly injured person unless **fire or traffic** makes it necessary. (p. 2-44)
- **Trap:** Drive to a service station for help with a fire. → **Correct:** **never** — stop in an open area away from anything that can burn. (p. 2-45)
- **Trap:** Open the hood to get at an engine fire. → **Correct:** **don't open the hood**; it feeds the fire air. (p. 2-45)
- **Trap:** Open the trailer doors to find a cargo fire. → **Correct:** **keep the doors shut** — oxygen makes it burn fast. (p. 2-45)
- **Trap:** Get close and aim at the flames. → **Correct:** stay **as far away as possible**, **upwind**, and aim at the **base**. (p. 2-45)
- **Trap:** The fire is out once you see no smoke or flame. → **Correct:** keep going until it has **cooled**; it can restart. (p. 2-45)
- **Trap:** A B:C extinguisher works on everything. → **Correct:** it is for **electrical and liquid** fires, not wood, paper, cloth — that needs A:B:C (or water). (p. 2-45)
- **Trap:** Water works on any fire. → **Correct:** **never** on electrical (shock) or gasoline (spreads) fires. (p. 2-45)
- **Trap:** Coffee, fresh air, or a cold shower sobers you up. → **Correct:** **only time** — the liver works at a fixed rate. (p. 2-47)
- **Trap:** Beer is "weaker" than liquor. → **Correct:** a 12-oz beer, 5-oz wine, and 1½-oz shot contain the **same** alcohol. (p. 2-47)
- **Trap:** The CMV limit is .08. → **Correct:** **0.04%**; .08 is the general "drunk driving limit" printed on the Figure 2.23 chart, not the CMV limit. And below .04 is **not** automatically safe or legal. (p. 2-46, 2-47)
- **Trap:** No drinking within 8 (or 2) hours of duty. → **Correct:** **4 hours**, any amount of alcohol. (p. 2-46)
- **Trap:** Over-the-counter medicine is always fine. → **Correct:** cold medicines can make you **drowsy**; a doctor's drug is OK only if the doctor says it won't affect driving. (p. 2-47)
- **Trap:** Pep pills help on long trips. → **Correct:** never use drugs that hide fatigue; only **rest** cures it. (p. 2-47)
- **Trap:** Any HazMat load needs an H endorsement. → **Correct:** only loads that **require placards**. (p. 2-49)
- **Trap:** Placards are 10¾ inches. → **Correct:** handbook says **at least 9.84 in (250 mm)**. (p. 2-48)
- **Trap:** Two placards (front and rear) are enough. → **Correct:** **at least 4 identical** — front, rear, and both sides. (p. 2-48)
- **Trap:** Keep HazMat papers in the glove box or under other papers. → **Correct:** clearly identified or kept **on top**; in the door pouch or in clear view within reach; on the **driver's seat** when you leave. (p. 2-48)

## Handbook review questions — answered
**TYK "SUBSECTIONS 2.20 AND 2.21" #1** At an accident scene, what can you do to keep another accident from happening?
→ If your own vehicle was in the crash, try to get it off to the side of the road; if you stop to assist, park away from the wreck so emergency vehicles have room; switch on your 4-way emergency flashers; and place reflective triangles so approaching drivers notice them early enough to steer clear. (p. 2-44)

**TYK "SUBSECTIONS 2.20 AND 2.21" #2** Give two causes of tire fires.
→ Under-inflated tires, and dual tires that touch each other. (p. 2-44)

**TYK "SUBSECTIONS 2.20 AND 2.21" #3** Which fires is a B:C extinguisher NOT made for?
→ Fires in ordinary materials (Class A) — burning **wood, paper, and cloth**. A B:C unit is designed for electrical fires and burning liquids; for wood, paper, or cloth you need an A:B:C unit (or water). The handbook charts also show that burning metals (Class D) need special powders. (p. 2-45)

**TYK "SUBSECTIONS 2.20 AND 2.21" #4** Should you get as close as you can to the fire when using an extinguisher?
→ **No.** Keep as much distance from the fire as you can, stand upwind so the wind carries the extinguishing agent to the fire, and point the nozzle at the base or source of the fire (what is actually burning), not up into the flames. (p. 2-45)

**TYK "SUBSECTIONS 2.20 AND 2.21" #5** List several things that can start a vehicle fire.
→ After accidents: spilled fuel, improper use of flares. Tires: under-inflation, duals touching. Electrical system: short circuits from damaged insulation, loose connections. Fuel: driver smoking, improper fueling, loose fuel connections. Cargo: flammable cargo, cargo improperly sealed or loaded, poor ventilation. (p. 2-44)

**TYK "SUBSECTIONS 2.22 AND 2.23" #1** True or false: ordinary cold remedies can cause drowsiness.
→ **True.** Prescription and over-the-counter drugs, including cold medicines, can make a driver drowsy or otherwise affect safe driving. Read warning labels and follow doctor's orders. (p. 2-47)

**TYK "SUBSECTIONS 2.22 AND 2.23" #2** True or false: a drinker can sober up faster with coffee and a bit of fresh air.
→ **False.** The liver removes alcohol at a fixed rate (about 1/3 ounce per hour), so only time sobers a person up — not coffee, fresh air, or a cold shower. (p. 2-47)

**TYK "SUBSECTIONS 2.22 AND 2.23" #3** Explain what a HazMat placard is.
→ A diamond-shaped warning sign, at least 9.84 inches (250 mm) square and turned upright on a point, placed on the outside of a vehicle to show the hazard class of the cargo. A placarded vehicle needs at least 4 identical placards — front, rear, and both sides — readable from all 4 directions. (p. 2-48)

**TYK "SUBSECTIONS 2.22 AND 2.23" #4** What is the purpose of placards?
→ To warn others that the vehicle carries hazardous materials and what kind of hazard it is. If there is a crash and the driver cannot speak, emergency crews can see the danger and act safely; without placards, they would not know about the hazardous cargo. (p. 2-48, 2-49)

## One-minute recap
- Accident: **Protect** (move rig aside, park away if helping, flashers, triangles) → **Notify** (call before getting out if you have a phone/CB) → **Care** (don't move the badly injured, direct pressure, keep warm) → **Collect** → **Report**.
- Fire causes: after accidents, **tires (under-inflated, duals touching)**, electrical, fuel, cargo. Check tires, wheels, body for heat at every stop; keep the extinguisher charged.
- Truck fire: open area, **never a service station**; engine fire → engine off, **hood closed**; van cargo fire → **doors shut**.
- Extinguisher: **far away, upwind, aim at the base**, keep going until cooled. **B:C** = electrical + liquids; **A:B:C** adds wood, paper, cloth; **no water** on electrical or gasoline fires.
- Alcohol: CMV limit **0.04%** [CA]; **no drinking within 4 hours** of duty; one drink = 12 oz beer = 5 oz wine = 1½ oz liquor; **only time sobers you** (liver ≈ 1/3 oz per hour).
- Drugs: cold medicine can cause drowsiness; doctor's drug OK only if doctor says it's safe; no drugs to hide fatigue — **rest** is the only cure. Too ill → don't drive (emergency: nearest safe stop).
- HazMat: placards warn others of the hazard class; **4 identical**, **9.84 in (250 mm)** diamonds; ID numbers are 4 digits after UN/NA. **H endorsement only if placards are required**; papers on top, within reach, or on the seat when you leave.
