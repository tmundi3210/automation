# CV-01 · Driving combination vehicles safely

> **Handbook:** Section 6.1, pages 6-1–6-4 · **Test:** Combination Vehicles · **Exam weight:** High · **Study time:** ~35 min

## What you'll be able to answer
- Why a loaded rig rolls over, and the two things that prevent a rollover.
- What "crack-the-whip" and "rearward amplification" mean, and which trailer is most likely to tip over.
- Why an empty rig or a bobtail tractor needs MORE distance to stop than a fully loaded rig.
- Which trailers get stuck on railroad crossings, and what to do if you are stuck on the tracks.
- How to spot and stop a trailer skid, and why the trailer hand valve is the wrong tool for it.
- What off-tracking is, how to make a tight turn, and which way to turn the wheel when backing a trailer.

## Learn it

**Key words**
- **Combination vehicle** (also called a **rig**): a power unit pulling one or more trailers. Examples: tractor-semitrailer, doubles, triples, a straight truck pulling a trailer. (p. 6-1)
- **Tractor**: the power unit that pulls a semitrailer. **Bobtail**: a tractor driving with no semitrailer attached. (p. 6-2)
- **Jackknife**: the rig folds at the hitch point, so the trailer and tractor form a "V" instead of a straight line.

Combination vehicles are usually heavier and longer than single vehicles, and they take more skill to drive. This module covers the safety points that apply only to combinations. (p. 6-1)

### 6.1.1 Rollover risks (p. 6-1)
- Rollovers cause **more than half** of the deaths of truck drivers in crashes.
- **Center of gravity** = the balance point of all the weight. The higher you stack cargo, the higher this point sits above the road, and the easier the truck tips over.
- A **fully loaded** rig is **10 times** more likely to roll over in a crash than an **empty** rig.

**Two things prevent a rollover:**
1. Keep the cargo **as low as possible** (close to the road).
2. Drive **slowly around turns**.

More points:
- Keeping cargo low matters **even more** in a combination than in a straight truck.
- Keep the load **centered** and spread out as much as you can. If the load sits to one side, the trailer leans, and a rollover becomes more likely. (Loading rules are in Section 3 — see GK-14.)
- Rollovers happen when you turn **too fast**. Slow down on corners, on-ramps and off-ramps.
- Do not make quick lane changes, especially with a full load.

> Handbook vs other sources: a few practice websites say "5 times". The handbook says **10 times** — use 10 on the test.

### 6.1.2 Steer gently (p. 6-1; Figure 6.1 on p. 6-2)
**Crack-the-whip effect:** when you change lanes quickly, the trailer swings out much harder than the tractor — like the tip of a whip. It can flip the trailer over. In many crashes, **only the trailer** turned over.

**Rearward amplification** is what causes crack-the-whip. "Rearward" means toward the back. "Amplification" means getting bigger. The sideways swing grows as it moves back through each unit, so the last trailer swings the most.

Figure 6.1 ranks eight rig types, from the least whip (top) to the most (bottom). How to read the numbers:

| Rearward amplification | What it means |
|---|---|
| **1.0** | 5-axle tractor-semitrailer with a 45-ft trailer — the lowest value in the chart (shown at the top) |
| **2.0** | The last trailer tips over **2 times** as easily as the tractor. (In the chart, the 65-ft conventional double with 27-ft trailers sits at 2.0.) |
| **3.5** | Triples with 27-ft trailers — the last trailer is **3.5 times** as easy to roll over as a 5-axle tractor. The highest in the chart. |

So: triples show the strongest whip in the chart, and the **last trailer** is the one most likely to turn over.

**[CA]** Triple trailers are not legal in California (p. 1-5). The 3.5 number can still appear on the test.

**How to steer gently**
- Turn the wheel smoothly. A sudden jerk of the wheel can tip the trailer.
- Keep a long following distance: leave at least **1 second for every 10 feet** of your rig's length, and add **1 more second when you drive over 40 mph**. Why: more space means you will not be forced into a sudden lane change.
- Look far down the road so nothing surprises you.
- At night, drive slowly enough that your headlights show a hazard while there is still time to change lanes or stop gently.
- Slow to a safe speed **before** you enter a turn, not in the middle of it.

*Worked example — following distance*
- 70-ft rig at 35 mph: 70 ÷ 10 = **7 seconds** (no extra second — not over 40 mph).
- Same rig at 55 mph: 7 + 1 = **8 seconds**.
- 50-ft rig at 60 mph: 5 + 1 = **6 seconds**.

### 6.1.3 Brake early (p. 6-2)
- Control your speed whether you are loaded or empty.
- An **empty** combination takes **longer** to stop than a fully loaded one. Why: with little weight on them, the tires cannot grip well (**poor traction**) — the suspension springs are very stiff and the brakes are strong, so the wheels lock up very easily. Then the trailer can swing out and hit other vehicles, or the tractor can jackknife very fast.
- A **bobtail** tractor is hard to stop smoothly. It needs **more** distance to stop than a tractor-semitrailer carrying its **maximum gross weight**.
- Whatever combination you drive, keep lots of space ahead and scan far down the road. Then you can start braking early and never be surprised into a "panic" stop.

### 6.1.4 Railroad-highway crossings (p. 6-2)
A trailer with little space underneath it (**low clearance**) can get stuck on a raised crossing. The handbook names:
- **Low-slung units:** lowboy, car carrier, moving van, possum-belly livestock trailer.
- A **single-axle tractor** pulling a **long trailer** whose **landing gear** (the legs that hold up the front of a parked trailer) is set for a **tandem-axle tractor**.

If you get stuck on the tracks for any reason:
1. Leave the vehicle and move **away** from the tracks.
2. Look at the signposts or the signal housing at the crossing for emergency contact information.
3. Call **9-1-1** or another emergency number.
4. Describe where the crossing is, using every landmark you can — especially the **DOT number** if one is posted.

(The general railroad crossing rules for all drivers are in GK-11.)

### 6.1.5 Prevent trailer skids (p. 6-3)
When the trailer's wheels **lock up**, the trailer tends to swing around to the side. This is called a **trailer jackknife** (Figure 6.2). It happens more often when the trailer is **empty or lightly loaded**.

**How to stop a trailer skid**
1. **Recognize the skid.** You will see it first — and best — in your **mirrors**. Whenever you brake hard, glance at the mirrors and confirm the trailer is still behind you where it belongs. If the trailer has already left your lane, stopping the jackknife becomes very difficult.
2. **Stop using the brake.** Release the brakes so the tires can grip the road again. When the trailer wheels have traction again, the trailer follows the tractor and straightens out.

**Do NOT** use the trailer hand brake (hand valve) to "straighten out the rig." Why: the locked trailer brakes caused the skid in the first place — more trailer braking makes it worse.

> Handbook vs other sources: some experienced drivers say they use the hand valve this way. The handbook says no. On the test, the answer is always: release the brakes, do not use the hand valve.

### 6.1.6 Turn wide (p. 6-3; Figure 6.4 on p. 6-4)
**Off-tracking** (also called **"cheating"**): in a turn, the rear wheels do not travel in the tracks of the front wheels — they cut toward the inside of the curve. Because of this, the path your rig covers is **wider than the rig itself** (Figure 6.3).
- **Longer** vehicles off-track **more**.
- The tractor's (or truck's) rear wheels cut in a little. The trailer's rear wheels cut in more. With two or more trailers, the **rear wheels of the last trailer** cut in **the most**.

**How to make a tight turn**
- Steer the front end wide enough that the rear wheels do not run over the curb, pedestrians, or other objects.
- Still, keep the **rear** of the rig **close to the curb**. Why: then no one can squeeze past you on the right side.
- Sometimes the turn cannot be finished without entering another lane. Then go **wide at the end of the turn**, as you finish it. This is the **"button hook"** — correct.
- Do **not** move out to the left first and then turn. This is the **"jug handle"** — incorrect. Why: it leaves an open space on your right, and a driver may try to pass you there.

### 6.1.7 Backing with a trailer (p. 6-4)
**The steering is reversed.**
- Car, bus or straight truck: the top of the wheel goes **toward** the side you want to move to.
- Trailer: turn the wheel the **opposite** way. When the trailer begins to swing, turn the wheel back the other way and follow it.
- Figure 6.5: top of the wheel to the **left** → trailer goes **right**. Top of the wheel to the **right** → trailer goes **left**.

**Set up for a straight line.** Whenever you back a trailer, try to place your rig so the backing path is **straight**. Only if a curve cannot be avoided, curve toward the **driver's side**. Why: you can see that side directly.

**Safe backing steps**
- **Look at your path.** Before you start, get out and walk around. Check space on the sides and overhead, in and near your path.
- **Use mirrors on both sides.** Check both outside mirrors often. If you are not sure, get out and look again.
- **Back slowly.** You can fix a mistake before you are far off course.
- **Correct drift immediately.** When the trailer starts to leave the right path, turn the **top of the wheel in the direction of the drift**. Example: the trailer drifts left → turn the top of the wheel left → the trailer is pushed back to the right.
- **Pull forward.** Make "pull-ups" (drive forward a little) to reposition when you need to.

## Exam traps
- **Trap:** "An empty rig stops faster because it is lighter." → **Correct:** An empty combination takes **longer** to stop. Lighter feels easier, but with stiff springs and strong brakes the tires grip poorly and the wheels lock. (p. 6-2)
- **Trap:** "A bobtail tractor stops more quickly than a fully loaded tractor-semitrailer." → **Correct:** The bobtail takes **longer** to stop. A small vehicle seems easier to stop. (p. 6-2)
- **Trap:** "Empty rigs roll over more easily." → **Correct:** **Fully loaded** rigs are **10×** more likely to roll over. Do not mix this up with the stopping rule, where empty is worse. (p. 6-1)
- **Trap:** In a sudden turn with doubles, the **first** trailer is most likely to tip. → **Correct:** The **rear (last)** trailer. The first trailer is closer to the driver and feels more "involved". (p. 6-1)
- **Trap:** Use the trailer hand valve to straighten a skidding trailer. → **Correct:** **Release the brakes.** The trailer brakes caused the skid. Some drivers say they do it — the test answer is still no. (p. 6-3)
- **Trap:** You first notice a trailer skid by feel or sound. → **Correct:** In your **mirrors**. (p. 6-3)
- **Trap:** Swing wide to the left before a right turn. → **Correct:** Turn wide **as you complete** the turn (button hook). Swinging left first invites drivers to pass on your right. (p. 6-3, 6-4)
- **Trap:** Keep the rear of the rig away from the curb in a turn to be safe. → **Correct:** Keep the rear **close to the curb** so no one passes on the right — just not so close that you run over it. (p. 6-3)
- **Trap:** When backing a trailer, turn the wheel the way you want the trailer to go. → **Correct:** Turn it the **opposite** way. That is how a car works, not a trailer. (p. 6-4)
- **Trap:** The best way to back a trailer is on a curve to the driver's side. → **Correct:** Set up to back in a **straight line**. The driver's side is only for when a curve cannot be avoided. (p. 6-4)
- **Trap:** To correct drift, turn the wheel away from the drift. → **Correct:** Turn the **top of the wheel toward the drift**. It sounds backward because trailer steering is reversed. (p. 6-4)
- **Trap:** Add the extra second of following distance at 40 mph. → **Correct:** Only when going **over** 40 mph. (p. 6-1)

## Handbook review questions — answered
**TYK 6.1 #1** Name the two most important things for preventing a rollover.
→ Load the cargo as low as you can, and take turns slowly. (It also helps to keep the load centered.) (p. 6-1)

**TYK 6.1 #2** You are pulling doubles and turn suddenly. Which trailer is most likely to overturn?
→ The rear (last) trailer. Rearward amplification makes the swing grow toward the back — the crack-the-whip effect. (p. 6-1)

**TYK 6.1 #3** Why is the trailer hand brake the wrong way to straighten out a jackknifing trailer?
→ Because the trailer brakes locking the trailer wheels caused the skid. Instead, release the brakes so the trailer wheels regain traction; then the trailer follows the tractor and straightens out. (p. 6-3)

**TYK 6.1 #4** What does "off-tracking" mean?
→ When the rig turns, its rear wheels take a tighter, different line than its front wheels (also called "cheating"), so the rig sweeps a path wider than itself. Longer rigs off-track more; the last trailer's rear wheels off-track the most. (p. 6-3)

**TYK 6.1 #5** True or false: before backing a trailer, you should set up so you back on a curved path toward the driver's side.
→ **False.** Set up so your backing path is a **straight line**. The driver's-side curve is only for when a curve cannot be avoided — it lets you see better. (p. 6-4)

**TYK 6.1 #6** Which kinds of trailers can hang up on railroad-highway crossings?
→ Trailers with low clearance underneath: low-slung units (lowboy, car carrier, moving van, possum-belly livestock trailer), and a single-axle tractor pulling a long trailer whose landing gear is set for a tandem-axle tractor. (p. 6-2)

## One-minute recap
- Rollovers kill more than half of truck drivers who die in crashes; loaded rigs are **10×** more likely to roll. Keep cargo **low** and **centered**, and go **slowly in turns**.
- Crack-the-whip comes from rearward amplification: **2.0** = last trailer tips 2× as easily as the tractor; triples **3.5**. The **last** trailer is the most likely to tip — steer smoothly.
- Following distance: **1 second per 10 ft**, **+1 second over 40 mph**.
- **Empty** rigs and **bobtails** take **longer** to stop — brake early.
- Low-slung trailers (lowboy, car carrier, moving van, possum-belly) and a single-axle tractor pulling a long trailer with landing gear set for a tandem-axle tractor can hang up on crossings. Stuck? Get out, get away, call, give the **DOT number**.
- Trailer skid: see it in the **mirrors**, **release the brakes**, never use the hand valve to straighten it.
- Off-tracking ("cheating"): last trailer's rear wheels cut in the most. Tight turn = **button hook** (wide at the end), never jug handle.
- Backing: wheel **opposite**; back **straight** if you can, else toward the **driver's side**; correct drift by turning **toward** it; go slowly, get out and look, use pull-ups.
