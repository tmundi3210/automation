# GK-12 · Driving emergencies, ABS, and skid control

> **Handbook:** Section 2.17–2.19, pages 2-38–2-43 · **Test:** General Knowledge · **Exam weight:** High · **Study time:** ~45 min

## What you'll be able to answer
- When steering around a hazard is better than stopping, and why the right side is usually the best escape.
- How to leave the road safely and how to get back on (the "steer-countersteer" move).
- The difference between controlled braking and stab braking, and why stab braking is only for trucks without ABS.
- What to do when hydraulic brakes fail, when brakes fail on a downgrade, and when a tire fails.
- What ABS does and does NOT do, which vehicles must have it, how to tell if a vehicle has it, and how to brake with it.
- The four causes of skids and how to correct drive-wheel and front-wheel skids.

## Learn it

### 2.17 Driving emergencies (p. 2-38)
There are two kinds of emergency:
- **Traffic emergency** — two vehicles are about to hit each other.
- **Vehicle emergency** — a tire, the brakes, or another critical part fails.

Following safe driving practices can help prevent emergencies. If one happens anyway, your chance of avoiding a crash depends on how well you act.

### 2.17.1 Steering to avoid an accident (p. 2-38 to 2-39)
**Stopping is not always the safest choice.** If there is not enough room to stop, steer around the problem.
- **Why:** a heavy truck needs a long distance to stop, while turning away from an object almost always takes less time than stopping.
- **Warning:** top-heavy vehicles and tractors pulling more than one trailer may **flip over** in a quick turn.

**Keep both hands on the wheel — all the time.** A quick turn needs a firm grip with both hands. The **best** way to be sure both hands are on the wheel when an emergency comes is to keep them there **all the time**.

**How to make a quick turn safely**
| Do this | Why |
|---|---|
| **Do not brake while turning.** | Wheels lock very easily during a turn. Locked wheels → skid → no control. |
| **Turn only as much as you need** to clear the object. | The sharper the turn, the higher the chance of a skid or rollover. |
| **Be ready to countersteer** once you are past the object. | Countersteer = turn the wheel back the other way. If you are not ready, you will be too slow. Think of steering and countersteering as **one action in two parts**. |

**Where to steer (p. 2-39)**
- **Oncoming driver drifts into your lane → move to your RIGHT.** Why: when that driver notices the mistake, they will most likely swerve back into their own lane — which is on your left.
- **Something blocks your path →** the best direction depends on the situation. Regular mirror checks tell you which lane is open.
- **Shoulder is clear → going right may be best.** Why: nobody is likely to be driving on the shoulder, but a car could be passing you on the left.
- **Blocked on both sides → going right may be best.** Why: you will not push another driver into the oncoming lane and cause a head-on crash.

**Leaving the road (p. 2-39)**
Sometimes driving off the road is less risky than hitting another vehicle. Most shoulders can hold the weight of a big truck, so they are a real escape route. If you leave the road:
- **Avoid braking until your speed is down to about 20 mph.** Then brake very gently. Why: the shoulder surface is loose, and hard braking on it causes a skid.
- **Keep one set of wheels on the pavement if you can.** Why: it helps you keep control.
- **Stay on the shoulder until you stop** (if the shoulder is clear). Then signal and check your mirrors before you pull back onto the road.

**Getting back on the road before you can stop**
If you are forced back onto the road while still moving:
1. Keep a **tight grip** on the wheel and make a turn **sharp enough to put you straight back on the road** safely.
2. **Do NOT edge back on slowly.** Why: the tires can suddenly grab the pavement edge and you can lose control.
3. **As soon as both front tires are on the pavement, countersteer immediately.** The two turns are one move: **"steer-countersteer."**

### 2.17.2 How to stop quickly and safely (p. 2-39)
If someone pulls out in front of you, braking is a good reaction — **if** you have room to stop and you brake correctly. Brake so the truck **stays in a straight line** and you can still **turn** if you need to. There are two methods:

**Controlled braking**
- Press the brakes **as hard as possible while the wheels keep rolling (not locked)**.
- Keep steering movements **very small**.
- Need a bigger steering move, or a wheel locks? **Let off the brakes**, then get back on them as soon as possible.

**Stab braking**
1. Apply the brakes **all the way**.
2. **Release** when the wheels lock.
3. **As soon as** the wheels begin rolling again, brake fully once more.
- After you let off, the wheels may need **up to 1 second** to begin rolling again. If you brake again before they are rolling, the vehicle **will not straighten out**.
- **Stab braking is only for vehicles WITHOUT ABS** (anti-lock brakes).

**Do not jam on the brakes.** Pushing the pedal as hard as possible and holding it only keeps the wheels locked, and a skidding vehicle cannot be controlled. "Emergency braking" means slowing down in response to the hazard, not stomping the pedal.

If your vehicle has ABS, follow the owner's manual for quick stops.

### 2.17.3 Brake failure (p. 2-40)
Brakes that are well maintained seldom fail. Most **hydraulic** (fluid-pressure) brake failures have **2 causes**:
1. **Loss of hydraulic pressure**
2. **Brake fade on long hills** (brakes get so hot they stop working well)

(Air brake failures are in Section 5 — you studied them for the Air Brakes test.)

**Loss of hydraulic pressure** — the pedal feels **spongy** or **goes to the floor**. What to do:
- **Downshift.** A lower gear helps slow the truck.
- **Pump the brakes.** Pumping may build enough pressure to stop.
- **Use the parking (emergency) brake.** It is a separate system from the hydraulic brakes. Hold the **release button or lever at the same time** so you can control how hard it brakes and keep the wheels from locking.
- **Find an escape route** while slowing: an open field, a side street, or an escape ramp. **Heading uphill** uses gravity to help you slow and stop.
- After you stop, make sure the truck **does not roll backward**: put it in a **low gear**, set the **parking brake**, and if needed, let it roll back into something that will stop it.

**Brake failure on a downgrade**
- Prevention: keep your speed down and use proper braking technique, and brakes will almost never fail on a long grade.
- After the brakes fail, only something **outside the truck** — a ramp, a hill, a field — can stop it.
- **Your best hope is an escape ramp** (runaway truck ramp). Signs warn you it is coming (for example, "RUNAWAY TRUCK RAMP 1 MILE"). You'll usually find them **a few miles below the top** of the grade.
  - Some ramps use **soft gravel** that resists the wheels and stops the truck.
  - Others **turn uphill**, using the hill to stop the truck and soft gravel to hold it there.
  - **Hundreds of drivers a year** are saved from injury or damage by these ramps. **If you lose your brakes going downhill and there is a ramp, use it.** Skipping it makes a serious crash much more likely.
- **No ramp?** Take the **least dangerous escape route** you can find — for example an open field, or a side road that levels off or climbs uphill.
- **Act as soon as you know the brakes do not work.** Why: every second you wait, the truck goes faster and becomes harder to stop.

### 2.17.4 Tire failure (p. 2-40)
Knowing quickly that a tire has failed gives you a few extra seconds to remember what to do.

**Signs of tire failure**
| Sign | What it tells you |
|---|---|
| **Sound** — a loud "bang" | A blowout. The truck may take a few seconds to react, so you may think it was another vehicle. **Assume it was yours.** |
| **Vibration** — heavy thumping or shaking | A tire may have gone flat. It may be your **only** warning of a failed **rear tire**. |
| **Feel** — steering feels **"heavy"** | Probably a **front** tire failed. |
| **Feel** — the rear slides side to side (**"fishtail"**) | Sometimes a rear tire failure. **Dual rear tires usually prevent this.** |

**What to do right away**
1. **Hold the steering wheel firmly** with both hands. Why: a failed front tire can **twist the wheel out of your hands**.
2. **Stay off the brake.** Braking after a tire failure can make you lose control. Unless you are about to hit something, let the truck **lose speed** first; then brake **very gently**, get off the road, and stop.
3. **Check all the tires** after you stop — even if the truck seems to handle fine. Why: if one tire of a **dual pair** fails, looking at it may be the only way you will know.

### 2.18 Anti-lock braking systems — ABS (p. 2-41)
**ABS** uses a **computer to prevent wheel lockup** when you brake hard.
- It works **on top of** your regular brakes. Your basic braking ability stays the same — **no more, no less**.
- It switches on **only at the moment a wheel is about to lock**.
- Its job is **control, not a shorter stop**: you may or may not stop sooner, but you **keep control** while braking hard.

### 2.18.1 How ABS works (p. 2-41)
- **Sensors** notice that a wheel is about to lock.
- The **ECU** (electronic control unit — the system's small computer) then **lowers the brake pressure** on that wheel.
- Pressure is adjusted to give the **most braking possible without lockup**.
- ABS reacts **far faster than any driver** could. The rest of the time, the brakes work normally.

### 2.18.2 Vehicles required to have ABS (p. 2-41)
The U.S. Department of Transportation (DOT) requires ABS on:

| Vehicle | Built on or after |
|---|---|
| **Truck tractors with air brakes** | **March 1, 1997** |
| **All other air-brake vehicles** — buses, trucks, trailers, converter dollies | **March 1, 1998** |
| **Hydraulic-brake trucks and buses** with a GVWR of **10,000 lb or more** | **March 1, 1999** |

- **GVWR** = gross vehicle weight rating, the maximum loaded weight the maker allows. A **converter dolly** is the wheeled unit used to hook up a second trailer.
- Memory aid: **97-98-99** → tractors first, then other air-brake units, then hydraulic trucks and buses.
- Many older CMVs (commercial motor vehicles) were **voluntarily** fitted with ABS.

### 2.18.3 How to know if your vehicle has ABS (p. 2-41)
- **Tractors, trucks, and buses:** a **yellow** ABS malfunction lamp on the **instrument panel** (dashboard).
- **Trailers:** a **yellow** ABS lamp on the **left side**, at either the **front or rear corner**.
- **Dollies built on or after March 1, 1998:** must have a **yellow** lamp on the **left side**.
- **Bulb check:** on newer vehicles the lamp lights at **start-up** and then goes out quickly. On **older** systems it may stay on until you are going **over 5 mph**.
- A lamp that **remains lit** once the bulb check is over, or that **lights up while you are driving**, means you may have **lost ABS control**.
- **Older towed units** (built before ABS was required) are harder to check: look **under the unit** for an **ECU** box and for **wheel-speed sensor wires** running from the backs of the brakes.

### 2.18.4 How ABS helps you (p. 2-41)
Without ABS, hard braking on a slippery road can lock the wheels:
- **Steering wheels lock** → you **lose steering control**.
- **Other wheels lock** → you may **skid, jackknife, or spin**.

With ABS, the wheels do not lock, so you keep control. You **may or may not stop faster**. But you **should** still be able to **steer around an obstacle while you brake**, and ABS helps you avoid skids caused by **over-braking**.

### 2.18.5 ABS on only the tractor or only the trailer (p. 2-42)
Partial ABS still helps: even ABS on just the tractor, just the trailer, or a **single axle** improves control while braking. **Brake normally.**
- **ABS on the tractor only:** you keep steering control and a jackknife is less likely. But **watch the trailer** — if it starts to swing out, **ease off the brakes** (if it is safe).
- **ABS on the trailer only:** there is **less chance the trailer swings out**. Still, if you **lose steering control** or the **tractor starts to jackknife**, **ease off the brakes** (if safe) until you are back in control.

### 2.18.6 Braking with ABS (p. 2-42)
**Brake the way you always have:**
- Use **only as much braking force as you need** for a safe, controlled stop.
- Brake **the same way** whether ABS is on the bus, the tractor, the trailer, or both.
- While slowing, **watch the tractor and trailer** and back off the brakes (if safe) to stay in control.

**The one exception:** if your straight truck or combination has **working ABS on ALL axles**, you may **brake fully** in an **emergency stop**.

### 2.18.7 Braking if ABS is not working (p. 2-42)
- Without ABS you **still have your normal brakes**. Drive and brake as usual.
- A lamp that stays on after the bulb check, or comes on while driving, means you may have lost ABS on **1 or more wheels**.
- **Drive normally, but get the ABS repaired soon.**

### 2.18.8 Safety reminders — what ABS will NOT do (p. 2-42)
- ABS is **no excuse** for more speed, shorter following distance, or careless driving.
- It does **not** stop **power skids** (spinning drive wheels) or **turning skids** (too fast in a turn). It should stop skids and jackknifes **caused by braking**.
- It will **not necessarily shorten** your stopping distance. It helps you keep control, but the stop may be no shorter.
- Your **ultimate stopping power** does not go up or down — ABS is an **add-on**, not a replacement for regular brakes.
- It does **not change how you normally brake**: in normal braking the vehicle stops just as it always has. ABS steps in only when a wheel **would have locked** from over-braking.
- **Worn or badly maintained brakes** are still bad brakes, ABS or not.

Remember: a careful **driver** is still the most important safety feature. Aim to drive so ABS **never has to kick in** — still, in a real emergency it can help you avoid a serious crash.

### 2.19 Skid control and recovery (p. 2-42)
You are **skidding** when the tires **stop gripping** the road surface. The **4 causes**:

| Cause | What happens |
|---|---|
| **Over-braking** | Braking so hard the wheels lock. **Using the speed retarder on a slippery road** can also cause a skid. |
| **Over-steering** | Turning the wheels harder than the vehicle is able to follow. |
| **Over-acceleration** | Giving so much power that the drive wheels spin. |
| **Driving too fast** | The cause of **most serious skids**. If your speed fits the conditions and you are gentle on the gas, you won't be forced to brake or steer too hard. |

### 2.19.1 Drive-wheel skids (p. 2-43)
This is **by far the skid you are most likely to have**: the **rear drive wheels lose grip** because of hard braking or too much throttle.

**Ice and snow** are where **acceleration skids** usually happen.
- Fix: **take your foot off the accelerator.**
- If it is **very slippery**, also **push in the clutch**. Why: otherwise the engine can stop the wheels from rolling freely and getting their grip back.

**Rear-wheel braking skids** happen when the rear drive wheels **lock**.
- **A locked wheel grips the road less than a rolling one.** The rear wheels tend to swing out sideways, as if racing to "catch up" with the front wheels.
- **Bus or straight truck:** the vehicle slides sideways in a **"spin out."**
- **Vehicle towing a trailer:** the trailer can **push the tractor sideways** → a sudden **jackknife** (Figure 2.19 shows a tractor jackknife caused by locked or spinning rear tractor wheels).

### 2.19.2 Correcting a drive-wheel braking skid (p. 2-43)
1. **Stop braking.** The rear wheels roll again and stop sliding.
2. **Countersteer.** When the vehicle comes back into line, it wants to **keep rotating** past straight. Turn the wheel **quickly the other way**, or you will skid in the **opposite direction**.

These skid skills (off the brake, fast steering, clutch in, countersteer) need lots of practice. A big driving range or **"skid pad"** is the best place to learn them.

### 2.19.3 Front-wheel skids (p. 2-43)
**Causes:**
- **Most often:** driving **too fast for conditions**.
- Also: **little tread on the front tires**, and cargo loaded so there is **not enough weight on the front axle**.

**What happens:** the front end keeps going **straight** no matter how much you turn the wheel. On very slick roads the truck may not follow a curve or corner at all.

**The only fix: let the vehicle slow down.** Stop turning so sharply and/or braking so hard. Bring the speed down quickly, but **not so hard that you start a new skid**.

## Exam traps
- **Trap:** "In an emergency, always stop." → **Correct:** stopping is **not always** safest; turning is usually quicker than stopping. The instinct to brake is strong, so this feels right. (p. 2-38)
- **Trap:** Steer **left** around an oncoming car in your lane. → **Correct:** steer **right**. Left looks like the "passing lane," but the other driver will likely swerve back to that side. (p. 2-39)
- **Trap:** Brake hard as soon as you leave the road. → **Correct:** avoid braking until about **20 mph**, then brake gently. Braking feels safe, but loose shoulders make locked wheels skid. (p. 2-39)
- **Trap:** Ease back onto the road slowly and gradually. → **Correct:** turn **sharply** back on, then **countersteer** when both front tires are on pavement. "Gentle" sounds safe, but tires can grab the edge. (p. 2-39)
- **Trap:** Emergency braking means pushing the pedal as hard as you can. → **Correct:** that locks the wheels and causes a skid; use **controlled** or **stab** braking. (p. 2-39)
- **Trap:** Use stab braking in a truck with ABS. → **Correct:** stab braking is **only for vehicles without ABS**. (p. 2-39)
- **Trap:** In stab braking, reapply the instant you release. → **Correct:** wait until the wheels **roll again** (up to 1 second), or the truck won't straighten. (p. 2-39)
- **Trap:** With a hydraulic failure, pull the parking brake hard and hold it. → **Correct:** hold the **release button/lever at the same time** so you can control it and prevent lockup. (p. 2-40)
- **Trap:** Brakes fail on a hill — wait to see if they come back. → **Correct:** take the escape route **as soon as** you know; speed only grows. (p. 2-40)
- **Trap:** Brake hard after a blowout. → **Correct:** **stay off the brake** unless about to hit something; hold the wheel firmly. The instinct to brake is the danger. (p. 2-40)
- **Trap:** ABS shortens stopping distance / adds braking power. → **Correct:** ABS helps you **keep control**; it does **not** necessarily shorten stopping distance or change stopping power. (p. 2-41, 2-42)
- **Trap:** ABS prevents all skids. → **Correct:** it prevents **braking** skids, **not** power skids or turning skids. (p. 2-42)
- **Trap:** With ABS, brake differently (pump or press hard every time). → **Correct:** brake **as you always have**; full application only with working ABS on **all axles** in an emergency. (p. 2-42)
- **Trap:** ABS lamp is red / on the right side of the trailer. → **Correct:** **yellow**, trailer lamp on the **left** side. (p. 2-41)
- **Trap:** 1998 is the tractor date. → **Correct:** air-brake **tractors 1997**; other air-brake vehicles **1998**; hydraulic ≥10,000 lb **1999**. (p. 2-41)
- **Trap:** The most common skid is a front-wheel skid. → **Correct:** **drive-wheel** skids are by far the most common. (p. 2-43)
- **Trap:** Drive-wheel braking skid — keep braking. → **Correct:** **stop braking**, then **countersteer**. (p. 2-43)
- **Trap:** Acceleration skid — hit the brakes. → **Correct:** **take your foot off the accelerator** (push in the clutch if very slippery). (p. 2-43)
- **Trap:** Front-wheel skid — turn the wheel more. → **Correct:** the front goes straight no matter what; **let the vehicle slow down**. (p. 2-43)
- **Trap:** Retarders help on slippery roads. → **Correct:** using the retarder on a slippery road can **cause a skid** (over-braking). (p. 2-42)

## Handbook review questions — answered
**TYK "SUBSECTIONS 2.17, 2.18, AND 2.19" #1** True or false: in an emergency, stopping isn't always the safest thing to do.
→ **True.** If there is not enough room to stop, steer away from the hazard; turning around it is almost always quicker than stopping (but top-heavy vehicles and multi-trailer rigs may roll over). (p. 2-38)

**TYK "SUBSECTIONS 2.17, 2.18, AND 2.19" #2** Why is steering right around an obstacle usually better than steering left?
→ (1) If an oncoming driver has drifted into your lane, their natural reaction is to swerve back to their own lane — your left — so moving right keeps you apart. (2) If the shoulder is clear, nobody is likely to be driving on it, while someone may be passing you on the left. (3) If you are blocked on both sides, going right will not push another driver into the opposing lane and cause a head-on crash. (p. 2-39)

**TYK "SUBSECTIONS 2.17, 2.18, AND 2.19" #3** What is an escape ramp?
→ A runaway-truck lane, built on many steep downgrades, that stops a vehicle whose brakes have failed **safely, without injuring the people on board**. Some use a long bed of soft, loose gravel to slow and stop the truck; others also turn uphill and use the grade to stop it, with gravel holding it in place. Signs announce ramps, which are usually a few miles from the top of the grade. If you lose your brakes going downhill and a ramp is there, use it. (p. 2-40; also p. 2-38)

**TYK "SUBSECTIONS 2.17, 2.18, AND 2.19" #4** True or false: after a tire blowout you should brake hard to stop quickly.
→ **False.** Hold the wheel firmly with both hands and stay off the brake unless you are about to hit something. When the vehicle has slowed, brake very gently, pull off the road, stop, and check all tires. (p. 2-40)

**TYK "SUBSECTIONS 2.17, 2.18, AND 2.19" #5** How can you tell whether a vehicle has anti-lock brakes?
→ Look for the **yellow ABS malfunction lamp**: on the instrument panel of tractors, trucks, and buses; on the **left side** (front or rear corner) of trailers; on the left side of dollies built on/after March 1, 1998. On newer vehicles it lights briefly at start-up as a bulb check. Vehicles built on or after the required dates must have ABS (air-brake tractors from March 1, 1997; other air-brake vehicles from March 1, 1998; hydraulic trucks/buses of 10,000 lb GVWR or more from March 1, 1999). For older towed units, check underneath for an ECU and for wheel-speed sensor wires running out of the backs of the brakes. (p. 2-41)

**TYK "SUBSECTIONS 2.17, 2.18, AND 2.19" #6** How should you brake in a vehicle with ABS?
→ Brake the way you always have: use only the force needed for a safe, controlled stop; brake the same way whether ABS is on the tractor, trailer, bus, or both; watch the tractor and trailer and ease off the brakes (if safe) to stay in control. The only exception: with working ABS on **all** axles of a straight truck or combination, you may apply the brakes fully in an emergency stop. Do not use stab braking with ABS. (p. 2-42; p. 2-39)

**TYK "SUBSECTIONS 2.17, 2.18, AND 2.19" #7** How does ABS help you?
→ It keeps the wheels from locking during hard braking, so you keep steering control. While braking, you should still be able to steer around an obstacle, and you avoid skids and jackknifes caused by over-braking. It may or may not help you stop sooner. (p. 2-41, 2-42)

## One-minute recap
- Stopping is not always best: turning is almost always faster. **Steer right** when a driver drifts into your lane (right is usually best), keep both hands on the wheel, don't brake while turning, and be ready to **countersteer**.
- Leaving the road: **no braking until about 20 mph**, one set of wheels on pavement, stay on the shoulder until stopped. Getting back on: turn **sharply**, countersteer when **both front tires** are on pavement.
- Quick stops: **controlled braking** (hard, no lockup) or **stab braking** (wait up to **1 second** for wheels to roll) — stab braking **only without ABS**. Never jam the brakes.
- Hydraulic failure: downshift, pump, parking brake (hold the release), escape route. Downgrade failure: **use the escape ramp**; act at once.
- Tire failure: **hold the wheel firmly, stay off the brake**, then stop gently and check all tires. Heavy steering = front tire.
- ABS keeps wheels from locking so you can **steer**; it does **not** add power or always shorten stops. Dates **1997 / 1998 / 1999**. **Yellow** lamp; trailer lamp on the **left**.
- Brake normally with ABS; full application only with working ABS on **all axles**. ABS failure = normal brakes remain.
- Skids: over-braking, over-steering, over-acceleration, too fast. **Drive-wheel skid** is most common → **stop braking, countersteer**. **Front-wheel skid** → **let the truck slow down**.
