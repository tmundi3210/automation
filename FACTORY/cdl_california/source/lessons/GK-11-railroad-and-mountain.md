# GK-11 · Railroad crossings and mountain driving

> **Handbook:** Section 2.15–2.16, pages 2-35–2-38 · **Test:** General Knowledge · **Exam weight:** High · **Study time:** ~35 min

## What you'll be able to answer
- The difference between passive and active crossings, and what each railroad sign, marking and signal tells you.
- Who must stop at a crossing, where to stop (**15–50 ft**), and what to do while stopping.
- How long a tractor-trailer needs to clear one or two tracks, which trailers get stuck, and what to do if you are stuck.
- How to choose a safe downhill speed and the right gear — and why you must pick the gear before the hill.
- The proper downhill braking method (often called "snub braking"), what brake fade is, and what escape ramps do.

## Learn it

### 2.15 Railroad-highway crossings (p. 2-35)
A **railroad-highway grade crossing** is a special intersection where a road crosses train tracks at the same level. These crossings are **always dangerous**.
- Approach **every** crossing as if a train is coming.
- It is very hard to judge **how far away** a train is and **how fast** it is going.

### 2.15.1 Types of crossings (p. 2-35)
| Type | What it has | What it means for you |
|---|---|---|
| **Passive** | **No** traffic control device | The decision is **all yours**: spot the crossing, search for trains, and decide if there is enough clear space to cross safely |
| **Active** | A traffic control device: **flashing red lights** (with or without bells), or **flashing red lights with bells and gates** | The device controls traffic, but you still must look (see "Do not rely on signals" below) |

### 2.15.2 Warning signs and devices (pp. 2-35–2-36)
- **Advance warning sign** — **round, black on yellow**. It stands **before** a public crossing. It tells you to **slow down, look and listen** for a train, and be ready to stop at the tracks if one is coming. **All vehicles carrying passengers or HazMat (hazardous materials) must stop.**
- **Pavement markings** — carry the **same message as the advance warning sign**. They are a large **"X" and "RR"** painted on the road, plus a no-passing marking on two-lane roads.
- **No-passing zone sign** — also posted on two-lane roads near crossings.
- **White stop line** — may be painted on the road before the tracks. A **school bus** must keep its front **behind** this line while stopped.
- **Crossbuck** — the X-shaped sign that marks the crossing itself. It tells you to **give the train the right-of-way** (yield).
  - Where **no white stop line** is painted, any vehicle that has to stop must stop **no closer than 15 ft** and **no farther than 50 ft** from the **nearest rail** of the nearest track.
  - If the road crosses **more than one track**, a sign **under the crossbuck** shows how many tracks there are.
- **Flashing red light signals** — often on the crossbuck, usually with bells. When the lights start flashing, **stop — a train is coming**. You must yield. If there is more than one track, make sure **all** tracks are clear before you cross.
- **Gates** — come with flashing red lights and bells. Stop as soon as the lights **start** flashing, **before** the gate comes down. Stay stopped until the gates are **all the way up** **and** the lights have **stopped flashing**. Then go when it is safe.

**Worked example (stop distance):** No stop line is painted. You stop with your front bumper 10 ft from the nearest rail → **too close**. You stop 70 ft back → **too far**. Anywhere from **15 ft to 50 ft** is correct.

### 2.15.3 Driving procedures (p. 2-36)
- **Never race a train** to a crossing. Why: you cannot judge a train's speed well.
- **Reduce speed** to match how well you can see trains coming from **any** direction. Keep your speed low enough to **stop before the tracks** if you need to.
- **Don't expect to hear a train.** At some crossings trains may not, or are not allowed to, sound their horns. (Public crossings like this should have signs.) Noise inside your cab can also hide the horn until the train is dangerously close.
- **Don't rely on signals.** Don't count only on warning signals, gates or flaggers to tell you a train is coming. Be **extra careful** at crossings that have **no gates or flashing lights**.
- **Double tracks need a double check.** A train on one track can **hide** a train on the other. Look both ways. After one train clears the crossing, make sure **no other train** is near before you start across.
- **Yard areas and town or city crossings** are **just as dangerous** as country crossings. Give them the same caution.

### 2.15.4 Stopping safely at crossings (p. 2-36)
A **full stop** is required at a crossing whenever:
- your **cargo** makes a stop mandatory under state or federal rules, or
- the **law** otherwise requires a stop.

When you stop:
- **Check traffic behind you** and stop **gradually**. Use a **pullout lane** if there is one.
- Turn on your **4-way emergency flashers**.

Why: drivers behind you may not expect a truck to stop at the tracks, so you must warn them and avoid being rear-ended.

### 2.15.5 Crossing the tracks (p. 2-36)
- Crossings with **steep approaches** can make your unit **hang up** (get stuck with the underside resting on the tracks).
- **Never let traffic trap you** in a spot where you would have to stop on the tracks. Before you start across, be sure you can get **all the way** to the other side.
- Clearing time for a typical tractor-trailer: a **single** track takes **at least 14 seconds**; a **double** track takes **more than 15 seconds**.
- **Don't change gears** while you are crossing the tracks. (Why: a missed shift could leave you stalled on the tracks.)

### 2.15.6 Special situations (pp. 2-36–2-37)
These can **hang up on a raised (humped) crossing**:
- **Low-slung units** — lowboys, car carriers, moving vans, and possum-belly livestock trailers.
- A **single-axle tractor** pulling a **long trailer** whose **landing gear is set for a tandem-axle tractor**.

Terms: a **lowboy** is a very low trailer used for heavy equipment. A **possum-belly** trailer has a compartment that hangs low between the axles. **Landing gear** is the pair of legs that hold up the front of a trailer when it is not coupled. A **tandem-axle** tractor has two rear axles; a single-axle tractor sits lower, so a trailer set up for a tandem tractor rides nose-down and can scrape.

**If you get stuck on the tracks — for any reason:**
1. **Leave the vehicle** and **get well away from the tracks**.
2. Look on the **signposts or the signal housing** at the crossing for emergency contact information.
3. Call **9-1-1** or another emergency number.
4. Give the crossing's location using every landmark you can — especially the **DOT number**, if one is posted.

### 2.16 Mountain driving (p. 2-37)
In the mountains, **gravity** is the big factor.
- **Going up:** gravity slows you. The **steeper** the grade, the **longer** the grade, and the **heavier** the load, the more you will need **lower gears** to climb.
- **Going down:** gravity makes you **speed up**. You must choose a **safe speed**, use a **low gear**, and use **proper braking**.
- **Plan ahead.** Find out about long, steep grades on your route. If you can, ask drivers who know those grades what speeds are safe.
- Keep your speed low enough that the brakes can hold the vehicle back **without overheating**.

**Brake fade:** overheated brakes need **more and more pedal pressure** to give the **same** stopping power. Keep braking hard and the fade can get worse until the brakes **can no longer slow or stop you at all**.

### 2.16.1 Select a "safe" speed (p. 2-37)
Your **most important** decision on a downgrade is a speed that is not too fast for these **five factors**:
1. **Total weight** of the vehicle and cargo
2. **Length** of the grade
3. **Steepness** of the grade
4. **Road conditions**
5. **Weather**

Memory hook: **"We Like Steep Roads, Wisely"** — Weight, Length, Steepness, Road, Weather.

- **Never go faster** than a posted speed limit or a **"Maximum Safe Speed"** sign. Also watch for and obey signs showing how **long and steep** the grade is.
- Your **engine's braking effect** must be the **main (principal)** way you control speed. **Engine braking** is the slowing force of the engine when you are off the accelerator. It is **strongest** when the engine is near its **governed rpm** (the top engine speed allowed by the governor) and the transmission is in a **lower gear**.
- **Save your service brakes** so they are there when road and traffic conditions make you slow down or stop.

### 2.16.2 Select the right gear BEFORE starting down (p. 2-37)
- Shift into a **low gear before** you start down the grade.
- **Don't try to downshift after your speed has built up.** Why:
  - you **won't be able** to get into a lower gear;
  - you may not be able to get into **any** gear, and then you **lose all engine braking**.
- **Automatic transmission:** forcing it into a lower gear at high speed can **damage the transmission** and also cause **loss of all engine braking**.

**Which gear?**

| Truck | Rule |
|---|---|
| **Older trucks** | Use the **same gear** going down that you would need to climb the hill |
| **Modern trucks** | They have low-friction parts, streamlined shapes and often stronger engines. They climb in higher gears, and going down they have less friction and air drag holding them back. So you **may need a LOWER gear going down** than going up |

Learn which gear is right for **your** truck.

### 2.16.3 Brake fading or failure (p. 2-37)
- Brakes work by **shoes or pads rubbing** against **drums or discs**. This makes **heat**. Brakes are built to handle a lot of heat, but **too much heat** — from using them too much and not relying on engine braking — makes them **fade or fail**.
- **Adjustment matters.** Each brake has to carry its fair share of the work. Brakes that are **out of adjustment stop doing their share first**. The other brakes then **overheat and fade**, and there isn't enough braking left to control the vehicle.
- Brakes can go out of adjustment **quickly**, especially when used a lot. Brake linings also **wear faster when hot**. So **check brake adjustment often**.

### 2.16.4 Proper braking technique (p. 2-38)
On a long or steep downgrade, the brakes are only a **supplement** (a helper) to engine braking. Once you are in the proper **low gear**:
1. Press the brakes **just firmly enough to feel a clear, definite slowdown**.
2. Once you are down to about **5 mph below your "safe" speed**, **let off** the brakes. Each application should take **about 3 seconds**.
3. When the speed has risen back to your "safe" speed, **repeat** steps 1 and 2.

**Worked example:** your safe speed is **40 mph**. Don't brake until you reach 40 mph. Then brake firmly enough to slow gradually to **35 mph**, and release. Let the speed come back up to 40, then do it again. Repeat until you reach the bottom of the grade.

> Name note: practice tests usually call this **"snub braking."** The handbook just calls it the proper braking technique — same method.

Why it works: the engine does most of the work, and short, firm brake applications with release time in between keep the brakes from building up the heat that causes fade.

**Escape ramps.** Many steep mountain downgrades have **escape ramps**, built to stop **runaway vehicles** safely without injuring the people inside.
- They slow the vehicle with a **long bed of loose, soft material**, sometimes combined with an **upgrade** (an uphill slope).
- **Know where the escape ramps are** on your route. **Signs** show where they are.
- Escape ramps save **lives, equipment and cargo**.
- (What to do if your brakes fail on a downgrade is covered in GK-12.)

## Exam traps
- **Trap:** It takes about 14 seconds to clear a double track. → **Correct:** **at least 14 s for a single** track, **more than 15 s for a double**. The two numbers are close and easy to swap. (p. 2-36)
- **Trap:** Stop as close to the tracks as possible to see better. → **Correct:** with no stop line, stop **15–50 ft** from the nearest rail. (p. 2-35)
- **Trap:** Go when the train has passed, even if lights are still flashing. → **Correct:** wait until the **gates are up and the lights stop flashing** — and check for a second train. (p. 2-36)
- **Trap:** Signals and gates will always warn you. → **Correct:** **don't rely on signals**; always look and listen yourself. (p. 2-36)
- **Trap:** You'll hear the train horn. → **Correct:** some trains don't or can't sound horns, and cab noise can hide them. (p. 2-36)
- **Trap:** City and yard crossings are safer than country ones. → **Correct:** they are **just as dangerous**. (p. 2-36)
- **Trap:** Downshift halfway across the tracks for power. → **Correct:** **don't shift gears** while crossing. (p. 2-36)
- **Trap:** If stuck on the tracks, try to rock or drive the truck free first. → **Correct:** **get out and away** from the tracks, then call for help with the DOT number. Instinct is to save the truck. (pp. 2-36–2-37)
- **Trap:** Downshift once you notice speed building on the hill. → **Correct:** choose the low gear **before** starting down; later you may not get into any gear. (p. 2-37)
- **Trap:** Always use the same gear down that you used going up. → **Correct:** that is the **old-truck** rule; modern trucks **may need a lower** gear going down. (p. 2-37)
- **Trap:** The service brakes are your main downhill speed control. → **Correct:** **engine braking** is the main control; brakes are only a **supplement**. (pp. 2-37–2-38)
- **Trap:** Brake down to 10 or 15 mph below the safe speed. → **Correct:** about **5 mph below**, for about **3 seconds**. (p. 2-38)
- **Trap:** Keep light, steady pressure on the brakes all the way down. → **Correct:** use firm applications (definite slowdown), release, and repeat — steady use builds heat and causes **fade**. (pp. 2-37–2-38)
- **Trap:** Brake adjustment only matters for the parking brake. → **Correct:** out-of-adjustment brakes make the **others overheat and fade**; check adjustment often. (p. 2-37)

## Handbook review questions — answered
**TYK 2.15 & 2.16 #1** On a long, steep downgrade, what decides your "safe" speed?
→ The **total weight** of the vehicle and cargo, the **length** of the grade, the **steepness** of the grade, **road conditions**, and **weather**. Never go faster than a posted speed limit or "Maximum Safe Speed" sign, and obey signs about the grade's length and steepness. (p. 2-37)

**TYK 2.15 & 2.16 #2** Why must you be in the right gear before you start down a hill?
→ Because once your speed has built up you **cannot downshift** into a lower gear, and you may not get into **any** gear — then you **lose all engine braking**, which is your main way of controlling speed. With an automatic, forcing a lower gear at high speed can damage the transmission and also lose all engine braking. Without engine braking, the service brakes overheat and **fade**. (p. 2-37)

**TYK 2.15 & 2.16 #3** What is the correct way to brake on a long, steep downgrade?
→ First get into the proper low gear; the brakes only help the engine. Then: (1) brake just firmly enough to feel a definite slowdown; (2) when you are about **5 mph below** your safe speed, release them — the application should take about **3 seconds**; (3) when your speed rises back to the safe speed, repeat. Example: safe speed 40 mph → brake at 40 down to 35, release, repeat. (p. 2-38)

**TYK 2.15 & 2.16 #4** Which vehicles can get stuck on a railroad-highway crossing?
→ **Low-slung units** — lowboys, car carriers, moving vans and possum-belly livestock trailers — and a **single-axle tractor towing a long trailer** whose landing gear is adjusted for a tandem-axle tractor. Crossings with steep approaches can also make any unit hang up. (p. 2-36)

**TYK 2.15 & 2.16 #5** How long does a typical tractor-trailer need to clear a double track?
→ **More than 15 seconds.** (A single track takes at least 14 seconds.) (p. 2-36)

## One-minute recap
- Treat **every** crossing as if a train is coming. Don't race trains, don't trust your ears, and don't rely only on signals.
- Advance sign = **round, black on yellow**. Crossbuck = **yield**. Passenger and **HazMat** vehicles must stop.
- No stop line → stop **15–50 ft** from the nearest rail. Check behind, stop gradually, use **4-way flashers**.
- Gates: wait until they're **up** and lights **stop flashing**. Double tracks: check for a **second train**.
- Clear a track: **at least 14 s** (single), **more than 15 s** (double). Be sure you can get all the way across; **don't shift gears** on the tracks.
- Low-slung units hang up. Stuck? **Get out and away**, call **9-1-1**, give the **DOT number**.
- Downhill: safe speed from **weight, length, steepness, road, weather**. Pick a low gear **before** the hill (modern trucks may need a **lower** gear than climbing). **Engine braking** is the main control.
- Snub braking: brake to **5 mph below** safe speed (~**3 s**), release, repeat. Hot or out-of-adjustment brakes **fade**. Know where the **escape ramps** are.
