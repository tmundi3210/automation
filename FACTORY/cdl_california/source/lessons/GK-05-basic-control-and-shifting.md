# GK-05 · Basic control, backing, shifting, retarders

> **Handbook:** Section 2.2–2.3, pages 2-9–2-11 · **Test:** General Knowledge · **Exam weight:** Medium · **Study time:** ~25 min

## What you'll be able to answer
- How to start moving on a hill without rolling back, and what to do if the drive wheels spin.
- The six safe-backing rules, why you back toward the driver's side, and the most important hand signal to agree on with a helper.
- Which way to turn the steering wheel when backing a trailer, and how to fix a trailer that drifts.
- The five steps of double clutching (up and down), and the two ways to know when to shift.
- The two situations where you must downshift, and how an automatic transmission helps on a downgrade.
- What a retarder does, its four types, and when you must turn it off.

## Learn it

### 2.2 Basic control of your vehicle (p. 2-9)
To control a truck or bus you must control two things: its **speed** and its **direction**. The handbook names **4 skills**:
- Accelerating (speeding up)
- Steering
- Stopping
- Backing safely

Two habits apply every time:
- Wear your **seatbelt** whenever you are on the road.
- Set the **parking brake** whenever you leave the vehicle. *Why:* a parked truck with no one in it can roll away.

### 2.2.1 Accelerating (p. 2-9)
**Rule 1 — Do not roll back when you start.** *Why:* the vehicle or person behind you may be hit.

How to prevent rollback:
- **Manual transmission:** let the clutch partly out (partly engaged) *before* your right foot leaves the brake pedal. ("Engage the clutch" = let the clutch pedal come up so the engine starts to drive the wheels.)
- **Parking brake:** use it any time you need help holding the vehicle. Take it off **only after** the engine is producing enough power to hold the vehicle by itself.
- **Tractor-trailer with a trailer brake hand valve:** you may apply the hand valve to hold the rig while starting. (The *hand valve* is a lever that works only the trailer brakes.)
- **Bus on a flat road with good traction:** you often do not need the parking brake to start.

> **Note — handbook conflict:** Section 6.2.1 (Combination test, p. 6-5) says the hand valve should be used **only to test trailer brakes**, never while driving and **never for parking**. On the General Knowledge test, "use the hand valve to keep from rolling back" is a correct answer (p. 2-9). On the Combination test, answer the Section 6 way.

**Rule 2 — Speed up smoothly and gradually.** *Why:* jerky starts can damage the vehicle's parts, and when you pull a trailer they can damage the **coupling** (the connection between tractor and trailer).

**Rule 3 — On poor traction (rain, snow), speed up very gradually.** *Why:* too much power makes the **drive wheels** (the wheels the engine turns) spin, and you can lose control.
- If the drive wheels start to spin → **take your foot off the accelerator.** Do not add more power.

### 2.2.2 Steering (p. 2-9)
Grip the wheel firmly with **both hands**, placed on **opposite sides** of the wheel.
*Why:* if a tire hits a curb or a pothole (also called a "chuckhole"), the wheel can be jerked out of a loose grip.

### 2.2.3 Stopping (p. 2-9)
- Press the brake pedal down **gradually**.
- The pressure you need depends on two things: your **speed**, and **how fast you must stop**.
- Adjust the pressure to get a smooth, safe stop.
- **Manual transmission:** press the clutch pedal down once the engine has slowed to **near idle** (idle = the slow engine speed with your foot off the gas). *Why:* this keeps the engine running as the truck comes to a stop.

### 2.2.4 Backing safely (p. 2-10)
Backing is **always dangerous**, because you cannot see everything behind you.
- Best rule: **avoid backing** when you can.
- When you park, choose a spot you can **pull forward** out of when you leave.

When you must back, follow the **6 rules**:

| # | Rule | What to do | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Start in the proper position | Place the truck where the backing will be easiest and safest. The best spot depends on the kind of backing. | A good start means fewer corrections. |
| 2 | Look at your path | Before starting, **get out of the cab and walk around** the vehicle. Check clearance at the sides, **overhead**, and in and near your path. | You can't see much once you are moving backward. |
| 3 | Use mirrors on both sides | Check both outside mirrors often. If you are unsure, **get out and look** again. | Each mirror shows a different side of the path. |
| 4 | Back slowly | Go as slowly as possible, in the **lowest reverse gear**. | You can fix steering errors easily and stop quickly. |
| 5 | Back and turn toward the driver's side | Set up so the vehicle turns toward **your (left) side**, even if you must drive around the block to get into that position. | You can watch the rear of the vehicle out your side window. Backing to the right (blind) side is very risky, since your view there is poor. |
| 6 | Use a helper | Whenever possible, have someone guide you. The helper stands **near the back of the vehicle where you can see them**. | There are **blind spots** you cannot see. |

**Helper hand signals:** Before you start, agree on a set of hand signals you both understand. The most important one is **"STOP."**

### 2.2.5 Backing with a trailer (p. 2-10)
Steering works differently with a trailer:
- **Car, straight truck, or bus:** steer **toward** the side you want the vehicle to go.
- **Trailer:** steer the **opposite** way. Once the trailer begins to turn, reverse the wheel so you **follow** the trailer.

*Worked example:* you want the back of the trailer to move to the left. Turn the steering wheel to the **right**. When the trailer begins to swing left, turn the wheel back toward the left to follow it.

More trailer-backing rules:
- Whenever possible, set up so the backing path is **straight**.
- If the path has to curve, curve toward the **driver's side**, where you can see.
- Go **slowly**, so you can fix errors before you get far off course.
- **Correct drift immediately:** the moment the trailer starts to leave the right path, steer **toward the drift** (in the direction the trailer is drifting).
- **Pull forward:** use **pull-ups** (drive forward a short way to straighten out and reposition) whenever you need them.

### 2.3 Shifting gears (p. 2-10)
If you can't get into the right gear while driving, you have **less control** of the vehicle. Correct shifting matters.

### 2.3.1 Manual transmissions (p. 2-10 to 2-11)
Key terms:
- **Unsynchronized transmission:** a manual gearbox without parts that match gear speeds for you. The driver must match them.
- **Double clutching:** pushing the clutch in **twice** for one gear change — once to go into Neutral, once to go into the new gear.
- Most heavy vehicles with an **unsynchronized** manual transmission **require** double clutching. A **synchronized** manual does **not** need it.
- **rpm** = revolutions per minute = engine speed, shown on the **tachometer**.

**Shifting up vs. shifting down — the 5 steps** (p. 2-10, 2-11)

| Step | Shifting UP | Shifting DOWN |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Release accelerator, push in clutch, shift to Neutral — all together | Same |
| 2 | Release the clutch | Same |
| 3 | **Let** engine and gears **slow down** to the rpm needed for the next gear | **Press the accelerator** to **raise** engine and gear speed to the rpm needed for the lower gear |
| 4 | Push in clutch and shift to the **higher** gear — together | Push in clutch and shift to the **lower** gear — together |
| 5 | Release clutch and press accelerator — together | Same |

*Why step 3 is different:* at the same road speed, a higher gear needs a slower engine, and a lower gear needs a faster engine. So you wait for the rpm to drop before an upshift, and you add gas to raise it before a downshift.

**If you stay in Neutral too long** and can't get into the next gear: **don't force it.** Go back to Neutral, let the clutch out, bring the engine rpm up to fit your road speed, then try once more. Double clutching takes practice.

**Knowing when to shift up — 2 ways** (p. 2-11)
1. **Engine speed (rpm).** Read your vehicle's driver manual and learn its operating rpm range. Watch the tachometer; upshift when the needle gets to the **top of that range**.
   - **"Progressive" shifting** (on some newer vehicles): the shift point climbs **higher** with each higher gear. Find out what fits the vehicle you drive.
2. **Road speed (mph).** Learn which speeds each gear is good for, then use the **speedometer** to know when to shift.

With either method, you may also learn to shift by the **sound of the engine**.

**Knowing when to shift down** (p. 2-11): the same idea — watch the tachometer or the speedometer, and make the downshift when rpm or road speed reaches the correct point.

**The 2 special times you must downshift** (p. 2-11)

| When | What to do | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Before starting down a hill** | Reduce speed and pick a gear that lets you control your speed **without hard braking**. This is **usually a lower gear than the one you would need to climb the same hill**. | Hard braking can **overheat** the brakes so they **lose braking power**. |
| **Before entering a curve** | Slow to a safe speed and shift to the right gear. | You can use a little power through the curve, which keeps the vehicle **more stable**, and you can speed up as soon as you leave the curve. |

The key word is **before** — not in the middle of the hill or curve. (Section 2.16 on mountain driving adds more on choosing a downhill gear.)

### 2.3.2 Multi-speed rear axles and auxiliary transmissions (p. 2-11)
- These give the vehicle **extra gears**.
- They are usually worked by a **knob or switch mounted on the main transmission's shift lever**.
- Shift patterns vary a lot. Learn the correct way to shift the vehicle **you** will drive.

### 2.3.3 Automatic transmissions (p. 2-11)
- On a downgrade, select a **low range**. This gives you more **engine braking** (the engine's own drag slowing the vehicle when you are off the gas).
- A low range stops the transmission from shifting **up** past the gear you picked — unless the engine goes over its **governor rpm** (the top engine speed the governor allows).
- Using this braking effect on downgrades is **very important**.

### 2.3.4 Retarders (p. 2-11)
A **retarder** is a device that helps slow the vehicle so you need your service brakes less.
- Benefits: **less brake wear**, and **another way to slow down**.
- **4 basic types:** **exhaust, engine, hydraulic, electric.**
- The **driver can turn any retarder on or off.** On some vehicles you can also adjust how strong it is.
- When switched on, it brakes **only the drive wheels**, and it works each time your foot comes **fully off** the accelerator.
- Retarders can be **noisy**. Know where their use is allowed.

**Caution — slippery roads:** when the drive wheels have poor traction, the retarder can make them **skid**. **Switch the retarder OFF** on any road that is **wet, icy, or snow-covered**.
*Why:* all of the retarder's force goes to the drive wheels. On a slick surface those wheels can stop turning and slide.

## Exam traps
- **Trap:** "Retarders help prevent skids on slippery roads." → **Correct:** False. A retarder can make the drive wheels skid; turn it off on wet, icy, or snowy roads. The word "retarder" sounds like a safety device, so people assume it always helps. (p. 2-11)
- **Trap:** Back toward the right side so traffic is away from you. → **Correct:** Back toward the **driver's side**. On the right (blind) side your view is poor, so it is very risky. (p. 2-10)
- **Trap:** When backing a trailer, turn the wheel the way you want the trailer to go. → **Correct:** Turn it the **opposite** way. That "toward" rule is right for cars, straight trucks and buses, which is why it tempts. (p. 2-10)
- **Trap:** When the trailer drifts, steer away from the drift. → **Correct:** Steer **toward the drift**, right away. "Away" feels natural but pushes the trailer further off. (p. 2-10)
- **Trap:** Downshift once you feel the truck speeding up on the hill, or use the same gear you climbed with. → **Correct:** Downshift **before** starting down, to a gear **usually lower** than the climbing gear. (p. 2-11)
- **Trap:** Drive wheels spinning → brake, or add power to push through. → **Correct:** Take your foot **off the accelerator**. (p. 2-9)
- **Trap:** Release the parking brake, then give it gas. → **Correct:** Give enough power **first**; release the parking brake only when the engine can hold the vehicle. (p. 2-9)
- **Trap:** The helper should stand in the middle of your path where they can see everything. → **Correct:** Near the back of the vehicle, **where you can see them**. If you can't see your helper, you can't see their signals. (p. 2-10)
- **Trap:** A retarder slows all wheels. → **Correct:** **Drive wheels only.** (p. 2-11)
- **Trap:** Can't get into gear → push harder on the shift lever. → **Correct:** Don't force it; back to Neutral, release clutch, match engine speed to road speed, try again. (p. 2-10)
- **Trap:** Synchronized transmissions need double clutching. → **Correct:** Not required on synchronized; most **unsynchronized** heavy-vehicle transmissions need it. (p. 2-10)

## Handbook review questions — answered
**TYK 2.2–2.3 #1** Why is backing toward the driver's side the better choice?
→ Your view is much better. You can keep an eye on the back of the vehicle through your side window. On the right side you see far less, which makes backing that way very risky. (p. 2-10)

**TYK 2.2–2.3 #2** You are stopped on a hill. How do you get moving without rolling backward?
→ With a manual transmission, let the clutch partly out before your right foot leaves the brake. Hold the vehicle with the parking brake when needed, and take it off only once the engine is giving enough power to stop any rollback. A tractor-trailer with a trailer brake hand valve can also be held with that valve while you start. (p. 2-9)

**TYK 2.2–2.3 #3** Why should you use a helper when backing?
→ Because there are blind spots around the vehicle that you cannot see. A helper standing near the back, where you can see them, can watch those areas for you. (p. 2-10)

**TYK 2.2–2.3 #4** Which hand signal is most important for you and your helper to agree on?
→ The signal for "stop." (p. 2-10)

**TYK 2.2–2.3 #5** Name the 2 special situations when you should downshift.
→ Before starting down a hill, and before entering a curve. (p. 2-11)

**TYK 2.2–2.3 #6** When should you downshift an automatic transmission?
→ When going down a grade: select a low range to get more engine braking. The low range keeps the transmission from shifting up past that gear (unless governor rpm is exceeded). As with any downshift for a hill, pick the gear before you start down. (p. 2-11)

**TYK 2.2–2.3 #7** True or false: retarders keep you from skidding on a slippery road.
→ **False.** When traction is poor, a retarder can cause the drive wheels to skid. Turn it off when the road is wet, icy, or snow-covered. (p. 2-11)

**TYK 2.2–2.3 #8** Name the two methods that tell you when it is time to shift.
→ By engine speed (rpm, using the tachometer and the vehicle's rpm range) and by road speed (mph, using the speedometer and knowing what speeds each gear is good for). (p. 2-11)

## One-minute recap
- Don't roll back: partly engage the clutch before lifting off the brake; release the parking brake only when the engine can hold you. Drive wheels spinning → foot off the gas.
- Both hands on opposite sides of the wheel; brake gradually; clutch in near idle.
- Avoid backing. If you must: good start position, get out and look, both mirrors, lowest reverse gear, toward the **driver's side**, use a helper, and agree on **"stop."**
- Backing a trailer: steer the **opposite** way; correct drift by steering **toward the drift**; use pull-ups.
- Double clutching: 5 steps. Upshift = let rpm drop in Neutral; downshift = raise rpm in Neutral. Never force a gear.
- Know when to shift by **engine speed (rpm)** or **road speed (mph)**.
- Downshift **before** a hill (usually lower than the climbing gear) and **before** a curve. Automatic: pick a low range on downgrades.
- Retarders: 4 types (exhaust, engine, hydraulic, electric), drive wheels only, **OFF on wet, icy, or snowy roads**.
