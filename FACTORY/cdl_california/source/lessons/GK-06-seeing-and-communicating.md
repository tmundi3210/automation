# GK-06 · Seeing, mirrors, signals, warning devices

> **Handbook:** Section 2.4–2.5, pages 2-12–2-15 · **Test:** General Knowledge · **Exam weight:** High · **Study time:** ~30 min

## What you'll be able to answer
- How far ahead a good driver looks (in seconds, and in city and highway distance), and the two main things to look for.
- The California mirror law, why convex mirrors fool your eyes, how to adjust mirrors, and the four mirror checks in a lane change.
- The three rules for turn signals, and how to warn drivers behind you when you slow down or stop.
- How to make other road users notice you: when passing, at dawn/dusk/rain/snow, and when parked at the roadside.
- Exactly where to place warning triangles — on a divided or one-way road, a two-lane road, and behind a hill or curve — and how fast you must put them out.

## Learn it

### 2.4 Seeing (p. 2-12)
A safe driver knows what is happening **all around** the vehicle. Poor looking habits are a **major cause of crashes**.

### 2.4.1 Seeing ahead (p. 2-12 to 2-13)
Everyone looks ahead, but many drivers don't look **far enough** ahead.

**Why look far ahead?** A big vehicle needs a lot of distance to **stop** or to **change lanes**. You need to see trouble early so you have room to make those moves safely.

**How far ahead?** Good drivers keep their eyes **12 to 15 seconds or more** down the road. This means: look at the spot you will reach 12 to 15 seconds from now.

| Where | 12–15 seconds ahead is about… |
|---|---|
| Lower speeds (city driving) | **One block** |
| Highway speeds (open highway) | **A quarter of a mile** |

*Worked example:* at 60 mph a vehicle travels 88 feet every second. 15 seconds × 88 feet = 1,320 feet. A mile is 5,280 feet, so 1,320 feet is one quarter of a mile — the handbook's highway figure.

- If you look less far, you may end up **braking too hard** or making **sudden lane changes** at the last moment.
- Looking far ahead does **not** mean ignoring what is close. Good drivers move their eyes **back and forth, near and far**.

**The 2 main things to look for ahead:**

1. **Traffic** (p. 2-12)
   - Vehicles **entering** the highway, moving **into your lane**, or **turning**.
   - **Brake lights** of vehicles that are slowing.
   - If you see these early, you can change your speed or change lanes to avoid trouble.
   - **"Stale" green light:** a signal that has stayed green for a long while is likely to change before you get there. **Begin to slow and prepare to stop.**
2. **Road conditions** (p. 2-13)
   - **Hills and curves** — anything that will make you slow down or change lanes.
   - **Traffic signals and signs.** Signs can warn you about road conditions ahead that need a change of speed.

### 2.4.2 Seeing to the sides and rear (p. 2-13)
You must also know what is **beside** and **behind** you. Your main tool is your **mirrors**. Check them **regularly**, and **more often** in special situations.

**[CA] California mirror law (CVC §26709)**
- Any motor vehicle registered in California needs **2 or more mirrors**.
- **One** of them must be on the **left-hand side**.
- Together they must show the road behind clearly for **200 feet or more**.
- You need **both** a left and a right mirror when the vehicle's design or load cuts off your view to the rear, or when something you **tow** cuts it off.

**How to use mirrors**
- Check them with **quick glances**, and understand what you see.
- Keep switching your eyes between the mirrors and the road in front.
- **Don't stare** into a mirror. *Why:* while you stare, the truck keeps moving, and you travel a long way without knowing what is in front of you.

**Convex mirrors** (also called "fisheye," "spot," or "bugeye" mirrors)
- A **convex** mirror curves outward. It shows a **wider area** than a flat (plane) mirror. This is often helpful.
- But everything in a convex mirror looks **smaller** and **farther away** than it really is.
- *So:* a car in your convex mirror is **closer and bigger** than it looks. Allow for this before you move over.
- Figure 2.7 shows the flat-mirror view, the wider convex-mirror view, and a **blind spot area** that neither mirror shows. (A **blind spot** is an area around your vehicle that you cannot see directly or in your mirrors.)

**Mirror adjustment**
- Check it **before the start of any trip**.
- You can check it accurately **only when the trailer(s) are straight**. *Why:* a trailer at an angle changes what the mirror shows.
- Adjust each mirror so it shows **some part of your own vehicle**. This gives you a **reference point** for judging where everything else is.

**Regular checks — 2 purposes**

| Check for | What to look for | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Traffic** | Vehicles on either side and behind; vehicles **overtaking** you; vehicles moving into your **blind spots** | If an emergency comes up, you may have to know instantly whether a fast move into another lane is possible. |
| **Your vehicle** | Your **tires** — one way to spot a **tire fire**. With **open cargo**: loose straps, ropes, or chains, and a **flapping or ballooning tarp** | You catch problems before they cause a crash or lost load. |

Remember: there are **blind spots your mirrors cannot show**. Regular checks tell you where other vehicles are and whether one has moved into a blind spot.

**Special situations — check more than usual**
There are **4**: turns, merges, tight maneuvers, and lane changes.

- **Lane changes** — make sure no one is beside you or about to pass. Check your mirrors **4 times**:
  1. **Before** you change lanes — is there enough room?
  2. **After you signal** — has anyone slipped into your blind spot?
  3. **Right after you start** the lane change — is the path still clear?
  4. **After you finish** the lane change.
- **Turns** — make sure the **rear** of your vehicle won't hit anything.
- **Merges** — make sure the **gap** in traffic is big enough to enter safely.
- **Tight maneuvers** — in close quarters, check often to be sure you have **enough clearance**.

### 2.5 Communicating (p. 2-14)
In safe driving, **communicating** means two things:
1. **Signaling your intentions** — telling others what you are going to do.
2. **Communicating your presence** — making sure others know you are there.

### 2.5.1 Signal your intentions (p. 2-14)
Other drivers can't guess your plan. The only way they know is if you show them. Signaling is a safety tool.

**Turns — 3 rules for turn signals**

| Rule | What it means | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 1. **Signal early** | Turn the signal on well before the turn. | Early signaling is the **most effective way to stop others from trying to pass you**. |
| 2. **Signal continuously** | Keep it on the whole time. Don't cancel it until the turn is **finished**. | A safe turn takes **both hands on the wheel**, so leave the signal alone until you are done. |
| 3. **Cancel your signal** | Turn it off after the turn (if your signals don't cancel themselves). | A signal left on misleads other drivers. |

**[CA]** The handbook points to California Vehicle Code **§§24951 and 24600** for which vehicles must have turn signal lamps and **2 stop lamps**.

**Lane changes**
- Turn on your signal **before** you change lanes.
- Change lanes **slowly and smoothly**. *Why:* a driver you didn't see then has a chance to honk or get out of your way.

**Slowing down**
- As soon as you know you must slow down, warn the drivers behind you.
- **Tap the brake pedal lightly a few times** — just enough to make the **brake lights blink**.
- Save the **4-way emergency flashers** for **very slow driving** or when you are **stopped**.

Warn drivers behind you in these situations:

| Situation | What to do | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Trouble ahead** | Flash your brake lights. | Your big vehicle hides the hazard from drivers behind you. |
| **Tight turns** | Brake **early, slowly, and gradually**. | Most car drivers don't know how slowly a large vehicle must go to make a tight turn. |
| **Stopping on the road** (to unload cargo or passengers, or at a railroad crossing) | Flash your brake lights. **Don't stop suddenly.** | Drivers behind don't expect a big vehicle to stop in the roadway. |
| **Driving slowly** | Turn on your 4-way flashers **if it is legal**. Flasher laws are different from state to state — check the laws where you drive. | Other drivers often misjudge how quickly they are gaining on a slow vehicle, and notice only when they are almost on top of it. |

**Do not direct traffic.** Don't wave or signal to other drivers that it is safe to pass you.
*Why:* your wave could lead to a crash, the **blame could fall on you**, and that could cost you a great deal of money (the handbook says **many thousands of dollars**).

### 2.5.2 Communicating your presence (p. 2-14 to 2-15)
Other drivers may not notice you, **even when you are in plain sight**. Help prevent crashes by making sure they know you are there.

**When you pass** another vehicle, a person on foot, or someone on a bike:
- **Expect that they have not seen you.** Any of them could step or swerve into your path without warning.
- Where the law allows, give the horn a **light tap**. **After dark**, you can instead switch your headlights **from low to high and back to low**.
- Drive with enough care that there is no crash **even if they never notice you**.

**When visibility is poor** — **dawn**, **dusk**, **rain**, or **snow**:
- If it's hard for you to see other vehicles, it's hard for them to see you too.
- Switch on your **headlights**. The **identification and clearance lights** alone (the small marker lights on the top and edges of a large vehicle) are not enough.
- Choose **low beams**. High beams annoy other people **by day and by night**.

> **Don't mix these up:** this low-beam rule is for dawn, dusk, rain and snow. For normal **night** driving, Section 2.11 (p. 2-31) says to use **high beams when it is safe and legal** (not within 500 feet of an approaching vehicle). See GK-10.

**Stopped on the roadside**
- Turn on your **4-way emergency flashers**. This matters most **at night**.
- **Don't rely on your taillights.** *Why:* drivers have run into the back of parked trucks because they believed the truck was still moving with traffic.

**Emergency warning devices** (your red reflective triangles — Section 2.1 lists **3**)
Any time you stop **on a road or its shoulder**, set out your warning devices **within 10 minutes**.

| Where you stopped | Where the devices go | Figure |
|---|---|---|
| **One-way or divided highway** | All 3 behind you, toward approaching traffic: **10 feet, 100 feet, and 200 feet** | 2.8 |
| **Two-lane road with traffic both ways, or undivided highway** | 1 no more than **10 feet** from the **front or rear corner** (to mark where the vehicle is), 1 **100 feet behind**, 1 **100 feet ahead** — either on the shoulder or in your own lane | 2.9 |
| **A hill, curve, or other obstruction** keeps drivers from seeing your vehicle within **500 feet** | Move the **rearmost** triangle back down the road, beyond the hill or curve, so drivers get warning in time. Figure 2.10 shows it **100 to 500 feet** back. | 2.10 (p. 2-15) |

*Why the patterns differ:*
- On a divided or one-way road, traffic comes from **one direction only** (behind you), so all devices go behind.
- On a two-lane road, traffic comes from **both directions**, so one device goes ahead of the vehicle too.
- Near a hill or curve, drivers must see the warning **before** they can see your truck, so the rear device goes farther back.

*Worked example:* your truck breaks down on a two-lane, two-way highway, just past a sharp curve that blocks the view of drivers coming up behind you. Put one triangle within 10 feet of the corner of the truck and one about 100 feet ahead. Move the rear triangle back beyond the curve (up to 500 feet) so drivers see it before they come around the curve. Do all of this within 10 minutes of stopping.

**Your safety while placing them:** carry the triangles **between yourself and the oncoming traffic**, so drivers notice you.

**The horn** (p. 2-15)
- The horn lets others know you are there and can help **avoid a crash**.
- But it can **startle** people. Using it when you don't need to can be **dangerous**.

## Exam traps
- **Trap:** Objects in a convex mirror look bigger and closer. → **Correct:** They look **smaller and farther away** than they really are. A wide-angle view feels like a zoom, so people guess the wrong way. (p. 2-13)
- **Trap:** On a divided highway, put one triangle in front of the truck. → **Correct:** All three go **behind** you, toward approaching traffic, at **10, 100 and 200 feet**. The "one ahead" pattern belongs to two-lane roads. (p. 2-14)
- **Trap:** On a two-lane road, put all three triangles behind the truck. → **Correct:** One within **10 feet** of a corner, one **100 feet behind**, one **100 feet ahead** — traffic comes from both ways. (p. 2-15)
- **Trap:** You have 15 or 30 minutes to put out triangles. → **Correct:** **Within 10 minutes.** (p. 2-14)
- **Trap:** Near a hill or curve, keep the rear triangle in its usual spot. → **Correct:** Move it back beyond the hill or curve so drivers get warning — Figure 2.10 shows **100 to 500 feet** back. (p. 2-15)
- **Trap:** 12–15 seconds ahead at highway speed is about one block (or half a mile). → **Correct:** About **one block** is for **lower speeds**; at **highway** speed it is about **a quarter mile**. (p. 2-12)
- **Trap:** Wave a car around you when the road ahead is clear — it's courteous. → **Correct:** **Do not direct traffic.** It could cause a crash, and you could be blamed. (p. 2-14)
- **Trap:** Taillights are enough when you're parked on the shoulder. → **Correct:** Turn on your **4-way flashers**. Drivers have hit parked trucks thinking they were moving. (p. 2-14)
- **Trap:** At dusk or in rain, clearance lights (or high beams) are enough. → **Correct:** Turn on your **headlights** and use **low beams**. (p. 2-14)
- **Trap:** Cancel the signal as you start turning so both hands are free. → **Correct:** Keep it on **until the turn is complete**, then cancel. (p. 2-14)
- **Trap:** A green light that has been green a long time — keep going. → **Correct:** It will probably change before you arrive; **begin slowing and prepare to stop**. (p. 2-12)
- **Trap:** Adjust mirrors while the trailer is at an angle. → **Correct:** Mirror adjustment can be checked accurately **only when the trailer(s) are straight**. (p. 2-13)
- **Trap:** In a lane change, one mirror check before you move is enough. → **Correct:** Check **4 times** — before, after signaling, right after starting, and after finishing. (p. 2-13)

→ Behind the vehicle, toward approaching traffic, at 10 feet, 100 feet, and 200 feet. (p. 2-14)

## One-minute recap
- Look **12–15 seconds** ahead: about **1 block** in the city, about **¼ mile** on the highway. Watch for **traffic** and **road conditions**. A "stale" green light → begin slowing, prepare to stop.
- **[CA]** At least **2 mirrors**, one on the **left**, **200-foot** rear view (CVC §26709). Glance, don't stare.
- **Convex mirrors:** wider view, but things look **smaller and farther away**. Adjust mirrors before each trip with the trailer **straight**, each showing part of your vehicle.
- Lane change = **4 mirror checks**. Extra checks in turns, merges, and tight spaces.
- Turn signals: **early, continuous, cancel**. Flash brake lights to warn of slowing; 4-way flashers when very slow or stopped. **Never direct traffic.**
- Be seen: assume others don't see you when passing; **headlights on low beam** at dawn, dusk, rain, snow; **4-way flashers** when parked at the roadside.
- Triangles **within 10 minutes**. Divided/one-way: **10-100-200 ft** behind. Two-lane: **10 ft** at a corner, **100 ft behind, 100 ft ahead**. Hill/curve: rear triangle back **up to 500 ft**. Hold them between you and traffic.
