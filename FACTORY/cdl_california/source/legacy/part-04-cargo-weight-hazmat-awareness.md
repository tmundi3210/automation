# B4 — Cargo, weight, and hazmat awareness (GK)

## B4.1 Driver responsibility

Even when someone else loaded the cargo, the driver is responsible for:
- Inspecting the cargo.
- Recognizing overloads and poor balance.
- Knowing the cargo is properly secured.
- Making sure cargo doesn't block your view, your arm movement, or access to emergency equipment.

**Inspection timing:** before the trip; within the first 50 miles; then every 3 hours or 150 miles, whichever comes first; after every break. Adjust tiedowns as needed.

You must know federal, state, and local weight limits, securement rules, covering rules, and where large loads may be driven.

## B4.2 Weight terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **GVW** | Total weight of a single vehicle plus its load |
| **GCW** | Total weight of the power unit, trailer(s), and load |
| **GVWR** | Manufacturer's maximum for a single vehicle plus load |
| **GCWR** | Manufacturer's maximum for a combination plus load |
| **Axle weight** | Weight transmitted to the ground by one axle or axle set |
| **Tire load** | Max safe weight a tire can carry at a stated pressure — stamped on the sidewall |

**Legal limits (federal Interstate):** 20,000 lb single axle, 34,000 lb tandem, 80,000 lb GVW. The **bridge formula** allows more weight on axles spaced farther apart and less on close-together axles, to protect bridges. Some states and loads need permits, escorts, or restricted routes.

**Overloading effects:** poor steering, braking, and speed control; too slow on upgrades and too fast on downgrades; possible brake failure. In bad weather or mountains you may need to run below the legal maximum.

## B4.3 Weight balance

| Condition | Effect |
|---|---|
| Too little weight on the steer axle | Poor steering and traction; dangerous in winter |
| Too much weight on the steer axle | Hard steering; damage to the axle and tires |
| Too little weight on the drive axles | Poor traction; drive wheels spin |
| High center of gravity | Greater rollover risk on curves and swerves |

Spread the weight evenly, centered, and balanced front to back and side to side; distribute across all axles; keep the heaviest cargo on the bottom.

## B4.4 Securement

- **Blocking:** at the front, back, and/or sides, fitted snugly and secured to the deck, to stop sliding.
- **Bracing:** runs from the upper part of the cargo to the floor or walls.
- **Tiedowns (handbook rule):** at least **1 per 10 ft** of cargo, and **never fewer than 2**. The aggregate working load limit must be at least **½ the cargo weight**.
- **Tiedowns (49 CFR 393.110 detail):** ≤5 ft and ≤1,100 lb → 1; ≤5 ft and >1,100 lb → 2; 5–10 ft → 2; add 1 for each additional 10 ft or fraction.
- Equipment: chain, cable, straps, webbing, ratchet binders; use edge protectors at sharp edges.
- **Header board** (front-end structure, "headache rack"): protects the driver from cargo shifting forward.
- **Covering:** protects the public from spilled cargo and the cargo from weather; required on many open loads. Check the cover in your mirrors — a flapping tarp can tear loose or block your view.
- **Sealed/containerized loads:** you may not be able to open them, but you remain responsible for gross and axle weight limits and an intact seal. Containers on a chassis are secured with locking devices.
- **Federal performance standard:** securement must withstand **0.8 g forward**, **0.5 g rearward**, **0.5 g lateral**.

## B4.5 Special cargo

- **Dry bulk tanks:** high center of gravity, load can shift — take curves and sharp turns carefully.
- **Hanging meat:** unstable, top-heavy, and swinging — go slowly on curves and ramps.
- **Livestock:** animals move and lean into curves, raising rollover risk. For a partial load, use **false bulkheads** to bunch the animals together.
- **Oversized loads:** special permits; limited times and routes; may need escorts, flags, lights, and "Oversize Load" signs.

## B4.6 Hazmat awareness required of every driver

**Definition:** material that poses a risk to health, safety, or property during transport.
**Purpose of the rules:** contain the product, communicate the risk, ensure safe drivers and equipment.

**Hazard classes:** 1 Explosives · 2 Gases · 3 Flammable liquids · 4 Flammable solids · 5 Oxidizers and organic peroxides · 6 Poisons and infectious substances · 7 Radioactive · 8 Corrosives · 9 Miscellaneous.

**Identifiers:** a 4-digit UN/NA identification number; package labels about 4 inches on a side; vehicle placards **10¾ in. square, turned on a point**, on **all 4 sides**.

**Shipping papers:** prepared and certified by the **shipper**. Hazmat entries must be listed first, highlighted in a contrasting color, or marked with an **X** (or **RQ**) in the HM column, and the papers must be tabbed or on top.

**Where papers go:** while driving, within reach with your seat belt on or in the driver's door holder; when away from the vehicle, on the driver's seat or in the door pocket.

**Endorsement triggers:** an **H** endorsement is required if the load must be placarded, or contains any amount of select agents or toxins. Table 1 materials need placards in **any amount**; Table 2 materials at **1,001 lb or more**. The H endorsement requires a TSA background check and fingerprints.

**Driver duties:** get trained; follow route and tunnel restrictions; never smoke near classes 1, 2.1, 3, 4, or 5; carry the emergency response information. The ERG helps responders identify the material and the actions to take.
